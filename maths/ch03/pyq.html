Question: Tarun correctly solved a pair of linear equations in two variables and found their only point of intersection as (5, – 1). One of the lines was x−y=6x - y = 6x−y=6.
Which of the following could have been the other line?
I: 3x−3y=183x - 3y = 183x−3y=18
II: 2x−3y=132x - 3y = 132x−3y=13
III: 2x−3y=162x - 3y = 162x−3y=16

Given: the only point of intersection is (5,−1)(5, -1)(5,−1); one line is x−y=6x - y = 6x−y=6. Asked: which line(s) could be the other one.

Concept used: the other line must pass through (5,−1)(5, -1)(5,−1) and cross x−y=6x - y = 6x−y=6 at exactly one point (Topics 2–3). Why it applies: "only point of intersection" rules out coincident lines.

  1. 1

    I: 3(5)−3(−1)=15+3=183(5) - 3(-1) = 15 + 3 = 183(5)−3(−1)=15+3=18 ✔ — it passes through (5,−1)(5, -1)(5,−1). But 3x−3y=183x - 3y = 183x−3y=18 is 3 times x−y=6x - y = 6x−y=6: the same line, with infinitely many common points. Reject.

  2. 2

    II: 2(5)−3(−1)=10+3=132(5) - 3(-1) = 10 + 3 = 132(5)−3(−1)=10+3=13 ✔ — it passes through (5,−1)(5, -1)(5,−1). Ratios 12≠−1−3=13\dfrac{1}{2} \neq \dfrac{-1}{-3} = \dfrac{1}{3}21​=−3−1​=31​: the lines intersect at one point. Accept.

  3. 3

    III: 2(5)−3(−1)=13≠162(5) - 3(-1) = 13 \neq 162(5)−3(−1)=13=16 — it does not pass through (5,−1)(5, -1)(5,−1). Reject.

  4. 4

    Only II works → option B.

  5. Check: solving x−y=6x - y = 6x−y=6 and 2x−3y=132x - 3y = 132x−3y=13: from the first, x=y+6x = y + 6x=y+6; then 2y+12−3y=132y + 12 - 3y = 132y+12−3y=13, so y=−1y = -1y=−1 and x=5x = 5x=5 ✔.

    Why the other options are wrong: A and C include I, which makes the pair dependent; D includes III, which misses the point.
    Common trap: Choosing C because (5,−1)(5, -1)(5,−1) satisfies I. It satisfies I only because I is the same line — "only point of intersection" fails.
(B) II only. (5,−1)(5, -1)(5,−1) satisfies I as well, but I makes the pair consistent and dependent (the same line); only II: 2x−3y=132x - 3y = 132x−3y=13 gives exactly one point of intersection
