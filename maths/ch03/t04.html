Topic 4 of 6

The substitution method

About 40 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Solve a pair of linear equations by substitution, including equations with fraction and decimal coefficients.
  • Recognise "no solution" (a false statement such as 3 = 4) and "infinitely many solutions" (a true statement such as 4 = 4) when substituting.

A graph gave us (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2) in Topic 2 because the lines happened to cross at a grid point. But what if they cross at (197,37)\left(\dfrac{19}{7}, \dfrac{3}{7}\right)(719​,73​)? No one can read that from graph paper. Algebra can find it exactly. The first algebraic method is like a swap at a shop counter: if one equation tells you exactly what y is worth in terms of x, you can replace y by that value in the other equation — and suddenly there is only one unknown left.

The idea

If one equation says y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1, then in the other equation y and 2x−12x - 12x−1 are interchangeable. Replacing y by (2x−1)(2x - 1)(2x−1) leaves an equation in x alone, which you already know how to solve.

The substitution routine

  1. Make one variable the subject of one equation. Choose a variable whose coefficient is 1 or −1-1−1 if you can — then there are no fractions.
  2. Substitute that expression, in brackets, into the other equation.
  3. Solve the resulting equation in one variable.
  4. Back-substitute: put this value into the expression from step 1 to get the other variable.
  5. Check the pair of values in both original equations.

Why the other equation in step 2? The expression came from the first equation, so putting it back into the first equation just gives a statement that is always true, such as 2x−1=2x−12x - 1 = 2x - 12x−1=2x−1. It tells you nothing.

Fractions and decimals

Clear them first (Topic 1): multiply every term of an equation with fractions by the LCM of its denominators, and every term of an equation with decimals by 10 or 100. Then follow the routine with whole numbers.

When the variable disappears

Sometimes, after step 2, both x-terms (or both y-terms) cancel and no variable is left:

  • A false statement, such as 3=43 = 43=4: no pair of values can satisfy both equations. The pair has no solution — the lines are parallel.
  • A true statement, such as 4=44 = 44=4 or 0=00 = 00=0: every solution of one equation also satisfies the other. The pair has infinitely many solutions — the lines are coincident.

For example, substituting y=3x−2y = 3x - 2y=3x−2 into 6x−2y=46x - 2y = 46x−2y=4 gives 6x−6x+4=46x - 6x + 4 = 46x−6x+4=4, that is 4=44 = 44=4: infinitely many solutions.

A statement with no variable is not a mistake in your working. It is the answer, and you should say what it means in words. And "0=00 = 00=0" does not mean x=0x = 0x=0.

Worked example 1

One equation already gives y

Question. Solve by substitution: y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1 and 3x+2y=123x + 2y = 123x+2y=12.

Given: a pair of linear equations; the first already has y as the subject. Asked: x and y.

Concept used: the substitution routine. Why it applies: y is already expressed in terms of x.

  1. 1

    Substitute y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1 into the other equation.

    3x+2(2x−1)=123x + 2(2x - 1) = 123x+2(2x−1)=12Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Expand the bracket.

    3x+4x−2=123x + 4x - 2 = 123x+4x−2=12Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Collect the x-terms.

    7x−2=127x - 2 = 127x−2=12Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Add 2 to both sides.

    7x=147x = 147x=14Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Divide by 7.

    x=2x = 2x=2Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    Back-substitute into y=2x−1y = 2x - 1y=2x−1.

    y=2(2)−1=3y = 2(2) - 1 = 3y=2(2)−1=3Swipe to see the whole equation →

  7. Check: (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) in both: 3=2(2)−13 = 2(2) - 13=2(2)−1 ✔ and 3(2)+2(3)=6+6=123(2) + 2(3) = 6 + 6 = 123(2)+2(3)=6+6=12 ✔.

  8. Answer x=2x = 2x=2, y=3y = 3y=3

    Common trap: Writing 3x+2×2x−13x + 2 \times 2x - 13x+2×2x−1 without brackets, which gives 7x−1=127x - 1 = 127x−1=12 and x=137x = \dfrac{13}{7}x=713​. The 2 multiplies the whole expression.

Worked example 2

Choosing the variable to make the subject

Question. Solve by substitution: 2x+3y=32x + 3y = 32x+3y=3 and 5x−y=165x - y = 165x−y=16.

Given: two equations in general form. Asked: x and y.

Concept used: the substitution routine. Why it applies: y has coefficient −1-1−1 in the second equation, so making y the subject avoids fractions.

  1. 1

    From 5x−y=165x - y = 165x−y=16, add y to both sides.

    5x=16+y5x = 16 + y5x=16+ySwipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Subtract 16 from both sides.

    y=5x−16y = 5x - 16y=5x−16Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Substitute into the other equation, 2x+3y=32x + 3y = 32x+3y=3.

    2x+3(5x−16)=32x + 3(5x - 16) = 32x+3(5x−16)=3Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Expand the bracket.

    2x+15x−48=32x + 15x - 48 = 32x+15x−48=3Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Collect the x-terms.

    17x−48=317x - 48 = 317x−48=3Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    Add 48 to both sides.

    17x=5117x = 5117x=51Swipe to see the whole equation →
  7. 7

    Divide by 17.

    x=3x = 3x=3Swipe to see the whole equation →
  8. 8

    Back-substitute into y=5x−16y = 5x - 16y=5x−16.

    y=15−16=−1y = 15 - 16 = -1y=15−16=−1Swipe to see the whole equation →

  9. Check: 2(3)+3(−1)=6−3=32(3) + 3(-1) = 6 - 3 = 32(3)+3(−1)=6−3=3 ✔ and 5(3)−(−1)=15+1=165(3) - (-1) = 15 + 1 = 165(3)−(−1)=15+1=16 ✔.

  10. Answer x=3x = 3x=3, y=−1y = -1y=−1

    Common trap: Making y the subject as y=16−5xy = 16 - 5xy=16−5x (signs of both terms flipped wrongly). That gives 2x+48−15x=32x + 48 - 15x = 32x+48−15x=3, so x=4513x = \dfrac{45}{13}x=1345​ — a messy value is often a sign of a slip. Always check in both equations.

Worked example 3

Fractions and decimals

Question. Solve by substitution: x3+y4=3\dfrac{x}{3} + \dfrac{y}{4} = 33x​+4y​=3 and 0.1x−0.2y=−0.20.1x - 0.2y = -0.20.1x−0.2y=−0.2.

Given: one equation with fractions, one with decimals. Asked: x and y.

Concept used: clear fractions and decimals, then the substitution routine. Why it applies: whole-number coefficients make every later step safer.

  1. 1

    Multiply every term of the first equation by 12 (the LCM of 3 and 4).

    4x+3y=364x + 3y = 364x+3y=36Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Multiply every term of the second equation by 10.

    x−2y=−2x - 2y = -2x−2y=−2Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Make x the subject of x−2y=−2x - 2y = -2x−2y=−2: add 2y2y2y to both sides.

    x=2y−2x = 2y - 2x=2y−2Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Substitute into 4x+3y=364x + 3y = 364x+3y=36.

    4(2y−2)+3y=364(2y - 2) + 3y = 364(2y−2)+3y=36Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Expand the bracket.

    8y−8+3y=368y - 8 + 3y = 368y−8+3y=36Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    Collect the y-terms.

    11y−8=3611y - 8 = 3611y−8=36Swipe to see the whole equation →
  7. 7

    Add 8 to both sides.

    11y=4411y = 4411y=44Swipe to see the whole equation →
  8. 8

    Divide by 11.

    y=4y = 4y=4Swipe to see the whole equation →
  9. 9

    Back-substitute into x=2y−2x = 2y - 2x=2y−2.

    x=8−2=6x = 8 - 2 = 6x=8−2=6Swipe to see the whole equation →

  10. Check: in the original equations: 63+44=2+1=3\dfrac{6}{3} + \dfrac{4}{4} = 2 + 1 = 336​+44​=2+1=3 ✔ and 0.1(6)−0.2(4)=0.6−0.8=−0.20.1(6) - 0.2(4) = 0.6 - 0.8 = -0.20.1(6)−0.2(4)=0.6−0.8=−0.2 ✔.

  11. Answer x=6x = 6x=6, y=4y = 4y=4

    Common trap: Multiplying x3+y4=3\dfrac{x}{3} + \dfrac{y}{4} = 33x​+4y​=3 by 12 on the left only, giving 4x+3y=34x + 3y = 34x+3y=3. That leads to x=0x = 0x=0, y=1y = 1y=1, which fails the original first equation: 0+14≠30 + \dfrac{1}{4} \neq 30+41​=3. The right side must be multiplied too.

Worked example 4

When the variable disappears

Question. Solve by substitution, if possible: 3x−6y=43x - 6y = 43x−6y=4 and x=2y+1x = 2y + 1x=2y+1.

Given: two equations; the second already gives x. Asked: the solution, if there is one.

Concept used: substitution; a false statement means no solution. Why it applies: x is already the subject of the second equation.

  1. 1

    Substitute x=2y+1x = 2y + 1x=2y+1 into 3x−6y=43x - 6y = 43x−6y=4.

    3(2y+1)−6y=43(2y + 1) - 6y = 43(2y+1)−6y=4Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Expand the bracket.

    6y+3−6y=46y + 3 - 6y = 46y+3−6y=4Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Collect the y-terms: 6y−6y=06y - 6y = 06y−6y=0.

    3=43 = 43=4Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    "3=43 = 43=4" is false, whatever the values of x and y. So no pair of values satisfies both equations.

  5. Check: the ratio test (Topic 3): x−2y−1=0x - 2y - 1 = 0x−2y−1=0 and 3x−6y−4=03x - 6y - 4 = 03x−6y−4=0 give a1a2=13\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{1}{3}a2​a1​​=31​, b1b2=−2−6=13\dfrac{b_1}{b_2} = \dfrac{-2}{-6} = \dfrac{1}{3}b2​b1​​=−6−2​=31​, c1c2=−1−4=14\dfrac{c_1}{c_2} = \dfrac{-1}{-4} = \dfrac{1}{4}c2​c1​​=−4−1​=41​. Equal, equal, not equal → parallel lines → no solution ✔.

  6. Answer The pair has no solution (it is inconsistent; the lines are parallel).

    Common trap: Deciding "I must have made a mistake" and changing a sign to force a value of y. The false statement is the answer.

While solving a pair by substitution, Arjun reaches the line 0=00 = 00=0. He writes "so x=0x = 0x=0". Is he right? What does 0=00 = 00=0 tell him?

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers see slips in substitution and elimination as one of the four commonest reasons for lost marks in this chapter. These are the substitution slips; Topic 5 has the elimination ones.

  • Substituting without brackets: 3x+2×2x−13x + 2 \times 2x - 13x+2×2x−1

    The multiplier must reach every term of the expression.

    Write 3x+2(2x−1)3x + 2(2x - 1)3x+2(2x−1), then expand.

  • Substituting back into the same equation

    It always gives a true statement such as 2x−1=2x−12x - 1 = 2x - 12x−1=2x−1.

    Substitute into the other equation.

  • Sign slip while making a variable the subject

    5x−y=165x - y = 165x−y=16 does not give y=16−5xy = 16 - 5xy=16−5x.

    One move at a time: 5x=16+y5x = 16 + y5x=16+y, then y=5x−16y = 5x - 16y=5x−16.

  • Reading 0=00 = 00=0 as "x=0x = 0x=0"

    No variable is left; the statement is true for every solution of the first equation.

    True statement → infinitely many; false statement → no solution.

  • Checking in one equation only

    A slip in the other equation goes unnoticed.

    Check the answer in both original equations.

Key points

  • Routine: make one variable the subject → substitute into the other equation (in brackets) → solve → back-substitute → check in both.
  • Choose a variable with coefficient 1 or −1-1−1 for the subject when you can.
  • Clear fractions (multiply by the LCM) and decimals (multiply by 10 or 100) first.
  • Variable vanishes and a false statement remains → no solution (parallel lines).
  • Variable vanishes and a true statement remains → infinitely many solutions (coincident lines).
