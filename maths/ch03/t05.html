Topic 5 of 6

The elimination method

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Solve a pair of linear equations by elimination.
  • Choose the more convenient method (substitution or elimination) for a given pair.
  • Interpret the outcomes 0 = c (c ≠ 0) and 0 = 0 when eliminating.

At a stationery shop, 3 pens and 2 erasers cost ₹ 36, and 5 pens and 2 erasers cost ₹ 52. Without any algebra you can say: the second bill has 2 more pens and the same erasers, and it costs ₹ 16 more — so one pen costs ₹ 8, and then the 2 erasers cost ₹ 12. You just eliminated the erasers by comparing the two bills. That is the whole idea of this method.

The idea

If the y-terms in two equations are equal (or opposite), subtracting (or adding) the equations removes y completely, leaving an equation in x alone. When they are not equal, you first multiply one or both equations by suitable numbers to make them equal. Multiplying every term of an equation by the same non-zero number does not change its solutions — the line stays the same.

The elimination routine

  1. Line up both equations in the form ax+by=cax + by = cax+by=c, with x-terms, y-terms and constants in columns.
  2. Match the coefficients of one variable: multiply one or both equations by suitable non-zero numbers so that these coefficients are numerically equal. Multiply every term, including the constant.
  3. Add or subtract the equations:
    - the matched coefficients have opposite signs (such as +2y+2y+2y and −2y-2y−2y) → add;
    - they have the same sign (such as +4y+4y+4y and +4y+4y+4y) → subtract, term by term, using brackets.
  4. Solve the equation in one variable.
  5. Back-substitute into either original equation to find the other variable.
  6. Check in both original equations.

When both variables vanish

If, in step 3, both variables disappear:

  • a false statement such as 0=70 = 70=7 → no solution (parallel lines, inconsistent);
  • a true statement 0=00 = 00=0 → infinitely many solutions (coincident lines, dependent).

Which method?

Both methods always work and give the same answer. If the question names a method ('by substitution', 'by elimination', 'graphically'), use that method. Otherwise, to pick the more convenient one, go down this list and use the first row that fits:

Check, in this order Use
1. One equation already says x=…x = \ldotsx=… or y=…y = \ldotsy=… substitution
2. The coefficients of x, or of y, are already equal or opposite elimination — add or subtract straight away
3. A variable has coefficient 1 or −1-1−1 substitution — make that variable the subject
4. None of the above elimination — multiply first

Worked example 1

Opposite coefficients: add

Question. Solve by elimination: 3x+2y=133x + 2y = 133x+2y=13 and 5x−2y=115x - 2y = 115x−2y=11.

Given: two equations with +2y+2y+2y and −2y-2y−2y. Asked: x and y.

Concept used: elimination by adding. Why it applies: the y-coefficients are opposite, so adding removes y.

  1. 1

    Add the two equations, column by column.

    (3x+5x)+(2y−2y)=13+11(3x + 5x) + (2y - 2y) = 13 + 11(3x+5x)+(2y−2y)=13+11Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Simplify each column.

    8x=248x = 248x=24Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Divide by 8.

    x=3x = 3x=3Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Back-substitute into 3x+2y=133x + 2y = 133x+2y=13.

    9+2y=139 + 2y = 139+2y=13Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Subtract 9 from both sides.

    2y=42y = 42y=4Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    Divide by 2.

    y=2y = 2y=2Swipe to see the whole equation →

  7. Check: 3(3)+2(2)=9+4=133(3) + 2(2) = 9 + 4 = 133(3)+2(2)=9+4=13 ✔ and 5(3)−2(2)=15−4=115(3) - 2(2) = 15 - 4 = 115(3)−2(2)=15−4=11 ✔.

  8. Answer x=3x = 3x=3, y=2y = 2y=2

    Common trap: Subtracting instead of adding: (3x−5x)+(2y+2y)=2(3x - 5x) + (2y + 2y) = 2(3x−5x)+(2y+2y)=2 gives −2x+4y=2-2x + 4y = 2−2x+4y=2 — y has not gone. Opposite signs → add.

Worked example 2

Multiply one equation, then subtract

Question. Solve by elimination: 5x+2y=165x + 2y = 165x+2y=16 and 3x+4y=183x + 4y = 183x+4y=18.

Given: two equations; no coefficient is 1 or −1-1−1. Asked: x and y.

Concept used: elimination — match the y-coefficients, then subtract. Why it applies: 4 is a multiple of 2, so one multiplication is enough.

  1. 1

    Multiply every term of the first equation by 2.

    10x+4y=3210x + 4y = 3210x+4y=32Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Both equations now have +4y+4y+4y (same sign), so subtract the second equation from this one, term by term.

    (10x−3x)+(4y−4y)=32−18(10x - 3x) + (4y - 4y) = 32 - 18(10x−3x)+(4y−4y)=32−18Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Simplify each column.

    7x=147x = 147x=14Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Divide by 7.

    x=2x = 2x=2Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Back-substitute into the original 5x+2y=165x + 2y = 165x+2y=16.

    10+2y=1610 + 2y = 1610+2y=16Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    Subtract 10 from both sides.

    2y=62y = 62y=6Swipe to see the whole equation →
  7. 7

    Divide by 2.

    y=3y = 3y=3Swipe to see the whole equation →

  8. Check: 5(2)+2(3)=165(2) + 2(3) = 165(2)+2(3)=16 ✔ and 3(2)+4(3)=6+12=183(2) + 4(3) = 6 + 12 = 183(2)+4(3)=6+12=18 ✔.

  9. Answer x=2x = 2x=2, y=3y = 3y=3

    Common trap: Multiplying only the left side by 2 and writing 10x+4y=1610x + 4y = 1610x+4y=16. Subtracting then gives 7x=−27x = -27x=−2. Multiply every term, including the constant.

Worked example 3

Multiply both equations

Question. Solve by elimination: 4x+3y=244x + 3y = 244x+3y=24 and 5x−2y=75x - 2y = 75x−2y=7.

Given: two equations whose coefficients are not multiples of each other. Asked: x and y.

Concept used: multiply both equations to match the y-coefficients (LCM of 3 and 2 is 6). Why it applies: +3y+3y+3y and −2y-2y−2y become +6y+6y+6y and −6y-6y−6y.

  1. 1

    Multiply every term of the first equation by 2.

    8x+6y=488x + 6y = 488x+6y=48Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Multiply every term of the second equation by 3.

    15x−6y=2115x - 6y = 2115x−6y=21Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    The y-coefficients are opposite: add the equations.

    (8x+15x)+(6y−6y)=48+21(8x + 15x) + (6y - 6y) = 48 + 21(8x+15x)+(6y−6y)=48+21Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Simplify each column.

    23x=6923x = 6923x=69Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Divide by 23.

    x=3x = 3x=3Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    Back-substitute into the original 4x+3y=244x + 3y = 244x+3y=24.

    12+3y=2412 + 3y = 2412+3y=24Swipe to see the whole equation →
  7. 7

    Subtract 12 from both sides.

    3y=123y = 123y=12Swipe to see the whole equation →
  8. 8

    Divide by 3.

    y=4y = 4y=4Swipe to see the whole equation →

  9. Check: 4(3)+3(4)=244(3) + 3(4) = 244(3)+3(4)=24 ✔ and 5(3)−2(4)=15−8=75(3) - 2(4) = 15 - 8 = 75(3)−2(4)=15−8=7 ✔.

  10. Answer x=3x = 3x=3, y=4y = 4y=4

    Common trap: After multiplying, subtracting by habit: (8x−15x)+(6y+6y)=48−21(8x - 15x) + (6y + 6y) = 48 - 21(8x−15x)+(6y+6y)=48−21 gives −7x+12y=27-7x + 12y = 27−7x+12y=27, with y still there. Look at the signs before you choose add or subtract.

Worked example 4

When both variables vanish

Question. Solve by elimination: 6x−9y=156x - 9y = 156x−9y=15 and 4x−6y=104x - 6y = 104x−6y=10.

Given: two equations. Asked: all solutions.

Concept used: elimination; a true statement with no variable means infinitely many solutions. Why it applies: matching the x-coefficients also matches the y-coefficients here.

  1. 1

    Multiply every term of the first equation by 2.

    12x−18y=3012x - 18y = 3012x−18y=30Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Multiply every term of the second equation by 3.

    12x−18y=3012x - 18y = 3012x−18y=30Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Subtract the second from the first.

    0=00 = 00=0Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    "0=00 = 00=0" is true and has no variable: every solution of one equation satisfies the other. The pair has infinitely many solutions.

  5. 5

    Both equations are multiples of 2x−3y=52x - 3y = 52x−3y=5 (divide the first by 3, the second by 2). Its solutions are all pairs with y=2x−53y = \dfrac{2x - 5}{3}y=32x−5​, for example (1,−1)(1, -1)(1,−1) and (4,1)(4, 1)(4,1).

  6. Check: the ratio test: 64=32\dfrac{6}{4} = \dfrac{3}{2}46​=23​, −9−6=32\dfrac{-9}{-6} = \dfrac{3}{2}−6−9​=23​, 1510=32\dfrac{15}{10} = \dfrac{3}{2}1015​=23​ — all equal, coincident ✔. And (4,1)(4, 1)(4,1): 24−9=1524 - 9 = 1524−9=15 ✔, 16−6=1016 - 6 = 1016−6=10 ✔.

  7. Answer Infinitely many solutions: every pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) with 2x−3y=52x - 3y = 52x−3y=5 (the lines are coincident).

    Common trap: Writing "0=00 = 00=0, so x=0x = 0x=0 and y=0y = 0y=0". Test it: (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) gives 0≠150 \neq 150=15. No variable is left, so nothing is being "solved" for.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers see slips in elimination and substitution as one of the four commonest reasons for lost marks in this chapter. These are the elimination slips.

  • Subtracting when the matched terms have opposite signs

    +2y−(−2y)=4y+2y - (-2y) = 4y+2y−(−2y)=4y: the variable stays.

    Opposite signs → add; same signs → subtract.

  • Multiplying the left side but not the constant

    The new equation is no longer the same line.

    Multiply every term, constant included.

  • −3y−(−3y)=−6y-3y - (-3y) = -6y−3y−(−3y)=−6y

    Subtracting a negative term adds it.

    Put brackets round the whole equation you subtract.

  • Back-substituting into a multiplied equation with a slip in it

    The slip passes into the second variable.

    Back-substitute into an original equation.

  • Stopping after finding one variable

    A solution is a pair of values.

    Find both x and y, then check in both equations.

  • "0=00 = 00=0, so x=y=0x = y = 0x=y=0"

    No variable is left; nothing was solved for.

    0=00 = 00=0 → infinitely many; 0=c0 = c0=c with c≠0c \neq 0c=0 → none.

Key points

  • Routine: line up → match one coefficient (multiply every term) → add if signs are opposite, subtract if the same → solve → back-substitute into an original equation → check in both.
  • Multiplying an equation by a non-zero number does not change its line.
  • Both variables vanish: 0=c0 = c0=c (c≠0c \neq 0c=0) → no solution; 0=00 = 00=0 → infinitely many.
  • Which method? Use the first row that fits: already x=…x = \ldotsx=… or y=…y = \ldotsy=… → substitution; equal or opposite coefficients → elimination; a coefficient of 1 or −1-1−1 → substitution; otherwise → elimination.
  • Both methods always give the same answer — the choice is only about convenience.
