Topic 3 of 6

Consistency from the coefficients

About 50 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Use the ratios a₁/a₂, b₁/b₂ and c₁/c₂ to decide whether a pair has one, no or infinitely many solutions.
  • Use the terms consistent, inconsistent and dependent correctly.
  • Find an unknown coefficient (k) for a given type of solution, and write a second equation that makes a given type of pair.

Two railway tracks are laid along the lines 2x−6y=52x - 6y = 52x−6y=5 and −3x+9y=1-3x + 9y = 1−3x+9y=1. Will they ever cross? You could draw both lines carefully and look — or you could answer in ten seconds, just by comparing the numbers in front of x, in front of y, and on their own. This topic shows you how.

Why the coefficients tell the story

Write both equations in the same form:

a1x+b1y+c1=0a2x+b2y+c2=0a_1x + b_1y + c_1 = 0 \qquad a_2x + b_2y + c_2 = 0a1​x+b1​y+c1​=0a2​x+b2​y+c2​=0Swipe to see the whole equation →
  • If the whole second equation is a multiple of the first (for example 2x+3y−5=02x + 3y - 5 = 02x+3y−5=0 and 4x+6y−10=04x + 6y - 10 = 04x+6y−10=0), both equations say the same thing: the lines are coincident.
  • If only the x- and y-parts match up as multiples but the constants do not (for example 2x+3y−5=02x + 3y - 5 = 02x+3y−5=0 and 4x+6y−7=04x + 6y - 7 = 04x+6y−7=0), then 4x+6y4x + 6y4x+6y would have to equal both 10 and 7 at once — impossible. The lines are parallel.
  • If even the x- and y-parts do not match up, the lines slant differently, so they intersect at exactly one point.

Comparing the ratios a1a2\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}a2​a1​​, b1b2\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}b2​b1​​ and c1c2\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}c2​c1​​ is a quick way of asking "is it a multiple?".

The ratio test (row 1 needs a2,b2≠0a_2, b_2 \neq 0a2​,b2​=0; rows 2–3 need a2,b2,c2≠0a_2, b_2, c_2 \neq 0a2​,b2​,c2​=0)

If a denominator you need is 0, use the multiple test below.

Two rules make the test reliable:

  1. Write both equations in the same form — both as ax+by+c=0ax + by + c = 0ax+by+c=0, or both as ax+by=dax + by = dax+by=d. If one has its constant on the left and the other on the right, the sign of c1c2\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}c2​c1​​ comes out wrong.
  2. Look at the first row first. If a1a2≠b1b2\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} \neq \dfrac{b_1}{b_2}a2​a1​​=b2​b1​​, you are done: one solution. Only when a1a2=b1b2\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{b_1}{b_2}a2​a1​​=b2​b1​​ do you need c1c2\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}c2​c1​​.

When a coefficient is 0: the multiple test

A ratio such as a1a2\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}a2​a1​​ has no value when a2=0a_2 = 0a2​=0. Lines like y=3y = 3y=3 (where a=0a = 0a=0) or x=−1x = -1x=−1 (where b=0b = 0b=0) are common, so use this test instead — it always works:

Look for a number m with a2=m×a1a_2 = m \times a_1a2​=m×a1​ and b2=m×b1b_2 = m \times b_1b2​=m×b1​.

  • No such m → the lines intersect: exactly one solution.
  • Such an m, and also c2=m×c1c_2 = m \times c_1c2​=m×c1​ → coincident: infinitely many solutions.
  • Such an m, but c2≠m×c1c_2 \neq m \times c_1c2​=m×c1​ → parallel: no solution.

For example, y=3y = 3y=3 and y=−1y = -1y=−1 are 0x+y−3=00x + y - 3 = 00x+y−3=0 and 0x+y+1=00x + y + 1 = 00x+y+1=0: with m=1m = 1m=1 the x- and y-parts match, but 1≠1×(−3)1 \neq 1 \times (-3)1=1×(−3), so the lines are parallel (two horizontal lines).

Writing a second equation of a given type

Start from a given line, say 3x−4y+6=03x - 4y + 6 = 03x−4y+6=0:

  • Intersecting: change the x- and y-coefficients so their ratio differs, e.g. 4x+3y−1=04x + 3y - 1 = 04x+3y−1=0.
  • Parallel: multiply the x- and y-parts by the same number but choose a different constant, e.g. 6x−8y+5=06x - 8y + 5 = 06x−8y+5=0.
  • Coincident: multiply the whole equation by a non-zero number, e.g. 9x−12y+18=09x - 12y + 18 = 09x−12y+18=0.

Worked example 5

Writing a partner equation

Working: a1a2=42=2\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{4}{2} = 2a2​a1​​=24​=2, b1b2=−6−3=2\dfrac{b_1}{b_2} = \dfrac{-6}{-3} = 2b2​b1​​=−3−6​=2, c1c2=35\dfrac{c_1}{c_2} = \dfrac{3}{5}c2​c1​​=53​. The first two are equal and the third is different: parallel lines, no solution.

"Inconsistent" means "no solution". Check c1c2\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}c2​c1​​ whenever the first two ratios are equal.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers see ratio conditions, including finding k, as one of the four commonest reasons for lost marks in this chapter.

  • Treating a1a2=b1b2≠c1c2\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{b_1}{b_2} \neq \dfrac{c_1}{c_2}a2​a1​​=b2​b1​​=c2​c1​​ as "infinitely many"

    Infinitely many needs all three ratios equal.

    Equal, equal, not equal → parallel → no solution.

  • One equation in "=0= 0=0" form, the other with the constant on the right

    The sign of c1c2\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}c2​c1​​ flips.

    Put both equations in the same form first.

  • "No solution, so k=3k = 3k=3" — without testing the constants

    That k may make the lines coincident.

    After a1a2=b1b2\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{b_1}{b_2}a2​a1​​=b2​b1​​, check c1c2\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}c2​c1​​.

  • For infinitely many, solving only a1a2=b1b2\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{b_1}{b_2}a2​a1​​=b2​b1​​

    k must satisfy both equalities.

    Solve both; if they disagree, write "no value of k".

  • Writing 00\dfrac{0}{0}00​ or 30\dfrac{3}{0}03​ as a number

    Division by 0 has no value.

    Use the multiple test when a coefficient or constant is 0.

  • Turning one ratio upside down (k2=36\dfrac{k}{2} = \dfrac{3}{6}2k​=63​)

    Each ratio is (first equation) ÷ (second equation).

    Keep the first equation on top in every ratio.

Key points

  • Same form for both equations, then compare a1a2\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}a2​a1​​, b1b2\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}b2​b1​​, c1c2\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}c2​c1​​.
  • a1a2≠b1b2\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} \neq \dfrac{b_1}{b_2}a2​a1​​=b2​b1​​: one solution. Equal, equal, not equal: no solution. All equal: infinitely many.
  • Consistent = at least one solution; inconsistent = none; dependent = infinitely many (and so consistent).
  • A coefficient is 0? Use the multiple test: a2=ma1a_2 = m a_1a2​=ma1​ and b2=mb1b_2 = m b_1b2​=mb1​; then compare c2c_2c2​ with mc1m c_1mc1​.
  • Finding k: unique → "k≠k \neqk= …"; no solution → solve, then test the constants; infinitely many → k must satisfy both equalities.
