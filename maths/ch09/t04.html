Topic 4 of 5

Two right triangles with a shared height

About 60 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Solve problems where one height is seen at two angles from two points on the same side — a moving observer, shadows at two times, two ships, a car approaching at a steady speed.
  • Solve problems where the two points are on opposite sides of the height — the banks of a river, two poles across a road.
  • Solve problems where an object moves along a horizontal line at a fixed height (a balloon, a drone, an aircraft), including the observer's eye height.

You look at the top of a temple tower from the gate: the angle of elevation is 30°. You walk 28 m straight towards it and look again: now it is 60°. Neither position alone tells you the height, because you do not know how far you are from the foot. But the two looks together do. The trick is that both right triangles share one side — the tower — and that one shared side ties the two equations together.

The shared-height method

In every problem of this topic, one vertical length is a side of both right triangles: the tower, the lighthouse, the height of the bridge, or the height of a balloon above the eye. Call it h.

  1. Draw both triangles on one figure, and also (in rough) separately. Mark the shared height h.
  2. Write each ground distance in terms of h, from tan⁡θ=hdistance\tan \theta = \dfrac{h}{\text{distance}}tanθ=distanceh​:
Angle Ground distance
60° h3\dfrac{h}{\sqrt{3}}3​h​ (closest)
45° hhh
30° h3h\sqrt{3}h3​ (farthest)
  1. Use the extra fact the question gives about the two distances:
    - both points on the same side (a walk towards the tower, two ships one behind the other, two shadows): the given length is the difference of the two distances;
    - points on opposite sides (two banks of a river, two poles across a road): the given length is the sum.
  2. Solve for h (or for the unknown distance), rationalise, and give the decimal last.

You may also let x be the shorter distance instead of using h — Worked example 1 shows this. Either way, the key is the same: the same h appears in both triangles, and the second distance is x+dx + dx+d (or x−dx - dx−d), not a new unknown.

An object moving at a fixed height

When a balloon, drone or aircraft moves along a horizontal line, its height above the observer's eye is the shared side. The observer stays still; the two angles give two horizontal distances, and the distance travelled is their difference. If the observer's eyes are above the ground, subtract the eye height first: the shared side is (height of the object) − (eye height).

Steady speed and time

If a car approaches the foot of a tower at a steady speed, time is in proportion to distance: if it covers a distance D1D_1D1​ in time t1t_1t1​, it covers D2D_2D2​ in t1×D2D1t_1 \times \dfrac{D_2}{D_1}t1​×D1​D2​​. Find the two distances in terms of h — the h then cancels.

Worked example 1

Walking towards a tower

Question. From a point on level ground, the angle of elevation of the top of a tower is 30°. On walking 28 m towards the tower, the angle of elevation becomes 60°. Find the height of the tower and the distance of the second point from the tower. (Take 3=1.732\sqrt{3} = 1.7323​=1.732.)

Given: angles 30° and 60°; the walk is 28 m. Asked: the height, and the distance of the second point from the foot.

Concept used: two right triangles share the height; the walk is the difference of the two ground distances. Why it applies: both points are on the same side, in a line with the foot.

Worked example 2

Two shadows

Question. The shadow of a vertical pole on level ground is 12 m longer when the Sun's altitude is 45° than when it is 60°. Find the height of the pole. (Take 3=1.732\sqrt{3} = 1.7323​=1.732.)

Given: altitudes 45° and 60°; the difference of the shadows is 12 m. Asked: the height h.

Concept used: shared height; the two shadows written in terms of h; their difference is 12 m. Why it applies: the pole is the vertical side of both triangles, and both shadows start at its foot.

  1. 1

    Let the pole be AB (height h m). Shadow at 60°: BC=htan⁡60°=h3BC = \dfrac{h}{\tan 60°} = \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{3}}BC=tan60°h​=3​h​.

  2. 2

    Shadow at 45°: BD=htan⁡45°=hBD = \dfrac{h}{\tan 45°} = hBD=tan45°h​=h.

  3. 3

    The 45° shadow is 12 m longer.

    h−h3=12h - \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{3}} = 12h−3​h​=12Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Multiply every term by 3\sqrt{3}3​: h3−h=123h\sqrt{3} - h = 12\sqrt{3}h3​−h=123​, so h(3−1)=123h(\sqrt{3} - 1) = 12\sqrt{3}h(3​−1)=123​.

  5. 5

    Divide and rationalise (the denominator becomes 3−1=23 - 1 = 23−1=2).

    h=1233−1=123(3+1)2=63(3+1)=18+63h = \dfrac{12\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3} - 1} = \dfrac{12\sqrt{3}(\sqrt{3} + 1)}{2} = 6\sqrt{3}(\sqrt{3} + 1) = 18 + 6\sqrt{3}h=3​−1123​​=2123​(3​+1)​=63​(3​+1)=18+63​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    Decimal: 6×1.732=10.3926 \times 1.732 = 10.3926×1.732=10.392, so h=18+10.392=28.392h = 18 + 10.392 = 28.392h=18+10.392=28.392, about 28.39 m.

  7. Check: shadows 28.3928.3928.39 m (at 45°) and 28.391.732≈16.39\dfrac{28.39}{1.732} \approx 16.391.73228.39​≈16.39 m (at 60°); their difference is 12 m ✔.

  8. Answer (18+63)(18 + 6\sqrt{3})(18+63​) m, that is 6(3+3)6(3 + \sqrt{3})6(3+3​) m, about 28.39 m

Worked example 3

Two ships seen from a lighthouse

Question. From the top of a lighthouse 54 m high above sea level, the angles of depression of two ships are 60° and 45°. One ship is exactly behind the other, on the same side of the lighthouse. Find the distance between the two ships. (Take 3=1.732\sqrt{3} = 1.7323​=1.732.)

Given: height 54 m; angles of depression 60° and 45°. Asked: the distance between the ships.

Concept used: alternate angles; shared height; the distance between the ships is the difference of their distances from the foot. Why it applies: both ships are on the same side, in a line with the foot.

  1. 1

    Let AB be the lighthouse (A the top), C the nearer ship (depression 60°) and D the farther ship (depression 45°). By alternate angles, ∠ACB=60°\angle ACB = 60°∠ACB=60° and ∠ADB=45°\angle ADB = 45°∠ADB=45°.

  2. 2

    In right triangle ABC: tan⁡60°=54BC\tan 60° = \dfrac{54}{BC}tan60°=BC54​, so BC=543=183BC = \dfrac{54}{\sqrt{3}} = 18\sqrt{3}BC=3​54​=183​.

  3. 3

    In right triangle ABD: tan⁡45°=54BD\tan 45° = \dfrac{54}{BD}tan45°=BD54​, so BD=54BD = 54BD=54.

  4. 4

    Distance between the ships: CD=BD−BC=54−183=18(3−3)CD = BD - BC = 54 - 18\sqrt{3} = 18(3 - \sqrt{3})CD=BD−BC=54−183​=18(3−3​).

  5. 5

    Decimal: 18×1.732=31.17618 \times 1.732 = 31.17618×1.732=31.176, so CD=54−31.176=22.824CD = 54 - 31.176 = 22.824CD=54−31.176=22.824, about 22.82 m.

  6. Check: the nearer ship is seen more steeply (60°), so it must be closer than 54 m: 31.18<5431.18 < 5431.18<54 ✔.

  7. Answer 18(3−3)18(3 - \sqrt{3})18(3−3​) m, about 22.82 m

    Common trap: Adding the two distances (54+18354 + 18\sqrt{3}54+183​). That would be right only if the ships were on opposite sides of the lighthouse.

Worked example 4

The width of a river (opposite sides)

Question. From a point on a bridge across a river, the angles of depression of the banks on opposite sides of the river are 30° and 60°. The bridge is 12 m above the level of the banks. Find the width of the river. (Take 3=1.732\sqrt{3} = 1.7323​=1.732.)

Given: height 12 m; angles of depression 30° and 60°, to opposite banks. Asked: the width.

Concept used: shared height; the width is the sum of the two ground distances. Why it applies: the banks are on opposite sides of the point directly below the observer.

  1. 1

    Let P be the point on the bridge, N the point on the line of the banks directly below it (PN=12PN = 12PN=12 m), A the bank seen at 30° and B the bank seen at 60°. By alternate angles, ∠PAN=30°\angle PAN = 30°∠PAN=30° and ∠PBN=60°\angle PBN = 60°∠PBN=60°.

  2. 2

    In right triangle PNA: tan⁡30°=12AN\tan 30° = \dfrac{12}{AN}tan30°=AN12​, so AN=123AN = 12\sqrt{3}AN=123​.

  3. 3

    In right triangle PNB: tan⁡60°=12NB\tan 60° = \dfrac{12}{NB}tan60°=NB12​, so NB=123=43NB = \dfrac{12}{\sqrt{3}} = 4\sqrt{3}NB=3​12​=43​.

  4. 4

    Width AB=AN+NB=123+43=163AB = AN + NB = 12\sqrt{3} + 4\sqrt{3} = 16\sqrt{3}AB=AN+NB=123​+43​=163​.

  5. 5

    Decimal: 16×1.732=27.71216 \times 1.732 = 27.71216×1.732=27.712, about 27.71 m.

  6. Check: the 30° bank is three times as far as the 60° bank (123=3×4312\sqrt{3} = 3 \times 4\sqrt{3}123​=3×43​), as it must be, since tan⁡60°=3tan⁡30°\tan 60° = 3\tan 30°tan60°=3tan30° ✔.

  7. Answer 16316\sqrt{3}163​ m, about 27.71 m

    Common trap: Subtracting (123−43=8312\sqrt{3} - 4\sqrt{3} = 8\sqrt{3}123​−43​=83​) as in the two-ships problem. Here the banks are on opposite sides, so the width is the sum.

Worked example 5

A car at a steady speed

Question. A man at the top of a vertical tower sees a car moving at a steady speed straight towards the foot of the tower. The angle of depression of the car changes from 45° to 60° in 12 seconds. How much longer will the car take to reach the foot of the tower? (Take 3=1.732\sqrt{3} = 1.7323​=1.732.)

Given: angles of depression 45° then 60°; the time between them is 12 s; steady speed. Asked: the time from the second position to the foot.

Concept used: shared height h; distances in terms of h; at a steady speed, time is in proportion to distance. Why it applies: the tower is the shared vertical side; the car moves along the ground line through its foot.

  1. 1

    Let the tower be AB (AB=hAB = hAB=h), D the first position (45°) and C the second (60°). By alternate angles ∠ADB=45°\angle ADB = 45°∠ADB=45° and ∠ACB=60°\angle ACB = 60°∠ACB=60°.

  2. 2

    BD=htan⁡45°=hBD = \dfrac{h}{\tan 45°} = hBD=tan45°h​=h and BC=htan⁡60°=h3BC = \dfrac{h}{\tan 60°} = \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{3}}BC=tan60°h​=3​h​.

  3. 3

    In 12 s the car covers DC=h−h3=h(3−1)3DC = h - \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{h(\sqrt{3} - 1)}{\sqrt{3}}DC=h−3​h​=3​h(3​−1)​.

  4. 4

    It still has to cover BC=h3BC = \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{3}}BC=3​h​. Time is in proportion to distance:

    t=12×BCDC=12×h3h(3−1)3=123−1t = 12 \times \dfrac{BC}{DC} = 12 \times \dfrac{\dfrac{h}{\sqrt{3}}}{\dfrac{h(\sqrt{3} - 1)}{\sqrt{3}}} = \dfrac{12}{\sqrt{3} - 1}t=12×DCBC​=12×3​h(3​−1)​3​h​​=3​−112​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Rationalise: t=12(3+1)2=6(3+1)t = \dfrac{12(\sqrt{3} + 1)}{2} = 6(\sqrt{3} + 1)t=212(3​+1)​=6(3​+1).

  6. 6

    Decimal: 6×2.732=16.3926 \times 2.732 = 16.3926×2.732=16.392, about 16.39 s.

  7. Check: the car still has more than half of BD to go (BC≈0.577hBC \approx 0.577hBC≈0.577h out of h), so it should need more than the 12 s it took for the first part (DC≈0.423hDC \approx 0.423hDC≈0.423h) ✔.

  8. Answer 6(3+1)6(\sqrt{3} + 1)6(3​+1) seconds, about 16.39 s

    Common trap: Saying "the angle grew by 15° in 12 s, so it will take another 24 s to grow by 30° to 90°". Angles do not change at a steady rate; only the distance does.

Worked example 6

A drone at a fixed height

Question. A boy whose eyes are 1.3 m above the ground watches a drone flying in a straight horizontal line, 31.3 m above the ground, away from him. At one instant the angle of elevation of the drone from his eyes is 60°; a little later it is 45°. Find the distance the drone travelled between the two observations. (Take 3=1.732\sqrt{3} = 1.7323​=1.732.)

Given: eye height 1.3 m; drone's height 31.3 m; angles 60° and 45°. Asked: the distance travelled.

Concept used: the shared side is the drone's height above the eye; the distance travelled is the difference of the two horizontal distances. Why it applies: the drone stays at one height, and the boy stays still.

  1. 1

    Height above the eye: 31.3−1.3=3031.3 - 1.3 = 3031.3−1.3=30 m.

  2. 2

    Let E be the eye, and M₁, M₂ the points at eye level directly below the two positions D₁, D₂ of the drone. Then D1M1=D2M2=30D_1M_1 = D_2M_2 = 30D1​M1​=D2​M2​=30 m.

  3. 3

    First position (60°): tan⁡60°=30EM1\tan 60° = \dfrac{30}{EM_1}tan60°=EM1​30​, so EM1=303=103EM_1 = \dfrac{30}{\sqrt{3}} = 10\sqrt{3}EM1​=3​30​=103​.

  4. 4

    Second position (45°): tan⁡45°=30EM2\tan 45° = \dfrac{30}{EM_2}tan45°=EM2​30​, so EM2=30EM_2 = 30EM2​=30.

  5. 5

    Distance travelled: D1D2=M1M2=EM2−EM1=30−103=10(3−3)D_1D_2 = M_1M_2 = EM_2 - EM_1 = 30 - 10\sqrt{3} = 10(3 - \sqrt{3})D1​D2​=M1​M2​=EM2​−EM1​=30−103​=10(3−3​).

  6. 6

    Decimal: 30−17.32=12.6830 - 17.32 = 12.6830−17.32=12.68 m.

  7. Check: the drone moves away, so the angle should fall (60° → 45°) and the horizontal distance should grow (17.32→3017.32 \to 3017.32→30) ✔.

  8. Answer 10(3−3)10(3 - \sqrt{3})10(3−3​) m, about 12.68 m

    Common trap: Using 31.3 m as the vertical side. The angles are measured at the eye, so the vertical side is 31.3−1.3=3031.3 - 1.3 = 3031.3−1.3=30 m.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers say that not using the shared side is one of the most common reasons for lost marks in two-triangle problems.

  • The walk (or the distance between two ships) used as the whole base of one triangle

    It is only the gap between the two points, not a distance from the foot.

    Let the nearer distance be x; the farther one is x+dx + dx+d.

  • The second distance written as a new letter y with no link to x

    Two unknown distances and a height cannot be found from two equations.

    Use the shared side: both distances in terms of h (or x and x+dx + dx+d).

  • Adding the distances for two points on the same side (or subtracting for opposite sides)

    Same side → difference; opposite sides → sum.

    Draw both points before you write the equation.

  • Using the full height of a balloon or drone when the eye height is given

    Angles are measured at the eye.

    Shared side = height of the object − eye height.

  • 1233−1=123(3−1)\dfrac{12\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3} - 1} = 12\sqrt{3}(\sqrt{3} - 1)3​−1123​​=123​(3​−1)

    The conjugate must be multiplied top and bottom, and the denominator becomes 2.

    123(3+1)2\dfrac{12\sqrt{3}(\sqrt{3} + 1)}{2}2123​(3​+1)​.

Key points

  • Two triangles with a shared height: mark h once; write each ground distance from the table — h3\dfrac{h}{\sqrt{3}}3​h​ at 60°, h at 45°, h3h\sqrt{3}h3​ at 30°.
  • Same side → the given length is the difference; opposite sides → the sum.
  • An object at a fixed height: the shared side is its height above the eye; the distance travelled is the difference of the horizontal distances.
  • Steady speed: time is in proportion to distance; the height cancels.
  • Rationalise, then give the decimal with the value of 3\sqrt{3}3​ the question gives.
