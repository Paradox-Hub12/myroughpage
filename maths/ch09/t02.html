Topic 2 of 5

Line of sight, angle of elevation and angle of depression

About 45 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Define the line of sight, the angle of elevation and the angle of depression, and identify them in a picture or a situation.
  • Draw a correct figure from a word problem — verticals at right angles to the ground, the eye height, the horizontal through the eye, and the angle marked from the horizontal.
  • Use alternate angles to show that the angle of depression from one point equals the angle of elevation from the other, and use the Sun's altitude for shadows.

Stand in a school corridor and look up at a fan on the ceiling, then down at a coin on the floor. Your eyes did the same thing twice: they turned away from straight ahead — up the first time, down the second. That amount of turning is an angle, and it is all this chapter needs. Measure it once, add one length you know, and trigonometry gives you a height or a distance you could never reach with a tape.

Three words

  • The line of sight is the straight line from the observer's eye to the point being looked at.
  • The horizontal is the level line through the observer's eye ("straight ahead"). On paper it is drawn parallel to the ground.
  • When the point is above the horizontal (you raise your head), the angle between the line of sight and the horizontal is the angle of elevation.
  • When the point is below the horizontal (you lower your head), the angle between the line of sight and the horizontal is the angle of depression.

Both angles are measured from the horizontal — never from the vertical. A person on a roof looking at a car below has an angle of depression; the person beside the car looking up at the roof has an angle of elevation.

Two facts about the real world that go into every figure

  1. Towers, poles, trees, buildings and walls are vertical, and the ground is horizontal, so each of them makes a right angle with the ground. That right angle is what gives us a right triangle.
  2. The eye is not at the feet. If an observer's eyes are 1.4 m above the ground, the horizontal starts 1.4 m up. The right triangle then has its corner at the eye, and its vertical side is only the part of the tower above eye level (Topic 3).

The depression angle moves into the triangle

Suppose you stand at the top A of a building and look down at a car C on the road. The angle of depression θ is outside the triangle — between the horizontal through A and the line AC. The ground through C is also horizontal, so the two horizontals are parallel, and AC cuts both of them. The angle of depression at A and the angle at C between the ground and CA are alternate angles, so they are equal:

Angle of depression of C from A = angle of elevation of A from C.

So you can always write the angle at the ground point, inside the right triangle, and use it there. (The angle of the triangle at A is 90°−θ90° - \theta90°−θ, not θ.)

The Sun's altitude

The altitude of the Sun is its angle of elevation. Sunlight falling past the top of a pole ends at the tip of the shadow, so the Sun's altitude is the angle at the tip of the shadow in the right triangle (pole, shadow, sunray). A higher Sun gives a bigger angle and a shorter shadow. At 45° the shadow is exactly as long as the pole.

The drawing routine (use it every time)

  1. Draw the ground as a horizontal line.
  2. Draw every tower, pole or building as a vertical line from the ground, with a right-angle mark.
  3. Mark the eye of each observer (at the eye height, if one is given) and draw a dashed horizontal through it if the observer looks down.
  4. Draw the line of sight to each point that is looked at.
  5. Mark each angle from the horizontal; move any angle of depression to the ground point (alternate angles). Write the given lengths on the figure and give each unknown a letter (h, x, d).

Then look for the right triangle(s) that contain one known length and the length you want.

Worked example 1

Naming the angles in a scene

Question. Riya stands on the flat roof of a building. She looks up at a kite and down at her friend Farhan, who is standing on the ground. Farhan looks up at Riya. (a) Name each of the three angles that are formed. (b) The angle of depression of Farhan from Riya is 30°. What is the angle of elevation of Riya from Farhan?

Given: two observers, three lines of sight; one angle of depression, 30°. Asked: the names of the angles, and Farhan's angle of elevation.

Concept used: elevation when the point is above the observer's horizontal, depression when it is below; the alternate-angle fact. Why it applies: the horizontal at Riya's eye and the ground at Farhan are parallel.

  1. 1

    (a) Riya → kite: the kite is above her horizontal, so this is an angle of elevation.

  2. 2

    Riya → Farhan: Farhan is below her horizontal, so this is an angle of depression.

  3. 3

    Farhan → Riya: Riya is above Farhan's horizontal (the ground), so this is an angle of elevation.

  4. 4

    (b) The horizontal through Riya's eye and the ground are parallel, and the line of sight between them crosses both. The angle of depression at Riya and the angle of elevation at Farhan are alternate angles, so they are equal: 30°.

  5. Check: draw it to scale with any numbers that fit — say Riya's eye 12 m up and Farhan 12312\sqrt{3}123​ m (about 20.8 m) from the foot of the building. Then tan⁡(angle at Farhan)=12123=13\tan(\text{angle at Farhan}) = \dfrac{12}{12\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}tan(angle at Farhan)=123​12​=3​1​, which is tan⁡30°\tan 30°tan30° ✔.

  6. Answer (a) elevation (Riya → kite), depression (Riya → Farhan), elevation (Farhan → Riya) (b) 30°

    Common trap: Answering 60° in (b), by measuring Riya's angle from the vertical wall instead of from the horizontal. 60° is the angle inside the triangle at Riya, not the angle of depression.

Worked example 2

Drawing the figure when the eye height is given

Question. A boy whose eyes are 1.4 m above the ground stands 24 m from the foot of a vertical pole. The angle of elevation of the top of the pole from his eyes is 30°. Draw the figure, name the right triangle you will use, and find the height of the pole. (Take 3=1.732\sqrt{3} = 1.7323​=1.732.)

Worked example 3

Moving the angle of depression

Question. From the top of a building 18 m high, the angle of depression of a car parked on the road is 60°. Draw the figure, move the angle into the triangle, and find how far the car is from the foot of the building.

Given: height 18 m; angle of depression 60°. Asked: the distance of the car from the foot of the building.

Concept used: alternate angles move the depression angle to the car; tan links the height and the ground distance. Why it applies: the horizontal through the top is parallel to the ground.

  1. 1

    Let AB be the building (A the top, B the foot) and C the car. Draw a dashed horizontal AX through A, on the side of the car. ∠XAC=60°\angle XAC = 60°∠XAC=60° is the angle of depression.

Worked example 4

The Sun's altitude from a shadow

Question. A tree 15 m tall casts a shadow 535\sqrt{3}53​ m long on level ground. (a) Find the Sun's altitude. (b) Later the same day the shadow is 15 m long. What is the Sun's altitude now — is the Sun higher or lower than before?

Given: height 15 m; shadow 535\sqrt{3}53​ m, then 15 m. Asked: the two altitudes.

Concept used: the Sun's altitude is the angle of elevation of the Sun at the tip of the shadow; tan = height ÷ shadow. Why it applies: tree, shadow and sunray make a right triangle.

  1. 1

    (a) Let θ be the Sun's altitude. In the right triangle (tree, shadow, sunray), the tree is opposite θ and the shadow is adjacent.

    tan⁡θ=1553=33=3\tan \theta = \dfrac{15}{5\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{3}{\sqrt{3}} = \sqrt{3}tanθ=53​15​=3​3​=3​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    tan⁡60°=3\tan 60° = \sqrt{3}tan60°=3​, so θ=60°\theta = 60°θ=60°.

  3. 3

    (b) tan⁡θ=1515=1\tan \theta = \dfrac{15}{15} = 1tanθ=1515​=1, so θ=45°\theta = 45°θ=45°.

  4. 4

    The altitude fell from 60° to 45°: the Sun is lower — and the shadow is longer.

  5. Check: in (b) the shadow equals the tree, and equal shorter sides mean a 45° angle ✔; in (a) the shadow (53≈8.75\sqrt{3} \approx 8.753​≈8.7 m) is shorter than the tree, so the angle must be more than 45° ✔.

  6. Answer (a) 60° (b) 45°; the Sun is lower.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers say that mixing up elevation and depression, or putting the angle of depression in the wrong place, is one of the most common reasons for lost marks in this chapter.

  • Angle of depression measured from the vertical wall or tower

    Both angles are measured from the horizontal.

    Draw a dashed horizontal through the eye and mark the angle below it.

  • The 60° depression written as the angle inside the triangle at the top

    Inside the triangle the angle at the top is 90°−60°=30°90° - 60° = 30°90°−60°=30°.

    Move the angle to the ground point: "∠ACB = 60° (alternate angles)".

  • Elevation and depression named the wrong way round

    The name depends on who is looking: down is depression, up is elevation.

    Ask "is the point above or below this observer's eye?"

  • The right triangle drawn from the observer's feet when the eye height is given

    Lines of sight start at the eye.

    Draw the horizontal through the eye; the triangle's vertical side is the part above eye level.

  • Tower drawn leaning, or no right-angle mark

    Without the right angle there is no right triangle.

    Verticals at right angles to the ground, with the right-angle box.

Key points

  • Line of sight: eye → point. Elevation: point above the horizontal. Depression: point below. Both from the horizontal.
  • Towers, poles and walls are vertical: they meet the ground at 90°.
  • Angle of depression at the top = angle of elevation at the ground point (alternate angles); the angle of the triangle at the top is 90°−θ90° - \theta90°−θ.
  • The Sun's altitude is the angle at the tip of the shadow: higher Sun, shorter shadow; at 45°, shadow = height.
  • Drawing routine: ground → verticals → eye and horizontal → line of sight → angle from the horizontal and letters.
