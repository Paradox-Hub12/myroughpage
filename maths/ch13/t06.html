Topic 6 of 6

Mean, median and mode together

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Find all three measures for one grouped table, and interpret and compare them.
  • Use the empirical relation 3 Median = Mode + 2 Mean.
  • Choose the measure that suits a situation, and solve multi-part case-study questions.

Five friends earn ₹18 000, ₹20 000, ₹22 000, ₹25 000 and ₹1 65 000 a month. Their mean income is ₹50 000 — more than four of the five earn. Their median income is ₹22 000. Which number describes a "typical" friend better? This lesson puts the three measures side by side and shows when to use which.

  1. What each measure tells you

Measure It tells you It is pulled by extreme values?
Mean the fair share: total ÷ number Yes — a few very large (or small) values pull it
Median the middle: half the data below, half above Hardly — only the order matters
Mode the most common value (the busiest class) No

  1. Which measure suits?

  • Mean: when every value should count, and to compare groups — e.g. the average marks of two schools (NCERT's example).
  • Median: when a few extreme values would distort the mean — e.g. the typical wage or income in a town.
  • Mode: for the most common or most popular item — e.g. the shoe size to stock most of, the most watched TV programme.

  1. The empirical relation

For many data sets that are not too lopsided, the three measures are connected, roughly, by

3×Median=Mode+2×Mean3 \times \text{Median} = \text{Mode} + 2 \times \text{Mean}3×Median=Mode+2×MeanSwipe to see the whole equation →

NCERT calls this an empirical relationship: it comes from observation, so it holds approximately, not exactly. The sample papers use it to find one measure from the other two — for example, Mode =3 Median−2 Mean= 3\,\text{Median} - 2\,\text{Mean}=3Median−2Mean.

  1. A shift moves all three

If the same number k is added to every observation, each of the mean, median and mode increases by k (every value, every class limit and every class mark moves by k; the frequencies stay the same). For example, if each of nine distinct observations is increased by 2, the median also increases by 2.

  1. Answering case-study questions

Case questions usually ask: (i) the modal class (largest frequency), (ii) the median class (first cf greater than n2\dfrac{n}{2}2n​), and (iii) one measure by its formula. Write the table with the columns you need — f, cf, and xix_ixi​, fixif_i x_ifi​xi​ (or uiu_iui​, fiuif_i u_ifi​ui​) — once, and use it for every part.

Worked example 2

Using the empirical relation

Question. (a) For a data set, the mean is 47.3 and the median is 45.8. Use the empirical relation to find the mode. (b) For another data set, the mode is 18.6 and the median is 20.4. Find the mean.

Given: two of the three measures each time. Asked: the third.

Concept used: 3 Median=Mode+2 Mean3\,\text{Median} = \text{Mode} + 2\,\text{Mean}3Median=Mode+2Mean. Why it applies: the question asks for the empirical relation.

Worked example 3

Which measure suits?

Question. (a) The monthly incomes of five friends are ₹18 000, ₹20 000, ₹22 000, ₹25 000 and ₹1 65 000. Find the mean and the median, and say which better describes a typical friend. (b) Which measure should a shopkeeper use to decide which shoe size to stock most of? (c) Which measure would you use to compare the results of two schools?

Given: five incomes; two situations. Asked: the measures and the choices.

Concept used: mean = fair share (pulled by extremes); median = middle; mode = most common. Why it applies: one income is far larger than the others.

  1. 1

    (a) Sum =18 000+20 000+22 000+25 000+1 65 000=2 50 000= 18\,000 + 20\,000 + 22\,000 + 25\,000 + 1\,65\,000 = 2\,50\,000=18000+20000+22000+25000+165000=250000; mean =2 50 0005=50 000= \dfrac{2\,50\,000}{5} = 50\,000=5250000​=50000, that is, ₹50 000.

  2. 2

    In order, the middle (3rd) value is ₹22 000, so the median is ₹22 000.

  3. 3

    Four of the five friends earn less than the mean; the one large income pulls it up. The median describes a typical friend better.

  4. 4

    (b) The mode — the size bought most often.

  5. 5

    (c) The mean — it uses every student's marks, so two schools can be compared fairly (as in NCERT).

  6. Check: without the large income, the mean of the other four is 85 0004=21 250\dfrac{85\,000}{4} = 21\,250485000​=21250 (₹21 250) — close to the median; so it is the one extreme value that moved the mean ✔.

  7. Answer (a) Mean ₹50 000, median ₹22 000; the median is more typical. (b) Mode. (c) Mean.

    Common trap: Always choosing the mean because it "uses all the data" — that is exactly why one extreme value can distort it.

Quick check · 2 of 4MCQ1 mark

A school wants to know which size of sports shoe to order in the largest number. The best measure to use is the:

The mean size may not even be a real size (for example 6.7).
The median is the middle size, not the most common one.
The mode is the size needed most often.
The range tells how spread out the sizes are, not which is most common.
Quick check · 3 of 4MCQ1 mark

The median of seven different observations is 18. If 3 is subtracted from each observation, the new median is:

Every value moved down by 3, so the middle value did too.
This divides by 3; the values were reduced by 3, not divided.
This adds 3 instead of subtracting.
The order is unchanged, and the middle value becomes 18−3=1518 - 3 = 1518−3=15.
Quick check · 4 of 4Short answer2 marks

For the salaries in a company, the mean is ₹48 000 and the median is ₹31 000. Which better describes the salary of a typical worker? Give a reason.

Answer: The median, ₹31 000: half the workers earn less than this and half more. The mean is much larger because a few very high salaries pull it up.

A mean far above the median is a sign of a few very large values.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers say that mixing up the empirical relation, rounding in the middle of the working and choosing a measure without a reason are among the most common reasons for lost marks in this topic.

  • 3 Mean=Mode+2 Median3\,\text{Mean} = \text{Mode} + 2\,\text{Median}3Mean=Mode+2Median

    The 3 goes with the median.

    3 Median=Mode+2 Mean3\,\text{Median} = \text{Mode} + 2\,\text{Mean}3Median=Mode+2Mean; say it as "three medians".

  • Treating the empirical relation as exact

    It is an observed, approximate relation.

    Use it when a question asks for it; otherwise use the formulas.

  • Rounding a fraction early (e.g. 614≈0.43\dfrac{6}{14} \approx 0.43146​≈0.43, then ×10\times 10×10)

    20+4.3=24.320 + 4.3 = 24.320+4.3=24.3, but the true mode is 24.29.

    Keep the fraction (307\dfrac{30}{7}730​) to the end; then round to two decimal places.

  • Rounding 48.686 to 48.68

    To two decimal places, look at the third: 6 rounds up.

    48.686 ≈ 48.69.

  • Choosing the mean for a typical income

    A few very large incomes pull the mean up.

    Median for "typical"; mode for "most common"; mean to compare groups.

Key points

  • Mean = fair share (pulled by extreme values); median = middle; mode = most common.
  • Median for a typical value when there are extremes; mode for the most popular item; mean to compare groups.
  • Empirical relation (approximate): 3 Median=Mode+2 Mean3\,\text{Median} = \text{Mode} + 2\,\text{Mean}3Median=Mode+2Mean, so Mode =3 Median−2 Mean= 3\,\text{Median} - 2\,\text{Mean}=3Median−2Mean.
  • Adding k to every observation adds k to the mean, the median and the mode.
  • Keep fractions exact until the last line; then round to two decimal places and say so.
