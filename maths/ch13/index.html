Why this chapter matters

A cricket board compares batters, a school plans remedial classes, a shop decides which size to stock, a city plans buses — all from tables of numbers that are far too long to read one by one. This chapter shows you how to turn a grouped frequency table into one number that represents it: the mean (the fair share), the median (the middle) and the mode (the most common value) — and how to choose the right one. The one new idea is simple: in grouped data you do not know each value, so you work with the classes — their middles, their running totals and the busiest class.

In the board exam this chapter is part of Unit VII, Statistics and Probability (11 marks together with Chapter 14, Probability), with the same content in Mathematics Standard and Mathematics Basic. In the four sample papers for 2025-26 and 2026-27 it carries 5 to 6 marks: 1-mark MCQs (the median class, a combined mean, the empirical relation, the effect of adding a number to every value) and a 3-, 4- or 5-mark question (a mean or a mode from a table; a missing frequency from the mean or the mode; the median from a cumulative table; a case study on the three measures). The syllabus asks for the mean by the direct, assumed-mean and step-deviation methods, and the median and mode by their formulas — with data that have only one modal class.

Not in the syllabus: ogives (cumulative frequency graphs) — the 2026-27 book no longer has the section on them, although the last line of its introduction and its "Note to the reader" still mention them — and the median and mode of data with unequal class sizes. Neither is taught here. Cumulative frequency tables are in the syllabus, and you will use them for the median.

What you'll learn

  • Find the mean, median and mode of a list or a frequency table; read class limits, class sizes and class marks; make inclusive classes continuous. Take the 5-question skip-check first — score 5 out of 5 and go straight to Topic 2.
  • Find the mean of grouped data from class marks; use an assumed mean to keep the numbers small; explain why the grouped mean is an estimate. Includes an interactive grouped-data explorer.
  • Use the step-deviation method (also for unequal and decimal classes), choose a method, and find one or two missing frequencies from the mean.
  • Find the modal class and the mode, compare the mode with the mean, and find a missing frequency from the mode.
  • Make less-than and more-than cumulative tables, find the median class and the median, and find missing frequencies from the median.
  • Find and compare all three, use 3 Median = Mode + 2 Mean, choose the measure that suits, and solve case studies.
  • Use the method card, the median and mode card and the "which measure?" card, and solve mixed problems in sample-paper styles.
