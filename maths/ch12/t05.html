Topic 5 of 5

Combined solids in real life

About 60 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Solve problems with a solid inside another — the space between a toy and a circumscribing cylinder or cube, the water left when a solid is placed in a full vessel, and the number of small spheres that displace a given volume.
  • Work backwards from a volume or surface area of a combination to an unknown height or radius.
  • Solve multi-part case-study questions with cost, wastage, percentages and units.

A toy shop packs each spinning top in a tight cylindrical box. How much of the box is empty air? A child drops marbles into a glass of water until the level rises by 2 cm — how many marbles is that? A tailor has 300 m² of canvas — is it enough for a tent? Each question uses what you already know about joined and cut-out solids, plus one careful idea: compare two volumes.

  1. A solid inside another

When a solid fits exactly inside a container, the container's size comes from the solid:

Container Its size
Cylinder around a toy (cone on a hemisphere) same radius r; height = cone height + r
Cylinder around a sphere same radius r; height 2r
Cube around a sphere edge = diameter = 2r
Cylinder around a capsule same radius; height = total length

Empty space = volume of the container − volume of the solid.

  1. A solid in a vessel of water

  • If a vessel is full and a solid is placed in it so that it is completely under water, the water that flows out equals the volume of the solid, and the water left is the vessel's volume minus the solid's volume.
  • If the vessel is not full and nothing flows out, the water rises: the volume of the solids = the volume of the cylinder of water they push up, πR2×rise\pi R^2 \times \text{rise}πR2×rise.
  • How many small spheres? number =volume to be pushed up (or out)volume of one sphere= \dfrac{\text{volume to be pushed up (or out)}}{\text{volume of one sphere}}=volume of one spherevolume to be pushed up (or out)​. In such questions π cancels.

  1. Working backwards

Write the volume (or surface area) of the combination with the unknown as a letter, put in what you know, and solve. Example: a cylinder of radius 7 cm with a cone of height 6 cm on top has volume 2156 cm³ (Worked example 4).

  1. Cost, wastage and percentages

  • Cost = area × rate (or volume × rate). A wastage or stitching allowance is extra area added before the cost is found.
  • Percentage: 25% of a volume V is 25100×V\dfrac{25}{100} \times V10025​×V.

  1. Not in the syllabus

The 2026-27 syllabus does not include melting a solid and recasting it into another shape, pouring or pumping a liquid from one vessel into another, or the frustum of a cone (a bucket shape). Questions of those kinds are not in this chapter.

Worked example 1

The water left in a cylinder

Question. A solid is a cone of radius 7 cm and height 12 cm standing on a hemisphere of radius 7 cm. It is lowered, tip upwards, into a brim-full cylindrical jar of radius 7 cm and height 25 cm until it rests on the jar's base. How much water is still in the jar? (Use π=227\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}π=722​.)

Given: solid: cone r=7r = 7r=7 cm, h=12h = 12h=12 cm, on a hemisphere r=7r = 7r=7 cm; cylinder r=7r = 7r=7 cm, height 25 cm. Asked: the water left.

Concept used: water left = cylinder − solid; the solid's volume = cone + hemisphere. Why it applies: the solid is completely under water (its height 12+7=1912 + 7 = 1912+7=19 cm is less than 25 cm), so its whole volume of water flows out.

  1. Hidden faces: the base of the cone and the flat face of the hemisphere are joined — the solid's volume is the sum of the two. The cylinder is the container around them.

  2. 1

    Cylinder =227×7×7×25=154×25=3850= \dfrac{22}{7} \times 7 \times 7 \times 25 = 154 \times 25 = 3850=722​×7×7×25=154×25=3850 cm³.

  3. 2

    Cone =13×154×12=616= \dfrac{1}{3} \times 154 \times 12 = 616=31​×154×12=616 cm³.

  4. 3

    Hemisphere =23×227×7×7×7=21563= \dfrac{2}{3} \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 7 \times 7 \times 7 = \dfrac{2156}{3}=32​×722​×7×7×7=32156​ cm³.

  5. 4

    Water left =3850−616−21563=75463≈2515.33= 3850 - 616 - \dfrac{2156}{3} = \dfrac{7546}{3} \approx 2515.33=3850−616−32156​=37546​≈2515.33 cm³ (about 2.52 litres).

  6. Check: in one step, πr2(25−123−2×73)=154×493=75463\pi r^2\left(25 - \dfrac{12}{3} - \dfrac{2 \times 7}{3}\right) = 154 \times \dfrac{49}{3} = \dfrac{7546}{3}πr2(25−312​−32×7​)=154×349​=37546​ cm³ ✔.

  7. Answer 75463≈2515.33\dfrac{7546}{3} \approx 2515.3337546​≈2515.33 cm³

    Common trap: Finding only the solid's volume (40043≈1334.67\dfrac{4004}{3} \approx 1334.6734004​≈1334.67 cm³) — that is the water that flows out, not the water left.

Worked example 2

The empty space round a toy

Question. A solid toy is a cone of height 3 cm on a hemisphere of radius 4 cm (the cone has the same radius). A cylinder just encloses the toy. Find the volume of the space inside the cylinder not filled by the toy. (Use π = 3.14.)

Given: r=4r = 4r=4 cm; cone height 3 cm. Asked: the empty space.

Concept used: space = cylinder − toy; the cylinder has radius 4 cm and height 3+4=73 + 4 = 73+4=7 cm. Why it applies: the cylinder touches the toy at its widest circle, its tip and its lowest point.

  1. Hidden faces: the cone's base and the hemisphere's flat face are joined (the toy's volume = cone + hemisphere); the cylinder is the container.

  2. 1

    Cylinder =π×4×4×7=112π= \pi \times 4 \times 4 \times 7 = 112\pi=π×4×4×7=112π cm³.

  3. 2

    Toy =13π×16×3+23π×64=16π+128π3=176π3= \dfrac{1}{3}\pi \times 16 \times 3 + \dfrac{2}{3}\pi \times 64 = 16\pi + \dfrac{128\pi}{3} = \dfrac{176\pi}{3}=31​π×16×3+32​π×64=16π+3128π​=3176π​ cm³.

  4. 3

    Space =112π−176π3=160π3=160×3.143≈167.47= 112\pi - \dfrac{176\pi}{3} = \dfrac{160\pi}{3} = \dfrac{160 \times 3.14}{3} \approx 167.47=112π−3176π​=3160π​=3160×3.14​≈167.47 cm³.

  5. Check: in one step, space =πr2(h+r)−13πr2(h+2r)=πr2×2h+r3=16π×103=160π3= \pi r^2(h + r) - \dfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2(h + 2r) = \pi r^2 \times \dfrac{2h + r}{3} = 16\pi \times \dfrac{10}{3} = \dfrac{160\pi}{3}=πr2(h+r)−31​πr2(h+2r)=πr2×32h+r​=16π×310​=3160π​ ✔.

  6. Answer about 167.47 cm³

    Common trap: Taking the cylinder's height as 3 cm (the cone only) — the hemisphere adds its radius, 4 cm.

Worked example 3

How many marbles raise the water?

Question. A cylindrical beaker of radius 5 cm contains some water. How many glass spheres of radius 1 cm must be dropped in to make the water rise by 4 cm? (No water flows out.) Work in terms of π.

Given: beaker radius 5 cm; rise 4 cm; sphere radius 1 cm. Asked: the number of spheres.

Concept used: volume of the spheres = volume of water pushed up =πR2×= \pi R^2 \times=πR2× rise. Why it applies: each sphere takes the place of its own volume of water.

  1. Hidden faces: none are joined — the spheres sit under the water, and the water pushed up is a cylinder of radius 5 cm and height 4 cm.

  2. 1

    Water pushed up =π×5×5×4=100π= \pi \times 5 \times 5 \times 4 = 100\pi=π×5×5×4=100π cm³.

  3. 2

    One sphere =43×π×1×1×1=4π3= \dfrac{4}{3} \times \pi \times 1 \times 1 \times 1 = \dfrac{4\pi}{3}=34​×π×1×1×1=34π​ cm³.

  4. 3

    Number =100π÷4π3=100×34=75= 100\pi \div \dfrac{4\pi}{3} = 100 \times \dfrac{3}{4} = 75=100π÷34π​=100×43​=75.

  5. Check: 75×4π3=100π75 \times \dfrac{4\pi}{3} = 100\pi75×34π​=100π cm³ — exactly the water pushed up ✔.

  6. Answer 75 spheres

    Common trap: Using the beaker's diameter (10 cm) as its radius — that gives 300 spheres.

Worked example 4

Working backwards to a height

Question. A solid is a cylinder of radius 7 cm with a cone of the same radius and height 6 cm on top. Its volume is 2156 cm³. Find the height of the cylinder and the total height of the solid. (Use π=227\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}π=722​.)

Given: r=7r = 7r=7 cm; cone height 6 cm; total volume 2156 cm³. Asked: the cylinder's height h and the total height.

Concept used: volume = πr2h+13πr2×6\pi r^2 h + \dfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 \times 6πr2h+31​πr2×6; solve for h. Why it applies: the two volumes add.

  1. Hidden faces: the top of the cylinder and the base of the cone are joined; their volumes add.

  2. 1

    Base area =227×7×7=154= \dfrac{22}{7} \times 7 \times 7 = 154=722​×7×7=154 cm².

  3. 2

    Cone =13×154×6=308= \dfrac{1}{3} \times 154 \times 6 = 308=31​×154×6=308 cm³.

  4. 3

    Cylinder =2156−308=1848= 2156 - 308 = 1848=2156−308=1848 cm³, so 154h=1848154h = 1848154h=1848 and h=12h = 12h=12 cm.

  5. 4

    Total height =12+6=18= 12 + 6 = 18=12+6=18 cm.

  6. Check: 154×12+308=1848+308=2156154 \times 12 + 308 = 1848 + 308 = 2156154×12+308=1848+308=2156 cm³ ✔.

  7. Answer cylinder 12 cm; total height 18 cm

    Common trap: Forgetting the 13\dfrac{1}{3}31​ for the cone (924 cm³), which gives h=8h = 8h=8 cm.

Worked example 5

Canvas with a wastage allowance

Question. A tent is a cylinder of radius 9 m and height 2.5 m with a conical top of height 12 m. The canvas for the wall and the roof is bought with an extra 14.8 m² for stitching and wastage, at ₹200 per m². Find the canvas bought and its cost. (Use π = 3.14.)

Given: r=9r = 9r=9 m; wall 2.5 m; cone height 12 m; extra 14.8 m²; ₹200 per m². Asked: the canvas bought and the cost.

Concept used: canvas = CSA of cylinder + CSA of cone + allowance; cost = area × rate. Why it applies: only the wall and the roof are canvas.

  1. Hidden faces: the cone's base and the cylinder's top are joined (no canvas there); the floor is not covered.

  2. 1

    Slant height l=92+122=225=15l = \sqrt{9^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{225} = 15l=92+122​=225​=15 m.

  3. 2

    Wall =2×π×9×2.5=45π= 2 \times \pi \times 9 \times 2.5 = 45\pi=2×π×9×2.5=45π m²; roof =π×9×15=135π= \pi \times 9 \times 15 = 135\pi=π×9×15=135π m².

  4. 3

    Canvas needed =180π=180×3.14=565.2= 180\pi = 180 \times 3.14 = 565.2=180π=180×3.14=565.2 m².

  5. 4

    Canvas bought =565.2+14.8=580= 565.2 + 14.8 = 580=565.2+14.8=580 m².

  6. 5

    Cost =580×200=116 000= 580 \times 200 = 116\,000=580×200=116000, so ₹1 16 000.

  7. Check: in one step, πr(2h+l)=3.14×9×(5+15)=3.14×180=565.2\pi r(2h + l) = 3.14 \times 9 \times (5 + 15) = 3.14 \times 180 = 565.2πr(2h+l)=3.14×9×(5+15)=3.14×180=565.2 m² ✔; then 580×200=116 000580 \times 200 = 116\,000580×200=116000 ✔.

Worked example 6

Filling in 50 chocolates

Question. A chocolate is shaped like a cylinder with two hemispherical ends. Its total length is 4 cm and its diameter is 1.4 cm. A cream filling makes up 25% of its volume. Find the volume of filling in 50 chocolates, to two decimal places. (Use π=227\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}π=722​.)

Given: total length 4 cm; r=0.7r = 0.7r=0.7 cm; 25% filling; 50 chocolates. Asked: the filling in 50 chocolates.

Concept used: volume of one = cylinder (length 4−1.4=2.64 - 1.4 = 2.64−1.4=2.6 cm) + a sphere; filling = 25% of the total. Why it applies: each chocolate is a capsule shape.

  1. Hidden faces: the cylinder's ends are joined to the hemispheres' flat faces; the volumes add.

  2. 1

    Cylinder =227×0.7×0.7×2.6=1.54×2.6=4.004= \dfrac{22}{7} \times 0.7 \times 0.7 \times 2.6 = 1.54 \times 2.6 = 4.004=722​×0.7×0.7×2.6=1.54×2.6=4.004 cm³.

  3. 2

    Two hemispheres (one sphere) =43×227×0.7×0.7×0.7=4.3123≈1.4373= \dfrac{4}{3} \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 0.7 \times 0.7 \times 0.7 = \dfrac{4.312}{3} \approx 1.4373=34​×722​×0.7×0.7×0.7=34.312​≈1.4373 cm³.

  4. 3

    One chocolate =4.004+4.3123=16.3243≈5.4413= 4.004 + \dfrac{4.312}{3} = \dfrac{16.324}{3} \approx 5.4413=4.004+34.312​=316.324​≈5.4413 cm³.

  5. 4

    Fifty chocolates =50×16.3243=816.23≈272.07= 50 \times \dfrac{16.324}{3} = \dfrac{816.2}{3} \approx 272.07=50×316.324​=3816.2​≈272.07 cm³.

  6. 5

    Filling =25100×816.23=204.053≈68.02= \dfrac{25}{100} \times \dfrac{816.2}{3} = \dfrac{204.05}{3} \approx 68.02=10025​×3816.2​=3204.05​≈68.02 cm³.

  7. Check: filling in one chocolate first: 14×16.3243=4.0813\dfrac{1}{4} \times \dfrac{16.324}{3} = \dfrac{4.081}{3}41​×316.324​=34.081​, then 50×4.0813=204.053≈68.0250 \times \dfrac{4.081}{3} = \dfrac{204.05}{3} \approx 68.0250×34.081​=3204.05​≈68.02 cm³ ✔.

  8. Answer about 68.02 cm³

    Common trap: Using the whole length 4 cm for the cylinder and adding the sphere — the ends are counted twice (about 94.97 cm³ of filling).

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers say that reading the height of a part wrongly, mixing up radius and diameter, adding a volume that should be taken away and mixing units are among the most common reasons for lost marks in this chapter.

  • Circumscribing cylinder of height h (the cone only)

    The toy is h + r tall.

    Height of the cylinder = total height of the solid.

  • Cube round a sphere of radius r: edge r

    The sphere touches opposite faces.

    Edge = diameter = 2r.

  • Water left = volume of the solid

    That is the water that flows out.

    Water left = vessel − solid.

  • Number of spheres with the jar's diameter as its radius

    The answer is 4 times too big.

    Halve the diameter first.

  • Wastage allowance forgotten, or added to the cost, not the area

    It is extra area to buy.

    Area bought = area needed + allowance; then × rate.

  • Rise of water with the jar's radius in cm and the volume in m³

    One formula, two units.

    Change every length to one unit before you divide.

Key points

  • A container that just fits: same radius; height = the solid's full height (a sphere in a cube: edge = 2r).
  • Empty space or water left = container − solid; water that flows out = the solid's volume.
  • Number of spheres = volume pushed up (or out) ÷ volume of one sphere; π cancels.
  • Working backwards: write the combination's volume with the unknown, and solve.
  • Cost = (area needed + allowance) × rate; 25% of V is 25100×V\dfrac{25}{100} \times V10025​×V.
