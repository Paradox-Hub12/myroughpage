Topic 3 of 5

Theorem 10.1: the tangent is perpendicular to the radius

About 60 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • State and prove Theorem 10.1 (the tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact).
  • Find a tangent length, a radius or a distance from the centre with the right triangle OTP (Pythagoras; also sin, cos, tan of 30°, 45°, 60°), including concentric circles.
  • Prove the consequences of Theorem 10.1 (the perpendicular at the point of contact passes through the centre; tangents at the ends of a diameter are parallel; the chord of the larger of two concentric circles that touches the smaller is bisected).

Look at a bicycle wheel standing on the road. The spoke that points straight down to the road looks exactly upright — at right angles to the road. That is no accident, and it is true of every circle and every tangent: the radius to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent. It is the first theorem of this chapter, the syllabus asks you to prove it, and it turns every tangent figure into a right triangle.

  1. Quick reminder: the shortest way to a line

From a point O to a straight line, many segments can be drawn. The shortest one is the perpendicular. Why: if OP ⊥ XY and Q is any other point of XY, then OPQ is a right triangle with the right angle at P, so OQ is its hypotenuse — the longest side. So OQ>OPOQ > OPOQ>OP: the perpendicular is shorter than every other segment from O to the line. Turned round: if OP is shorter than every other segment from O to the line, then P cannot be any point other than the foot M of the perpendicular (because OM would be shorter than OP), so P = M and OP is perpendicular to the line.

  1. The theorem and its proof

Theorem 10.1. The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.

Given: a circle with centre O and a tangent XY touching it at P.

To prove: OP⊥XYOP \perp XYOP⊥XY.

Construction: take any point Q on XY other than P, and join OQ.

Statement Reason
1. Q lies outside the circle XY is a tangent, so P is its only common point with the circle. If Q were inside, XY would cut the circle in two points and be a secant.
2. OQ>OPOQ > OPOQ>OP Q is outside the circle, so OQ is longer than the radius OP.
3. OP is the shortest of all the segments from O to the points of XY Step 2 holds for every point Q of XY other than P.
4. OP⊥XYOP \perp XYOP⊥XY The shortest segment from a point to a line is the perpendicular. Hence proved.
Optional — not for the exam

NCERT backs step 4 with "Theorem A1.7" in its Appendix 1, which is not in the 2026-27 syllabus. In the exam, the reason "the shortest distance from a point to a line is the perpendicular" is enough.

  1. What follows at once

  • One tangent at each point. At P there is only one line perpendicular to OP, so a circle has one and only one tangent at each of its points.
  • The perpendicular at the point of contact passes through the centre. The line through P perpendicular to the tangent is the line OP. (NCERT calls this line the normal at P.)
  • The right triangle OTP. If a tangent from an outside point P touches the circle at T, then ∠OTP=90°\angle OTP = 90°∠OTP=90°, so
OP2=OT2+PT2OP^2 = OT^2 + PT^2OP2=OT2+PT2Swipe to see the whole equation →

where OT is the radius, PT the length of the tangent and OP — from the centre to the outside point — the hypotenuse. So PT2=OP2−OT2PT^2 = OP^2 - OT^2PT2=OP2−OT2 and OT2=OP2−PT2OT^2 = OP^2 - PT^2OT2=OP2−PT2.

  • With an angle. In the same triangle, the angle at P is ∠OPT\angle OPT∠OPT. Then
sin⁡∠OPT=OTOP,tan⁡∠OPT=OTPT\sin \angle OPT = \dfrac{OT}{OP}, \qquad \tan \angle OPT = \dfrac{OT}{PT}sin∠OPT=OPOT​,tan∠OPT=PTOT​Swipe to see the whole equation →

and the angles of 30°, 45° and 60° from Chapter 8 give exact answers.

  • Concentric circles. If a chord AB of the larger circle touches the smaller circle at M, then OM is a radius of the smaller circle and OM⊥ABOM \perp ABOM⊥AB. The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, so AM=MBAM = MBAM=MB — and AM2=(big radius)2−(small radius)2AM^2 = (\text{big radius})^2 - (\text{small radius})^2AM2=(big radius)2−(small radius)2.

Worked example 1

Proving Theorem 10.1

Question. Prove that the tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.

Given: a circle with centre O; a line AB touching it at C. To prove: OC⊥ABOC \perp ABOC⊥AB. Construction: take a point D on AB, other than C; join OD.

Concept used: a tangent has only one common point with the circle; the shortest segment from a point to a line is the perpendicular. Why it applies: every point of AB except C is outside the circle.

  1. Statement Reason
    1. D is outside the circle AB is a tangent, so C is its only common point with the circle (if D were inside, AB would be a secant)
    2. OD>OCOD > OCOD>OC OD reaches beyond the circle; OC is a radius
    3. OC is the shortest segment from O to AB Step 2 is true for every point D of AB other than C
    4. OC⊥ABOC \perp ABOC⊥AB The shortest segment from a point to a line is perpendicular to it. Hence proved.

  2. Check (with numbers): circle x2+y2=36x^2 + y^2 = 36x2+y2=36, tangent the line y=−6y = -6y=−6 at C(0,−6)C(0, -6)C(0,−6). For D(8,−6)D(8, -6)D(8,−6): OD=64+36=10>6=OCOD = \sqrt{64 + 36} = 10 > 6 = OCOD=64+36​=10>6=OC ✔, and OC is vertical while AB is horizontal ✔.

  3. Answer OC⊥ABOC \perp ABOC⊥AB — proved.

    Common trap: Writing "OC⊥ABOC \perp ABOC⊥AB because AB is a tangent". That is the statement you are asked to prove; using it as a reason proves nothing. The reason must be "shortest distance".

Worked example 2

The length of a tangent

Question. A point P is 17 cm from the centre O of a circle of radius 8 cm. Find the length of the tangent from P to the circle.

Given: OP=17OP = 17OP=17 cm; radius OT=8OT = 8OT=8 cm. Asked: PT.

Concept used: Theorem 10.1 (∠OTP=90°\angle OTP = 90°∠OTP=90°) and Pythagoras. Why it applies: O, T and P form a right triangle with the right angle at the point of contact T.

  1. 1

    Join OT. ∠OTP=90°\angle OTP = 90°∠OTP=90° (the tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact).

  2. 2

    In right triangle OTP the hypotenuse is OP.

    PT2=OP2−OT2=172−82PT^2 = OP^2 - OT^2 = 17^2 - 8^2PT2=OP2−OT2=172−82Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    PT2=289−64=225PT^2 = 289 - 64 = 225PT2=289−64=225, so PT=15PT = 15PT=15 cm.

  4. Check: 82+152=64+225=289=1728^2 + 15^2 = 64 + 225 = 289 = 17^282+152=64+225=289=172 ✔ (the 8, 15, 17 triple).

  5. Answer The tangent is 15 cm long.

    Common trap: Adding the squares, 172+82=353\sqrt{17^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{353}172+82​=353​ cm — longer than OP itself. The tangent is a shorter side; OP is the hypotenuse.

Worked example 3

The radius from a tangent

Question. The length of the tangent from a point P to a circle is 20 cm, and P is 29 cm from the centre. Find the radius.

Given: PT=20PT = 20PT=20 cm; OP=29OP = 29OP=29 cm. Asked: the radius OT.

Concept used: Theorem 10.1 and Pythagoras. Why it applies: the right angle is at T.

  1. 1

    ∠OTP=90°\angle OTP = 90°∠OTP=90° (tangent ⟂ radius at the point of contact), so OP is the hypotenuse.

    OT2=OP2−PT2=292−202OT^2 = OP^2 - PT^2 = 29^2 - 20^2OT2=OP2−PT2=292−202Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    OT2=841−400=441OT^2 = 841 - 400 = 441OT2=841−400=441, so OT=21OT = 21OT=21 cm.

  3. Check: 202+212=400+441=841=29220^2 + 21^2 = 400 + 441 = 841 = 29^2202+212=400+441=841=292 ✔.

  4. Answer The radius is 21 cm.

    Common trap: Taking the radius as 29−20=929 - 20 = 929−20=9 cm. Lengths in a right triangle are linked by their squares, not by subtracting the lengths.

Worked example 5

Concentric circles

Question. Two concentric circles have radii 10 cm and 6 cm. Find the length of the chord of the larger circle that touches the smaller circle.

Given: radii 10 cm and 6 cm, centre O; chord AB of the larger circle touches the smaller circle at M. Asked: AB.

Concept used: Theorem 10.1 (OM⊥ABOM \perp ABOM⊥AB); the perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord; Pythagoras. Why it applies: AB is a tangent to the small circle and a chord of the big one.

  1. 1

    OM is a radius of the smaller circle and AB touches it at M, so OM⊥ABOM \perp ABOM⊥AB (Theorem 10.1).

  2. 2

    AB is a chord of the larger circle and OM⊥ABOM \perp ABOM⊥AB, so M is the mid-point of AB.

  3. 3

    In right triangle OMB: MB2=OB2−OM2=102−62=64MB^2 = OB^2 - OM^2 = 10^2 - 6^2 = 64MB2=OB2−OM2=102−62=64, so MB=8MB = 8MB=8 cm.

  4. 4

    AB=2×MB=16AB = 2 \times MB = 16AB=2×MB=16 cm.

  5. Check: 62+82=36+64=100=1026^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10^262+82=36+64=100=102 ✔, and M is the middle of a 16 cm chord ✔.

  6. Answer The chord is 16 cm long.

    Common trap: Stopping at 8 cm. Pythagoras gives half the chord.

Worked example 6

Parallel tangents touch at the ends of a diameter

Question. Two parallel lines ℓ\ellℓ and mmm touch a circle with centre O at A and B. Prove that AB is a diameter.

Given: ℓ∥m\ell \parallel mℓ∥m; ℓ\ellℓ touches the circle at A, mmm at B. To prove: AB passes through O. Construction: join OA and OB.

Concept used: Theorem 10.1, twice; a line perpendicular to one of two parallel lines is perpendicular to the other. Why it applies: OA and OB are radii to points of contact.

  1. Statement Reason
    1. OA⊥ℓOA \perp \ellOA⊥ℓ Tangent ⟂ radius at the point of contact (Theorem 10.1)
    2. OA⊥mOA \perp mOA⊥m ℓ∥m\ell \parallel mℓ∥m, and OA is perpendicular to ℓ\ellℓ
    3. OB⊥mOB \perp mOB⊥m Tangent ⟂ radius at the point of contact (Theorem 10.1)
    4. OA and OB lie along one line Through O there is only one line perpendicular to mmm (steps 2 and 3)
    5. A, O, B are collinear, so AB is a diameter AB is a chord through the centre. Hence proved.

  2. Check (with numbers): circle x2+y2=16x^2 + y^2 = 16x2+y2=16 with tangents y=4y = 4y=4 at A(0,4)A(0, 4)A(0,4) and y=−4y = -4y=−4 at B(0,−4)B(0, -4)B(0,−4): the lines are parallel, and AB=8=2×4AB = 8 = 2 \times 4AB=8=2×4 passes through O ✔.

  3. Answer AB passes through O, so it is a diameter.

    Common trap: Stopping at "OA ⟂ ℓ and OB ⟂ m". You must say why the two radii make one straight line — through O there is only one perpendicular to mmm.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers say that the right angle in the wrong place, Pythagoras with the wrong hypotenuse, a concentric-circle chord not doubled and proof gaps are among the most common reasons for lost marks in this chapter.

  • The right angle marked at the outside point P, or at the centre O

    Theorem 10.1 puts the right angle at the point of contact.

    Join the radius to the point of contact and put the box there.

  • "Tangent ⟂ radius" used for a radius that does not go to the point of contact

    The theorem is only about the radius through the point of contact.

    Check that the radius ends exactly where the tangent touches.

  • PT2=OP2+OT2PT^2 = OP^2 + OT^2PT2=OP2+OT2

    OP (centre to outside point) is opposite the right angle — it is the hypotenuse.

    OP2=OT2+PT2OP^2 = OT^2 + PT^2OP2=OT2+PT2, so PT2=OP2−OT2PT^2 = OP^2 - OT^2PT2=OP2−OT2.

  • Concentric circles: answer 8 cm when the chord is 16 cm

    Pythagoras gives half the chord.

    Double MB at the end: AB=2×MBAB = 2 \times MBAB=2×MB.

  • Proof of Theorem 10.1: "OP ⟂ XY because XY is a tangent"

    That is the statement to be proved.

    Reason: Q outside → OQ>OPOQ > OPOQ>OP → OP is the shortest → perpendicular.

Key points

  • Theorem 10.1: the tangent at a point is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact. Proof: every other point of the tangent is outside, so OP is the shortest segment from O to the tangent.
  • At each point of a circle there is exactly one tangent; the perpendicular to a tangent at its point of contact passes through the centre.
  • Right triangle OTP: right angle at T; hypotenuse OP; PT2=OP2−OT2PT^2 = OP^2 - OT^2PT2=OP2−OT2; with an angle at P use sin⁡∠OPT=OTOP\sin \angle OPT = \dfrac{OT}{OP}sin∠OPT=OPOT​.
  • Tangents at the two ends of a diameter are parallel; two parallel tangents touch at the ends of a diameter.
  • Concentric circles: the chord of the larger circle touching the smaller one is bisected; half-chord2=R2−r2\text{half-chord}^2 = R^2 - r^2half-chord2=R2−r2, then double it.
