Topic 2 of 5

Lines and circles: the tangent

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Name the three positions of a line and a circle (non-intersecting line, secant, tangent) and the point of contact; describe a tangent as the limiting position of a secant.
  • State how many tangents pass through a point inside, on and outside a circle, and how many tangents can be parallel to a given line.
  • Define the length of a tangent from an external point and recognise tangents in real situations.

A bicycle rolls along a straight road. At every moment the wheel touches the road at just one point — the road is a tangent to the wheel. A rope runs over a pulley at the top of a well; where it leaves the pulley on each side, the rope is a tangent to the pulley's circle. In this topic you learn the words for these pictures, and three counting facts that are asked as 1-mark questions almost every year.

  1. A line and a circle: three possibilities

Draw a circle and a straight line in the same plane. Exactly one of these happens:

Position Common points Name
The line misses the circle none non-intersecting line
The line cuts the circle two secant
The line just touches the circle exactly one tangent

There is no fourth possibility: a straight line cannot meet a circle in three or more points.

The single common point of a tangent and the circle is called the point of contact, and we say the tangent touches the circle at that point.

A useful way to picture the three cases: compare the distance of the centre from the line with the radius. If the distance is more than the radius, the line misses; equal to the radius, it touches; less than the radius, it cuts. (In Topic 3 you will prove the key fact behind the middle case: the radius to the point of contact meets the tangent at a right angle, so the point of contact is the point of the line nearest to the centre.)

  1. A tangent is a secant whose two points have come together

Take a secant through a point P of a circle; it cuts the circle again at Q. Turn the line slowly about P so that Q slides along the circle towards P. The chord PQ gets shorter and shorter. In the one position where Q reaches P, the line meets the circle only at P — it is the tangent at P. Turn it further and it becomes a secant again, on the other side.

So a tangent is the special case of a secant when the two ends of its chord coincide. This also suggests that at each point of a circle there is exactly one tangent (Topic 3 proves it) — and since a circle has infinitely many points, a circle has infinitely many tangents.

  1. Tangents parallel to a given line

Draw a secant and slide it parallel to itself. The chord it cuts gets shorter, until on one side the line just touches the circle; slide the other way and the same happens on the opposite side. So at most two tangents can be parallel to a given line — one on each side of the circle. (In Topic 3 you will prove that these two points of contact are the ends of a diameter.)

  1. How many tangents through a point?

Where the point P is Tangents through P
inside the circle none — every line through P cuts the circle in two points
on the circle exactly one
outside the circle exactly two

  1. The length of a tangent

When P is outside the circle and a tangent from P touches the circle at T, the segment PT is called the length of the tangent from P. There are two tangents from P, so there are two such lengths, PT₁ and PT₂. In Topic 4 you will prove that they are always equal.

Optional — not for the exam

The word "tangent" comes from the Latin tangere, "to touch"; NCERT notes that the Danish mathematician Thomas Fineke introduced it in 1583. (Drawing tangents with ruler and compasses was in an older version of the book and is not in the 2026-27 syllabus.)

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Which kind of line?

Question. A circle has centre O and radius 6 cm. Three lines are drawn: line ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ is 4 cm from O, line ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​ is 6 cm from O and line ℓ3\ell_3ℓ3​ is 9 cm from O. Name each line, and say how many points it shares with the circle.

Given: radius 6 cm; distances 4, 6 and 9 cm. Asked: the name of each line and its number of common points.

Concept used: compare the distance of the centre from the line with the radius. Why it applies: the point of the line nearest to O is inside, on or outside the circle.

  1. 1

    ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​: 4<64 < 64<6, so the nearest point of the line is inside the circle; the line must cross the circle twice → secant, 2 points.

  2. 2

    ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​: 6=66 = 66=6, so the nearest point is on the circle and every other point of the line is farther away → tangent, 1 point.

  3. 3

    ℓ3\ell_3ℓ3​: 9>69 > 69>6, so even the nearest point is outside → non-intersecting line, 0 points.

  4. Check: draw a circle of radius 6 squares on squared paper and three horizontal lines 4, 6 and 9 squares below the centre — you see 2, 1 and 0 meeting points ✔.

  5. Answer ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ secant (2 points); ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​ tangent (1 point); ℓ3\ell_3ℓ3​ non-intersecting (0 points)

    Common trap: Calling ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ a tangent "because it is close to the circle". A line that comes nearer to the centre than the radius goes into the circle and out again.

Worked example 2

How many tangents through each point?

Question. A circle has centre O and radius 7 cm. Points A, B and C are such that OA=4OA = 4OA=4 cm, OB=7OB = 7OB=7 cm and OC=11OC = 11OC=11 cm. How many tangents to the circle can be drawn through each point?

Given: radius 7 cm; OA=4OA = 4OA=4, OB=7OB = 7OB=7, OC=11OC = 11OC=11 (cm). Asked: the number of tangents through A, B and C.

Concept used: inside → none; on → one; outside → two. Why it applies: comparing each distance with the radius tells us where the point is.

  1. 1

    OA=4<7OA = 4 < 7OA=4<7: A is inside the circle → no tangent.

  2. 2

    OB=7OB = 7OB=7: B is on the circle → exactly one tangent.

  3. 3

    OC=11>7OC = 11 > 7OC=11>7: C is outside the circle → exactly two tangents.

  4. Check: the counts go 0, 1, 2 as the point moves outwards ✔ — as in the explorer.

  5. Answer A: 0 · B: 1 · C: 2

    Common trap: Saying "one tangent from C". From a point outside there are always two, one on each side of the line OC.

Worked example 3

Tangents parallel to a line

Question. A circle has radius 4.5 cm, and ℓ\ellℓ is a line in its plane. (a) How many tangents to the circle are parallel to ℓ\ellℓ? (b) How far apart are they?

Given: radius 4.5 cm; a line ℓ\ellℓ. Asked: the number of tangents parallel to ℓ\ellℓ and the distance between them.

Concept used: sliding a line parallel to itself, it touches the circle once on each side; the two points of contact are the ends of a diameter (proved in Topic 3). Why it applies: every tangent parallel to ℓ\ellℓ is one of these two positions.

  1. 1

    (a) Slide a line parallel to ℓ\ellℓ across the circle: it touches once on one side and once on the other → two tangents.

  2. 2

    (b) They touch at the two ends of the diameter perpendicular to ℓ\ellℓ, and both tangents are perpendicular to that diameter. So the distance between them is the diameter.

  3. 3

    Distance =2×4.5=9= 2 \times 4.5 = 9=2×4.5=9 cm.

  4. Check: each tangent is one radius (4.5 cm) from the centre, on opposite sides: 4.5+4.5=94.5 + 4.5 = 94.5+4.5=9 cm ✔.

  5. Answer (a) 2 (b) 9 cm

    Common trap: "Infinitely many": a circle has infinitely many tangents, but only two of them point in any one direction.

Worked example 4

Naming the parts of a tangent figure

Question. In the figure, a circle has centre O. From an outside point P, two lines PA and PB touch the circle at A and B. A third line from P cuts the circle at C and D. Name (a) the tangents, (b) the points of contact, (c) the lengths of the tangents from P, (d) a secant, (e) a chord.

  1. (Figure, described: a circle with centre O; P is to the right of the circle; PA touches the circle at the top, PB at the bottom; the line PDC passes between them and cuts the circle at D (nearer P) and C.)

    Given: the figure. Asked: five names.

    Concept used: the definitions of this topic. Why it applies: each line meets the circle in a known number of points.

  2. 1

    (a) PA and PB each meet the circle at one point → tangents.

  3. 2

    (b) The single common points, A and B, are the points of contact.

  4. 3

    (c) The segments PA and PB (from P to the points of contact) are the lengths of the tangents.

  5. 4

    (d) The line PC meets the circle at two points, C and D → secant.

  6. 5

    (e) CD joins two points of the circle → chord (so are AB, AC and so on).

  7. Check: every tangent has exactly one point of contact, and the secant has two common points ✔.

  8. Answer (a) PA, PB (b) A, B (c) PA, PB (d) PC (e) CD

    Common trap: Calling the segment PO the length of the tangent. O is the centre, not a point of the circle; the length of a tangent ends at the point of contact.

Quick check · 1 of 4MCQ1 mark

The number of tangents that can be drawn to a circle from a point inside it is:

Every line through a point inside the circle cuts the circle in two points, so none is a tangent.
One tangent passes through a point on the circle.
Two tangents pass through a point outside the circle.
The circle has infinitely many tangents, but none passes through an inside point.
Quick check · 2 of 4MCQ1 mark

The greatest number of tangents to a circle that can be parallel to a given line ℓ\ellℓ is:

There is a second one on the other side.
One on each side of the circle, at the ends of a diameter.
A line parallel to ℓ\ellℓ can touch the circle only on one side or the other — two positions in all.
Infinitely many tangents exist altogether, but they point in different directions.
Quick check · 3 of 4MCQ1 mark

A circle has radius 8 cm. A line is 8 cm from its centre. The line is:

A secant comes nearer to the centre than the radius.
The distance equals the radius, so the nearest point of the line is on the circle — one common point.
A non-intersecting line is farther from the centre than the radius.
A diameter passes through the centre (distance 0) and is a chord, not a whole line.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers say that counting tangents wrongly is among the most common reasons for lost marks in this chapter.

  • "One tangent can be drawn from a point inside a circle"

    Every line through an inside point crosses the circle twice.

    Inside 0 · on 1 · outside 2.

  • "Only one tangent from a point outside"

    There are two, one on each side of the line to the centre.

    Draw both; label both points of contact.

  • "A circle can have only two tangents"

    There is one tangent at each point of the circle.

    Infinitely many tangents; at most two parallel to a given line.

  • "A tangent meets the circle in two points"

    That is a secant.

    A tangent has exactly one common point, the point of contact.

  • The length of a tangent measured to the centre

    The tangent touches the circle at the point of contact, not at O.

    Length of the tangent = distance from the external point to the point of contact.

Key points

  • Non-intersecting line: 0 common points · secant: 2 · tangent: exactly 1, the point of contact.
  • A tangent is the limiting position of a secant when its two common points come together.
  • One tangent at each point → infinitely many tangents; at most two parallel to any given line.
  • Tangents through a point: inside 0, on 1, outside 2.
  • The length of the tangent from P is PT, where T is the point of contact.
