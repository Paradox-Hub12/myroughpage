Topic 3 of 6

Solving by factorisation

About 50 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain what a root is and check a root by substituting it into the equation.
  • Use the zero-product rule to solve a quadratic equation by factorisation, including fraction and surd coefficients.
  • Recognise repeated (equal) roots.

Two numbers multiply to give 0. What can you say about them? Think for a second. However big or small the other number is, one of them must be 0. That single fact turns a hard quadratic equation into two easy linear ones.

Quick reminder (Chapter 2)

The zeroes of the polynomial p(x)=x2−4x−21p(x) = x^2 - 4x - 21p(x)=x2−4x−21 are the values of xxx that make p(x)=0p(x) = 0p(x)=0. They are the points where the graph y=p(x)y = p(x)y=p(x) cuts the x-axis.

Roots

A number is a root (or solution) of a quadratic equation if it makes the equation true when you substitute it for xxx. The roots of ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 are exactly the zeroes of the polynomial ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c. A quadratic equation has at most two roots.

To check a root, substitute it. Is 7 a root of x2−4x−21=0x^2 - 4x - 21 = 0x2−4x−21=0? 72−4(7)−21=49−28−21=07^2 - 4(7) - 21 = 49 - 28 - 21 = 072−4(7)−21=49−28−21=0 ✔. Yes.

The zero-product rule

If P×Q=0, then P=0 or Q=0.\text{If } P \times Q = 0, \text{ then } P = 0 \text{ or } Q = 0.If P×Q=0, then P=0 or Q=0.Swipe to see the whole equation →

So if (x−7)(x+3)=0(x - 7)(x + 3) = 0(x−7)(x+3)=0, either x−7=0x - 7 = 0x−7=0 (so x=7x = 7x=7) or x+3=0x + 3 = 0x+3=0 (so x=−3x = -3x=−3).

The rule works only when the right side is 0. From (x−1)(x+4)=6(x - 1)(x + 4) = 6(x−1)(x+4)=6 you can say nothing about each bracket — two numbers can multiply to 6 in endless ways (2 × 3, 1 × 6, 0.5 × 12 …). Expand and bring the 6 across first.

The method

  1. Write the equation in standard form (right side 0).
  2. Factorise the left side (split the middle term — Topic 2).
  3. Set each factor equal to 0.
  4. Solve each linear equation.
  5. Check each root by substituting it into the original equation.

Never divide by xxx

From 5x2=3x5x^2 = 3x5x2=3x it is tempting to divide both sides by xxx and get x=35x = \dfrac{3}{5}x=53​. But that throws away the root x=0x = 0x=0 (dividing by xxx is not allowed when xxx might be 0). Instead, bring everything to one side and take xxx out as a common factor: x(5x−3)=0x(5x - 3) = 0x(5x−3)=0, so x=0x = 0x=0 or x=35x = \dfrac{3}{5}x=53​.

Repeated roots

Sometimes both factors are the same, as in (3x−4)2=0(3x - 4)^2 = 0(3x−4)2=0. Both brackets give x=43x = \dfrac{4}{3}x=34​. We say the equation has two equal roots (or a repeated root): 43,43\dfrac{4}{3}, \dfrac{4}{3}34​,34​. You will see in Topic 5 how to predict this without factorising.

A repeated root

Question. Solve 9x2−24x+16=09x^2 - 24x + 16 = 09x2−24x+16=0.

  2. 2

  • Splitting with the wrong signs (sum +5+5+5 instead of −5-5−5)

    The roots come out with the wrong signs.

    Check product =ac= ac=ac and sum =b= b=b; expand your factors to make sure.

Key points

  • A root makes the equation true; check it by substituting. A quadratic has at most two roots.
  • Zero-product rule: if PQ=0PQ = 0PQ=0 then P=0P = 0P=0 or Q=0Q = 0Q=0 — only when the right side is 0.
  • Method: standard form → factorise → each factor = 0 → solve → check.
  • Never divide by xxx; take it out as a common factor so that x=0x = 0x=0 is kept.
  • A squared factor such as (3x−4)2=0(3x - 4)^2 = 0(3x−4)2=0 gives two equal roots.
  • Clear fractions by multiplying every term by the LCM; surd coefficients split the same way (use 3×3=3\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{3} = 33​×3​=3).
