Algebra toolkit: substitution, splitting the middle term and identities

About 30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Substitute values into a polynomial, including negative numbers and fractions.
  • Factorise a quadratic by splitting the middle term, including surd and fraction coefficients.
  • Use the identities (a + b)², (a − b)² and a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b).
Why this lesson exists

Every question in this chapter uses three skills from Classes 8 and 9: putting a number into an expression, factorising a quadratic, and the square identities. When a polynomial question goes wrong, the slip is nearly always in one of these — a lost minus sign, or a middle term split the wrong way. If you already have these skills, prove it below and go straight to Topic 2.

A shopkeeper says, "Think of a number, square it, take away three times the number, and add 2." If you choose 5 the answer is 12; if you choose 1 or 2 the answer is 0. Why do exactly those two numbers give 0? Because x2−3x+2=(x−1)(x−2)x^2 - 3x + 2 = (x - 1)(x - 2)x2−3x+2=(x−1)(x−2), and a product is 0 only when one of its factors is 0. Factorising is how this whole chapter finds such numbers. First, the tools.

  1. Substituting a value

To find the value of an expression at x=kx = kx=k, replace every x by k in brackets, then work out powers first, then multiplication, then addition and subtraction.

  • Brackets matter for negative numbers: (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9, but −32-3^2−32 means −(32)=−9-(3^2) = -9−(32)=−9.
  • Two minus signs multiply to a plus: −5×(−3)=+15-5 \times (-3) = +15−5×(−3)=+15.
  • For fractions, square the top and the bottom: (23)2=49\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^2 = \dfrac{4}{9}(32​)2=94​.

  1. Splitting the middle term

To factorise ax2+bx+cax^2 + bx + cax2+bx+c:

  1. Multiply a×ca \times ca×c.
  2. Find two numbers whose product is acacac and whose sum is bbb.
  3. Split bxbxbx into those two terms.
  4. Group in pairs and take out the common factor of each pair.
  5. Take out the common bracket.

The signs tell you where to look. If acacac is positive, the two numbers have the same sign (the sign of b). If acacac is negative, they have opposite signs, and the larger one (ignoring sign) takes the sign of b.

Fractions in the coefficients. Take out a fraction first so that what is left has whole-number coefficients: x2−16x−13=16(6x2−x−2)x^2 - \dfrac{1}{6}x - \dfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{1}{6}(6x^2 - x - 2)x2−61​x−31​=61​(6x2−x−2). Then split as usual.

Surds in the coefficients. The same method works. You need two numbers whose product is acacac and whose sum is b, and these may contain a root. Remember that 2×2=2\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{2} = 22​×2​=2, and 2×32=3×2=6\sqrt{2} \times 3\sqrt{2} = 3 \times 2 = 62​×32​=3×2=6.

  1. Three identities

(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2(a−b)2=a2−2ab+b2a2−b2=(a−b)(a+b)(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \qquad (a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2 \qquad a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2(a−b)2=a2−2ab+b2a2−b2=(a−b)(a+b)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  • The middle term 2ab2ab2ab is the one students forget: (x+3)2(x + 3)^2(x+3)2 is x2+6x+9x^2 + 6x + 9x2+6x+9, not x2+9x^2 + 9x2+9.
  • Difference of two squares also works when b is a root: x2−5=(x−5)(x+5)x^2 - 5 = (x - \sqrt{5})(x + \sqrt{5})x2−5=(x−5​)(x+5​), because (5)2=5(\sqrt{5})^2 = 5(5​)2=5.
  • Rearranging the first identity gives a result you will use in Topic 5:
a2+b2=(a+b)2−2aba^2 + b^2 = (a + b)^2 - 2aba2+b2=(a+b)2−2abSwipe to see the whole equation →

So if you know only the sum and the product of two numbers, you can still find the sum of their squares.

Key points

  • Substitute in brackets; powers first; minus times minus is plus.
