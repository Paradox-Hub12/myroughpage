Topic 3 of 5

The nth term

About 60 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Derive the nth term formula a_n = a + (n − 1)d.
  • Find a term, a, d or n; decide whether a given number is a term of an AP.
  • Find an AP from two of its terms; find the middle term(s) and the nth term from the end; count the multiples of a number in a range.

The front row of a cinema hall has 14 seats; the row behind it has 17, the one behind that 20 — every row has 3 more seats than the row in front. How many seats are in row 40? Adding 3 thirty-nine times would work, but it is slow and easy to get wrong. One short formula answers it in a single line — and the same formula tells you whether some row could have exactly 100 seats.

Deriving the formula for the nth term

Let the AP have first term a and common difference d. Build the terms one at a time:

Statement Reason
a1=aa_1 = aa1​=a The first term is called a.
a2=a1+d=a+da_2 = a_1 + d = a + da2​=a1​+d=a+d In an AP, each term is the term before it plus d.
a3=a2+d=a+2da_3 = a_2 + d = a + 2da3​=a2​+d=a+2d Add d again.
a4=a3+d=a+3da_4 = a_3 + d = a + 3da4​=a3​+d=a+3d Add d again.
The number of d's is always one less than the term number. Going from term 1 to term n takes n−1n - 1n−1 steps, and each step adds one d.
an=a+(n−1)da_n = a + (n - 1)dan​=a+(n−1)d So the nth term is a plus (n−1)(n - 1)(n−1) lots of d.

ana_nan​ is also called the general term. For a finite AP with n terms, the last term is l=an=a+(n−1)dl = a_n = a + (n - 1)dl=an​=a+(n−1)d.

Why n−1n - 1n−1 and not n? Think of a staircase: you stand on step 1. To reach step 5 you climb 4 steps, not 5. The first term already "uses up" position 1 without adding any d.

Back to the cinema: row 40 has 14+39×3=13114 + 39 \times 3 = 13114+39×3=131 seats. No row has exactly 100, because 100=14+(n−1)×3100 = 14 + (n - 1) \times 3100=14+(n−1)×3 gives n−1=863n - 1 = \dfrac{86}{3}n−1=386​, which is not a whole number.

One formula, four jobs

an=a+(n−1)da_n = a + (n - 1)dan​=a+(n−1)d links four quantities: a, d, n and ana_nan​. If you know any three, you can find the fourth.

  • Is a number a term? Put ana_nan​ equal to that number and solve for n. The number is a term only if n comes out as a positive whole number.
  • Two terms given? For the pth and qth terms, aq−ap=(q−p)da_q - a_p = (q - p)daq​−ap​=(q−p)d: subtract to find d, then find a.

Counting from the end, and the middle term

  • Number of terms in a finite AP: put l=a+(n−1)dl = a + (n - 1)dl=a+(n−1)d and solve for n.
  • kth term from the end: it is the (n−k+1)(n - k + 1)(n−k+1)th term from the start. Or write the AP backwards: its first term is l and its common difference is −d-d−d, so the kth term from the end is l−(k−1)dl - (k - 1)dl−(k−1)d.
  • Middle term: if n is odd, there is one middle term, the (n+12)\left(\dfrac{n + 1}{2}\right)(2n+1​)th. If n is even, there are two middle terms, the (n2)\left(\dfrac{n}{2}\right)(2n​)th and the (n2+1)\left(\dfrac{n}{2} + 1\right)(2n​+1)th.

Counting multiples in a range

The multiples of a number m form an AP with d=md = md=m. Find the first and last multiple inside the range, then use the formula to find n.

Quick reminder (Chapter 1). A number that is a multiple of both 4 and 6 is a multiple of their LCM, 12 — not of their product, 24. (LCM = product only when the two numbers have no common factor other than 1.)

Finding a term

Which term?

Question. Which term of the AP 7,16,25,…7, 16, 25, \ldots7,16,25,… is 214?

Concept used: an=a+(n−1)da_n = a + (n - 1)dan​=a+(n−1)d, solved for n. Why it applies: we know the term's value but not its position.

  1. 1

    Substitute.

Is it a term?

Worked example 4

Finding the AP from two terms

Worked example 5

Counting from the end, and the middle term

Question. For the AP 6,11,16,…,1566, 11, 16, \ldots, 1566,11,16,…,156, find (a) the number of terms (b) the 8th term from the end (c) the middle term. (d) If one more term, 161, is added at the end, what are the middle terms now?

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers see writing a+nda + nda+nd for the nth term as one of the four commonest reasons for lost marks in this chapter.

  • a25=a+25da_{25} = a + 25da25​=a+25d

    From the 1st to the 25th term there are 24 steps, not 25.

    a25=a+24da_{25} = a + 24da25​=a+24d — always (n−1)(n - 1)(n−1).

  • Stopping at "n−1=23n - 1 = 23n−1=23" and answering "23rd term"

    n−1n - 1n−1 counts steps, not the position.

    Add 1: n=24n = 24n=24.

  • Rounding a non-whole n ("about the 36th term")

    Term numbers are whole.

    n not whole → the number is not a term.

  • kth from the end =a+(k−1)d= a + (k - 1)d=a+(k−1)d or l−kdl - kdl−kd

    The first counts from the start; the second takes one step too many.

    l−(k−1)dl - (k - 1)dl−(k−1)d, or the (n−k+1)(n - k + 1)(n−k+1)th term from the start.

  • aq−ap=q×da_q - a_p = q \times daq​−ap​=q×d

    The gap between the pth and qth terms is q−pq - pq−p steps.

    a15−a6=9da_{15} - a_6 = 9da15​−a6​=9d.

  • Multiples of 4 and 6 counted as multiples of 24

    12, 36, 60, … are missed.

    Use the LCM, 12.

Key points

  • an=a+(n−1)da_n = a + (n - 1)dan​=a+(n−1)d: from the 1st term to the nth there are n−1n - 1n−1 steps of d.
  • Know any three of a, d, n, ana_nan​ → find the fourth.
  • A number is a term only if n comes out a positive whole number.
  • aq−ap=(q−p)da_q - a_p = (q - p)daq​−ap​=(q−p)d; kth from the end =l−(k−1)d= l - (k - 1)d=l−(k−1)d.
  • Middle term: n odd → the (n+12)\left(\dfrac{n + 1}{2}\right)(2n+1​)th; n even → the (n2)\left(\dfrac{n}{2}\right)(2n​)th and (n2+1)\left(\dfrac{n}{2} + 1\right)(2n​+1)th.
  • Multiples of both p and q are the multiples of LCM(p, q).
