Chapter 1 · Unit I

Real Numbers

6 topicsAbout 7–8 hoursBoard exam
Why this chapter matters

Every time you shop online or send a message, your data is locked with codes built from huge prime numbers. Multiplying two huge primes is easy, but splitting the answer back into them is extremely hard. Behind this is one simple fact you will meet in this chapter: every whole number bigger than 1 is built from primes in exactly one way. The same fact tells you when two buses will next leave a depot together, how big a floor tile can be — and lets you prove that a number like 2\sqrt{2}2​ can never be written as a fraction.

You will learn the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic and how to write any number as a product of primes, how to find the HCF and LCM of two or three numbers from their prime factors, how to decide whether a word problem needs the HCF or the LCM, and how to write a proof by contradiction that 2\sqrt{2}2​, 3\sqrt{3}3​, 5\sqrt{5}5​ and numbers like 3+253 + 2\sqrt{5}3+25​ are irrational. In the board exam this chapter is the whole of Unit I, Number Systems — 6 marks in both Mathematics Standard and Mathematics Basic. In the 2026-27 sample papers it supplies a 1-mark MCQ, a 2-mark question with an internal choice (HCF/LCM, or "never ends in 0") and a 3-mark irrationality proof.

What you'll learn

  • Spot primes and composites (1 is neither), use the divisibility tests for 2, 3, 5, 9 and 11, write powers, and tell rational from irrational numbers. Take the 5-question skip-check first — score 5 out of 5 and go straight to Topic 2.
  • State the theorem; write any number as a product of primes by a factor tree or repeated division; explain why the factorisation is unique; decide whether a number like 6n6^n6n can end in 0; show that a number is composite.
  • Find the HCF and LCM of two or three numbers with a prime-power table; use HCF × LCM = product for two numbers (and know it is not true in general for three); find missing values and unknown powers.
  • Decide whether a problem needs the HCF or the LCM; solve problems on meeting again, bells or lights together, the largest tile or group, and numbers with given remainders; check that the answer makes sense.
  • Place rational and irrational numbers on the number line; use the fact "if a prime p divides a2a^2a2, then p divides a"; prove 2\sqrt{2}2​, 3\sqrt{3}3​, 5\sqrt{5}5​ (and p\sqrt{p}p​ for any prime p) irrational by contradiction. (Includes a short, not-for-exam note on Baudhāyana's value of 2\sqrt{2}2​.)
  • Prove numbers like 3+253 + 2\sqrt{5}3+25​, 757\sqrt{5}75​, 12\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}2​1​ and 2−5\sqrt{2} - \sqrt{5}2​−5​ irrational; use the rules for mixing rational and irrational numbers; show by example that two irrational numbers can add up to a rational one.
  • Recognise each question type quickly with the "Which method?" card and the proof template, and solve mixed problems.
