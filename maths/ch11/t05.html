Topic 5 of 5

Shaded regions built from sectors and segments

About 60 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Find the total area of sectors at the corners of a triangle or quadrilateral (angles adding to 180° or 360°) and the area left over.
  • Find the area and boundary of a ring and of a ring-sector (as in a fan).
  • Find regions between circles, squares and triangles (inscribed and circumscribed circles, quadrants in a square, lens shapes made of two segments).

Look at the Olympic rings: where two rings cross, there is a small lens-shaped overlap. Look at a paper hand-fan: the coloured sheet is a slice of a ring. Neither shape has a formula of its own — but each one is built from pieces you already know: sectors, segments, triangles, squares. The skill in this topic is to see the pieces, write the region as "this plus that" or "this minus that", and only then put in numbers.

Not in the syllabus: NCERT's former section on long "areas of combinations of plane figures" (removed from the book). The regions below are the kinds the 2025-26 and 2026-27 sample papers ask, built from sectors and segments.

  1. The region routine

  1. Shade the region you want.
  2. Name the pieces: circle, sector, segment, ring, triangle, square, rectangle.
  3. Write a word equation, e.g. "shaded = square − 4 quadrants" or "lens = 2 segments".
  4. Calculate each piece with the given π, then add or subtract. Round only at the end.

  1. Lens shapes: two segments

Where two circles overlap, the common chord cuts a segment off each circle; the overlap (a lens) is the two segments together. Two equal circles of radius r whose centres are r apart (each passes through the other's centre) make an equilateral triangle with each end of the common chord, so the chord subtends 2×60°=120°2 \times 60° = 120°2×60°=120° at each centre: the lens is two 120° segments. Two quadrants of a square drawn from opposite corners overlap in a "leaf" of two 90° segments.

Worked example 1

Three animals at the corners of a field

Question. A triangular field has sides 30 m, 40 m and 50 m. A horse, a cow and a goat are tied at its three corners, each with a rope 7 m long. Find (a) the total area they can graze and (b) the area of the field that none of them can graze. (Use π=227\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}π=722​.)

Given: sides 30, 40, 50 m; three ropes of 7 m. Asked: the grazed and ungrazed areas.

Concept used: the three grazed regions are sectors of radius 7 m whose angles are the triangle's angles, which add up to 180°; 302+402=50230^2 + 40^2 = 50^2302+402=502, so the field is right-angled. Why it applies: each rope is shorter than half of every side, so the sectors do not overlap.

  1. 1

    Total grazed area =A+B+C360×πr2=180360×227×7×7= \dfrac{A + B + C}{360} \times \pi r^2 = \dfrac{180}{360} \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 7 \times 7=360A+B+C​×πr2=360180​×722​×7×7.

  2. 2

    =12×154=77= \dfrac{1}{2} \times 154 = 77=21​×154=77 m².

  3. 3

    302+402=900+1600=2500=50230^2 + 40^2 = 900 + 1600 = 2500 = 50^2302+402=900+1600=2500=502, so the angle between the 30 m and 40 m sides is 90°.

  4. 4

    Area of the field =12×30×40=600= \dfrac{1}{2} \times 30 \times 40 = 600=21​×30×40=600 m².

  5. 5

    (b) Not grazed =600−77=523= 600 - 77 = 523=600−77=523 m².

  6. Check: the field's shortest height is 2×60050=24\dfrac{2 \times 600}{50} = 24502×600​=24 m, more than 7 m, so each sector fits inside the field ✔.

  7. Answer (a) 77 m² (b) 523 m²

    Common trap: Taking each grazed region as a quadrant (3 × 38.5 = 115.5 m²). Only a 90° corner gives a quadrant; the three angles together make 180°.

Worked example 2

Arcs at the four corners

Question. With centres at the four corners of a quadrilateral PQRS, arcs of radius 3.5 cm are drawn inside it (the arcs do not meet each other or the opposite sides). Find the total area of the four sectors. (Use π=227\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}π=722​.)

Given: four sectors of radius 3.5 cm at the corners of a quadrilateral. Asked: their total area.

Concept used: the angles of a quadrilateral add up to 360°. Why it applies: each sector's angle is the angle of its corner.

  1. 1

    Total =P+Q+R+S360×πr2=360360×227×3.5×3.5= \dfrac{P + Q + R + S}{360} \times \pi r^2 = \dfrac{360}{360} \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 3.5 \times 3.5=360P+Q+R+S​×πr2=360360​×722​×3.5×3.5.

  2. 2

    =227×12.25=38.5= \dfrac{22}{7} \times 12.25 = 38.5=722​×12.25=38.5 cm².

  3. Check: the same as one whole circle of radius 3.5 cm — four corner pieces fit together round a point ✔.

  4. Answer 38.5 cm²

    Common trap: Thinking the angles are needed. Only their sum, 360°, is used.

Worked example 3

The sheet of a fan (a ring-sector)

Question. The paper sheet of a hand-fan is the part of a 120° sector between radii 7 cm and 14 cm. Find (a) the area of the sheet and (b) the length of its whole boundary. (Use π=227\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}π=722​.)

Given: θ=120°\theta = 120°θ=120°; R=14R = 14R=14 cm; r=7r = 7r=7 cm. Asked: the area and the boundary.

Concept used: ring-sector = big sector − small sector; boundary = two arcs + two straight edges. Why it applies: the sheet lies between two arcs with the same centre and the same angle.

  1. 1

    (a) Area =120360×227×(142−72)=13×227×147=13×462=154= \dfrac{120}{360} \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times (14^2 - 7^2) = \dfrac{1}{3} \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 147 = \dfrac{1}{3} \times 462 = 154=360120​×722​×(142−72)=31​×722​×147=31​×462=154 cm².

  2. 2

    (b) Outer arc =13×2×227×14=883= \dfrac{1}{3} \times 2 \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 14 = \dfrac{88}{3}=31​×2×722​×14=388​ cm; inner arc =13×2×227×7=443= \dfrac{1}{3} \times 2 \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 7 = \dfrac{44}{3}=31​×2×722​×7=344​ cm.

  3. 3

    Arcs together: 883+443=1323=44\dfrac{88}{3} + \dfrac{44}{3} = \dfrac{132}{3} = 44388​+344​=3132​=44 cm.

  4. 4

    Straight edges: 2×(14−7)=142 \times (14 - 7) = 142×(14−7)=14 cm.

  5. 5

    Boundary =44+14=58= 44 + 14 = 58=44+14=58 cm.

  6. Check: big sector 13×616=6163\dfrac{1}{3} \times 616 = \dfrac{616}{3}31​×616=3616​ and small sector 13×154=1543\dfrac{1}{3} \times 154 = \dfrac{154}{3}31​×154=3154​; difference 4623=154\dfrac{462}{3} = 1543462​=154 ✔.

  7. Answer (a) 154 cm² (b) 58 cm

    Common trap: For the boundary, adding only the outer arc and the two edges. The inner arc is part of the boundary too.

Worked example 4

A square with two circles

Question. A square has side 20 cm. One circle is drawn inside it touching all four sides, and another passes through all four corners. Find (a) the area of the square outside the inner circle and (b) the area of the outer circle outside the square. (Use π=3.14\pi = 3.14π=3.14.)

Given: side 20 cm. Asked: the two regions.

Concept used: the inner circle's diameter is the side; the outer circle's diameter is the diagonal. Why it applies: the inner circle touches opposite sides; the corners of the square lie on the outer circle, and its diagonal passes through the centre.

  1. 1

    Inner circle: r=202=10r = \dfrac{20}{2} = 10r=220​=10 cm; area =3.14×10×10=314= 3.14 \times 10 \times 10 = 314=3.14×10×10=314 cm².

  2. 2

    (a) 20×20−314=400−314=8620 \times 20 - 314 = 400 - 314 = 8620×20−314=400−314=86 cm².

  3. 3

    Outer circle: diagonal =202= 20\sqrt{2}=202​ cm, so R=102R = 10\sqrt{2}R=102​ cm and R2=200R^2 = 200R2=200.

  4. 4

    Area =3.14×200=628= 3.14 \times 200 = 628=3.14×200=628 cm².

  5. 5

    (b) 628−400=228628 - 400 = 228628−400=228 cm².

  6. Check: the outer circle has exactly twice the area of the inner one (R2=200=2×100R^2 = 200 = 2 \times 100R2=200=2×100): 628=2×314628 = 2 \times 314628=2×314 ✔.

  7. Answer (a) 86 cm² (b) 228 cm²

    Common trap: Taking the outer radius as 20 cm (the side). The outer circle passes through the corners, so its diameter is the diagonal.

Worked example 5

A lens made of two 120° segments

Question. Two circles, each of radius 10 cm, are drawn so that each passes through the centre of the other. Find the area of the region common to both. (Use π=3.14\pi = 3.14π=3.14 and 3=1.73\sqrt{3} = 1.733​=1.73.)

Given: two circles of radius 10 cm; centres O1O_1O1​ and O2O_2O2​ with O1O2=10O_1O_2 = 10O1​O2​=10 cm. Asked: the area of the overlap.

Concept used: the common chord PQ cuts off a segment from each circle; the overlap is these two segments. Why it applies: the lens is bounded by one arc of each circle, both ending at P and Q.

  1. 1

    O1P=O1O2=O2P=10O_1P = O_1O_2 = O_2P = 10O1​P=O1​O2​=O2​P=10 cm, so △O1O2PO_1O_2PO1​O2​P is equilateral and ∠PO1O2=60°\angle PO_1O_2 = 60°∠PO1​O2​=60°. In the same way ∠QO1O2=60°\angle QO_1O_2 = 60°∠QO1​O2​=60°, so ∠PO1Q=120°\angle PO_1Q = 120°∠PO1​Q=120° (and likewise at O2O_2O2​).

  2. 2

    One 120° segment: sector =13×3.14×100=3143= \dfrac{1}{3} \times 3.14 \times 100 = \dfrac{314}{3}=31​×3.14×100=3314​ cm²; triangle =34×100=253=43.25= \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4} \times 100 = 25\sqrt{3} = 43.25=43​​×100=253​=43.25 cm².

  3. 3

    Segment =3143−43.25= \dfrac{314}{3} - 43.25=3314​−43.25 cm².

  4. 4

    Lens =2×(3143−43.25)=6283−86.5≈122.83= 2 \times \left(\dfrac{314}{3} - 43.25\right) = \dfrac{628}{3} - 86.5 \approx 122.83=2×(3314​−43.25)=3628​−86.5≈122.83 cm².

  5. Check: the lens is smaller than one circle (314 cm²) and larger than the two triangles together (86.5 cm²) ✔.

  6. Answer about 122.83 cm²

    Common trap: Using 60° for each segment (the angle in the equilateral triangle). The common chord makes two such angles at each centre: 120°.

Worked example 6

A leaf in a square

Question. ABCD is a square of side 12 cm. Quadrants of radius 12 cm are drawn inside it with centres A and C. Find the area of the leaf-shaped region where they overlap. (Use π=3.14\pi = 3.14π=3.14.)

Given: side 12 cm; quadrants centred at A and C. Asked: the area of the leaf.

Concept used: both arcs pass through B and D; the diagonal BD cuts the leaf into two 90° segments. Why it applies: AB = AD = 12 cm, so the arc with centre A goes from B to D; the same for C.

  1. 1

    One segment (centre A, chord BD, angle DAB = 90°): quadrant =14×3.14×144=113.04= \dfrac{1}{4} \times 3.14 \times 144 = 113.04=41​×3.14×144=113.04 cm²; △ABD =12×12×12=72= \dfrac{1}{2} \times 12 \times 12 = 72=21​×12×12=72 cm².

  2. 2

    Segment =113.04−72=41.04= 113.04 - 72 = 41.04=113.04−72=41.04 cm².

  3. 3

    Leaf =2×41.04=82.08= 2 \times 41.04 = 82.08=2×41.04=82.08 cm².

  4. Check (another way): quadrant A + quadrant C − square =113.04+113.04−144=82.08= 113.04 + 113.04 - 144 = 82.08=113.04+113.04−144=82.08 cm² ✔ (the two quadrants cover the square once and the leaf twice).

  5. Answer 82.08 cm²

    Common trap: Giving one segment, 41.04 cm². The leaf has two halves, one on each side of BD.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers say that reading the angle wrongly, leaving parts out of a boundary and mixing units or values of π are among the most common reasons for lost marks with shaded regions.

  • Corner sectors of a triangle taken as quadrants

    Only a right angle is 90°.

    Total angle = 180° (triangle) or 360° (quadrilateral).

  • Ring-sector =θ360π(R−r)2= \dfrac{\theta}{360}\pi(R - r)^2=360θ​π(R−r)2

    Each radius must be squared first.

    θ360π(R2−r2)\dfrac{\theta}{360}\pi(R^2 - r^2)360θ​π(R2−r2).

  • Fan boundary without the inner arc or the edges

    The boundary has four parts.

    Outer arc + inner arc + 2(R−r)2(R - r)2(R−r).

  • Outer circle of a square with radius = side

    The corners are on the outer circle.

    Diameter = diagonal =a2= a\sqrt{2}=a2​.

  • Lens = one segment, or two 60° segments

    The overlap has two halves; the chord makes 120° at each centre (equal circles through each other's centres).

    Lens = 2 segments, each with the correct angle at its centre (120° for equal circles through each other's centres).

Key points

  • Shade → name the pieces → word equation → numbers.
  • Equal-radius sectors at the corners: triangle → 12πr2\dfrac{1}{2}\pi r^221​πr2; quadrilateral → πr2\pi r^2πr2.
  • Ring: π(R2−r2)\pi(R^2 - r^2)π(R2−r2); ring-sector: θ360π(R2−r2)\dfrac{\theta}{360}\pi(R^2 - r^2)360θ​π(R2−r2); boundary: two arcs + 2(R−r)2(R - r)2(R−r).
  • Circle in a square: r=a2r = \dfrac{a}{2}r=2a​. Square in a circle: diameter = diagonal a2a\sqrt{2}a2​.
  • Overlaps: two equal circles through each other's centres → two 120° segments; two quadrants from opposite corners of a square → two 90° segments.
