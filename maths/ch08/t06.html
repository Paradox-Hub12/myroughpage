Topic 6 of 6

Proving identities

About 75 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Use the five proof moves.
  • Write a proof in the correct layout (from one side to the other).
  • Prove identities of 1, 2 and 3 moves, including those that need the conjugate.

A proof is like a road trip from one city to another: you start at the left-hand side, and every line is one short, legal step along the road, until you arrive at the right-hand side. Proving identities can look like a bag of tricks, but almost every proof in your exam uses the same five moves — and a fixed way of writing that earns full marks.

What "prove" means here

To prove an identity you must show that the left-hand side (LHS) can be turned into the right-hand side (RHS) using only facts that are always true: definitions, the three identities of Topic 5, and algebra. You are not solving for the angle, and you are not allowed to assume the result you want.

The layout that earns the marks

  1. Choose the harder side — usually the one with more terms, fractions or brackets — and write "LHS =" (or "RHS =") followed by it.
  2. Make one change per line. Each new line starts with "=". Say in words (or in a short bracket) which move you used.
  3. Stop when you reach the other side exactly, and write "= RHS. Hence proved."

Two rules you must never break:

  • Never work on both sides at once. Do not write the whole identity down and change both sides together.
  • Never move terms across the "=" sign as if solving an equation. You do not yet know the two sides are equal — that is what you are proving.

When both sides are complicated (some NCERT questions say "simplify LHS and RHS separately"), simplify the LHS to some expression X, then separately simplify the RHS to the same X, and conclude "LHS = RHS". That is still one-side-at-a-time.

Quick reminder (algebra)

(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2, (a−b)2=a2−2ab+b2(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2(a−b)2=a2−2ab+b2 and (a+b)(a−b)=a2−b2(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2(a+b)(a−b)=a2−b2. Counting moves

We label each example by how many moves it needs. 1-move and 2-move proofs are the "simple identities" the syllabus asks for, and most exam questions are like these. 3-move proofs are harder; the sample paper does include one, so they are worth practising too. Lines that only cancel or multiply out are ordinary algebra and are not counted as moves.

Cancelling safely

You may cancel a factor that multiplies the whole top and the whole bottom: cos⁡A (1+sin⁡A)cos⁡A=1+sin⁡A\dfrac{\cos A\,(1 + \sin A)}{\cos A} = 1 + \sin AcosAcosA(1+sinA)​=1+sinA. You may not cancel part of a sum: 1+sin⁡Asin⁡A\dfrac{1 + \sin A}{\sin A}sinA1+sinA​ is 1sin⁡A+1\dfrac{1}{\sin A} + 1sinA1​+1, never just "1 + 1".

You may cancel only a factor that is not zero. For acute angles, sin⁡A\sin AsinA, cos⁡A\cos AcosA, 1±sin⁡A1 \pm \sin A1±sinA and 1±cos⁡A1 \pm \cos A1±cosA are never 0. But 1−tan⁡A1 - \tan A1−tanA, 1−cot⁡A1 - \cot A1−cotA, cos⁡A−sin⁡A\cos A - \sin AcosA−sinA and 2cos⁡2A−12\cos^2 A - 12cos2A−1 are 0 at 45°, so a proof that cancels one of them holds for A≠45°A \neq 45°A=45°.

Think before you write

Spend twenty seconds planning on rough paper before you start the proof:

  • Compare the two sides. Which ratios appear on each side? If the target has only sin and cos, you will probably need Move 1. If the target is a single ratio such as 2sec⁡A2\sec A2secA, the other side must "collapse", so expect an LCM and a cancellation.
  • Spot the squares. A 1−sin⁡2A1 - \sin^2 A1−sin2A, sec⁡2A−1\sec^2 A - 1sec2A−1 or cosec⁡2A−cot⁡2A\operatorname{cosec}^2 A - \cot^2 Acosec2A−cot2A is an invitation to Move 4.
  • Look at the denominators. 1±sin⁡A1 \pm \sin A1±sinA, 1±cos⁡A1 \pm \cos A1±cosA or sec⁡A±tan⁡A\sec A \pm \tan AsecA±tanA on the bottom usually means Move 3, the conjugate.
  • Decide your target form. It often helps to write down what the other side looks like in sin and cos, so you know where you are heading.

If a proof is going badly after four or five lines, do not keep pushing: go back to the start and try changing everything to sin and cos. Examiners give marks for every correct step, so a clear, honest attempt is worth writing down.

Answer: She started from the statement she was asked to prove and changed both sides at once (multiplying both sides by cos⁡A\cos AcosA). That assumes the result. A correct proof works on one side only:

Common mistakes!

  • Proof writing is one of the four most common reasons for lost marks in this chapter. Most lost marks come from the layout, not the trigonometry. (Misusing the identities — Topic 5 — is the other big one.)

  • Writing the whole identity and changing both sides together

    It assumes what you are trying to prove.

    Start "LHS =" and change only that side, one line at a time.

  • "Moving" a term across "=" (e.g. taking cot⁡A\cot AcotA to the RHS)

    You are not solving an equation; you do not yet know the sides are equal.

    Keep the other side untouched until the last line.

  • Two or three changes squeezed into one line

    The examiner cannot follow, and slips hide.

    One change per line, with the move named.

  • Stopping without "= RHS"

    The proof is not finished until it reaches the other side.

    End with "= RHS. Hence proved."

  • Cancelling part of a sum: 1+sin⁡Asin⁡A=1+1\dfrac{1 + \sin A}{\sin A} = 1 + 1sinA1+sinA​=1+1

    Only factors of the whole top and bottom cancel.

    Split: 1sin⁡A+sin⁡Asin⁡A=cosec⁡A+1\dfrac{1}{\sin A} + \dfrac{\sin A}{\sin A} = \operatorname{cosec} A + 1sinA1​+sinAsinA​=cosecA+1.

  • (1+sin⁡A)(1−sin⁡A)=1−sin⁡A(1 + \sin A)(1 - \sin A) = 1 - \sin A(1+sinA)(1−sinA)=1−sinA or =1+sin⁡2A= 1 + \sin^2 A=1+sin2A

    (a+b)(a−b)=a2−b2(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2(a+b)(a−b)=a2−b2.

    =1−sin⁡2A=cos⁡2A= 1 - \sin^2 A = \cos^2 A=1−sin2A=cos2A.

  • Checking with one angle and calling it a proof

    A numerical check is evidence, not proof.

    Use letters throughout; check with a number only on rough paper.

Key points

  • Prove by changing one side into the other: LHS = … = RHS. Hence proved.
  • One change per line; name the move. Never work on both sides; never move terms across "=".
  • Five moves: change to sin and cos · take the LCM · multiply by the conjugate · use an identity form (including replacing 1) · factorise.
  • Stuck? Change everything to sin and cos and simplify.
  • Cancel only whole factors; (1−sin⁡A)(1+sin⁡A)=cos⁡2A(1 - \sin A)(1 + \sin A) = \cos^2 A(1−sinA)(1+sinA)=cos2A is the engine of conjugate proofs.
