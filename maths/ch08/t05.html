Topic 5 of 6

Trigonometric identities

About 55 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Prove sin²A + cos²A = 1 from Pythagoras' theorem and derive 1 + tan²A = sec²A and 1 + cot²A = cosec²A.
  • Use the three identities in their rearranged forms.
  • Express every ratio in terms of one given ratio.
  • Evaluate expressions with the identities (e.g. 9 sec²A − 9 tan²A).

Pick any acute angle you like — 30°, 45°, 71.3° — square its sine, square its cosine, and add. You will get exactly 1, every single time. A statement that is true for every angle is called an identity, and three of them are enough to handle almost every expression in this chapter.

Equation or identity?

An equation is true only for some values: 2x+1=72x + 1 = 72x+1=7 is true only when x=3x = 3x=3. An identity is true for every value for which both sides are defined. You already know algebraic identities: (a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2 holds for all numbers aaa and bbb.

A trigonometric identity is an equation involving trigonometric ratios that is true for all angles for which the ratios are defined. In Topic 3 you found that 1−tan⁡2θ1+tan⁡2θ\dfrac{1 - \tan^2\theta}{1 + \tan^2\theta}1+tan2θ1−tan2θ​ and cos⁡2θ−sin⁡2θ\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\thetacos2θ−sin2θ came out equal for one particular angle. That was not luck: they are equal for every acute angle.

The first identity, straight from Pythagoras

Take △ABC right-angled at B. Pythagoras says AB2+BC2=AC2AB^2 + BC^2 = AC^2AB2+BC2=AC2. Divide every term by AC2AC^2AC2:

(ABAC)2+(BCAC)2=1\left(\dfrac{AB}{AC}\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{BC}{AC}\right)^2 = 1(ACAB​)2+(ACBC​)2=1Swipe to see the whole equation →

From ∠A\angle A∠A, BCAC=sin⁡A\dfrac{BC}{AC} = \sin AACBC​=sinA and ABAC=cos⁡A\dfrac{AB}{AC} = \cos AACAB​=cosA. So

sin⁡2A+cos⁡2A=1\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1sin2A+cos2A=1Swipe to see the whole equation →

Worked example 1 writes this out as a full proof. A neat way to picture it: if the hypotenuse is 1 unit, the opposite side is sin⁡A\sin AsinA and the adjacent side is cos⁡A\cos AcosA, so the identity is just Pythagoras for that triangle. It also holds at 0° and 90° (02+12=10^2 + 1^2 = 102+12=1).

Now you can see the Topic 3 result: multiply the top and bottom of 1−tan⁡2θ1+tan⁡2θ\dfrac{1 - \tan^2\theta}{1 + \tan^2\theta}1+tan2θ1−tan2θ​ by cos⁡2θ\cos^2\thetacos2θ to get cos⁡2θ−sin⁡2θcos⁡2θ+sin⁡2θ\dfrac{\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta}cos2θ+sin2θcos2θ−sin2θ​, and the bottom is 1.

Two more, by dividing

Divide sin⁡2A+cos⁡2A=1\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1sin2A+cos2A=1 by cos⁡2A\cos^2 Acos2A, and then (separately) by sin⁡2A\sin^2 Asin2A:

1+tan⁡2A=sec⁡2A1 + \tan^2 A = \sec^2 A1+tan2A=sec2ASwipe to see the whole equation →

(true for 0°≤A<90°0° \leq A < 90°0°≤A<90°)

1+cot⁡2A=cosec⁡2A1 + \cot^2 A = \operatorname{cosec}^2 A1+cot2A=cosec2ASwipe to see the whole equation →

(true for 0°<A≤90°0° < A \leq 90°0°<A≤90°)

The angle limits are there because cos⁡90°=0\cos 90° = 0cos90°=0 and sin⁡0°=0\sin 0° = 0sin0°=0: you cannot divide by zero, and tan⁡90°\tan 90°tan90°, sec⁡90°\sec 90°sec90°, cot⁡0°\cot 0°cot0°, cosec⁡0°\operatorname{cosec} 0°cosec0° are not defined.

A memory hook: each identity pairs a ratio with its "partner" — sin with cos, tan with sec, cot with cosec — and the 1 always goes with tan or cot, never with sec or cosec: 1+tan⁡2A=sec⁡2A1 + \tan^2 A = \sec^2 A1+tan2A=sec2A, 1+cot⁡2A=cosec⁡2A1 + \cot^2 A = \operatorname{cosec}^2 A1+cot2A=cosec2A.

Rearranged forms — the ones you actually use

Moving terms across gives the forms that appear in exam questions. Learn to recognise them in both directions:

One ratio in terms of another

Because sin⁡2A+cos⁡2A=1\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1sin2A+cos2A=1, knowing sin⁡A\sin AsinA tells you cos⁡A\cos AcosA without drawing a triangle:

cos⁡A=1−sin⁡2A\cos A = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 A}cosA=1−sin2A​Swipe to see the whole equation →

We take the positive square root because, for an acute angle, every ratio is positive. In the same way, sec⁡A=1+tan⁡2A\sec A = \sqrt{1 + \tan^2 A}secA=1+tan2A​ and cosec⁡A=1+cot⁡2A\operatorname{cosec} A = \sqrt{1 + \cot^2 A}cosecA=1+cot2A​. This is the identity route to the questions of Topic 3; use whichever route you find clearer.

Identity or not? How to test

To show that a statement is not an identity, one counter-example is enough: find a single angle where the two sides differ. To show that it is an identity, a few numerical checks are never enough — you need a proof that works for every angle (Topic 6). Numerical checks are still a useful test on rough paper. Try 30° and 60°, and be wary of 45°: there sin⁡A=cos⁡A\sin A = \cos AsinA=cosA and tan⁡A=cot⁡A\tan A = \cot AtanA=cotA, so many wrong statements happen to work at 45° and nowhere else.

What the identities do NOT say

The identities are about squares. Students lose marks by "simplifying" things that are not identities:

  • sin⁡A+cos⁡A\sin A + \cos AsinA+cosA is not 1. At 45° it is 12+12=2\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}2​1​+2​1​=2​.
  • 1−sin⁡2A1 - \sin^2 A1−sin2A is cos⁡2A\cos^2 Acos2A, not cos⁡A\cos AcosA.
  • (sin⁡A+cos⁡A)2(\sin A + \cos A)^2(sinA+cosA)2 is not 1. Expand it: sin⁡2A+2sin⁡Acos⁡A+cos⁡2A=1+2sin⁡Acos⁡A\sin^2 A + 2\sin A\cos A + \cos^2 A = 1 + 2\sin A\cos Asin2A+2sinAcosA+cos2A=1+2sinAcosA.
  • sec⁡2A+tan⁡2A\sec^2 A + \tan^2 Asec2A+tan2A is not 1. It is the difference sec⁡2A−tan⁡2A\sec^2 A - \tan^2 Asec2A−tan2A that equals 1.

Using the identities to find a value

Many 1- and 2-mark questions look long but collapse to a single number. The routine is short:

  1. Look for a common factor and take it out: 9sec⁡2A−9tan⁡2A=9(sec⁡2A−tan⁡2A)9\sec^2 A - 9\tan^2 A = 9(\sec^2 A - \tan^2 A)9sec2A−9tan2A=9(sec2A−tan2A).
  2. Look for one side of an identity inside a bracket — sec⁡2A−tan⁡2A\sec^2 A - \tan^2 Asec2A−tan2A, 1−cos⁡2A1 - \cos^2 A1−cos2A, 1+cot⁡2A1 + \cot^2 A1+cot2A — and replace it by the other side.
  3. Use reciprocals to cancel: sin⁡2A×cosec⁡2A=1\sin^2 A \times \operatorname{cosec}^2 A = 1sin2A×cosec2A=1.
  4. Check with a table angle on rough paper. If the answer is a constant, it must come out the same at 30°, 45° and 60°.

That last check is powerful: if you try 45° and 60° and get two different numbers, the expression is not constant, and you have made a slip somewhere.

Proving sin⁡2A+cos⁡2A=1\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1sin2A+cos2A=1

Question. Prove that sin⁡2A+cos⁡2A=1\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1sin2A+cos2A=1 for an acute angle A.

Given: △ABC with ∠B=90°\angle B = 90°∠B=90°. To prove: sin⁡2A+cos⁡2A=1\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1sin2A+cos2A=1.

  1. Statement Reason
    1. AB2+BC2=AC2AB^2 + BC^2 = AC^2AB2+BC2=AC2 Pythagoras' theorem in △ABC (right angle at B)
    2. AB2AC2+BC2AC2=AC2AC2\dfrac{AB^2}{AC^2} + \dfrac{BC^2}{AC^2} = \dfrac{AC^2}{AC^2}AC2AB2​+AC2BC2​=AC2AC2​ Divide every term by AC2AC^2AC2 (AC≠0AC \neq 0AC=0)
    3. (ABAC)2+(BCAC)2=1\left(\dfrac{AB}{AC}\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{BC}{AC}\right)^2 = 1(ACAB​)2+(ACBC​)2=1 Write each fraction as a square; AC2AC2=1\dfrac{AC^2}{AC^2} = 1AC2AC2​=1
    4. cos⁡A=ABAC\cos A = \dfrac{AB}{AC}cosA=ACAB​ and sin⁡A=BCAC\sin A = \dfrac{BC}{AC}sinA=ACBC​ Definitions: adjacent ÷ hypotenuse, opposite ÷ hypotenuse (from ∠A\angle A∠A)
    5. (cos⁡A)2+(sin⁡A)2=1(\cos A)^2 + (\sin A)^2 = 1(cosA)2+(sinA)2=1 Substitute step 4 into step 3
    6. sin⁡2A+cos⁡2A=1\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1sin2A+cos2A=1 Write (sin⁡A)2(\sin A)^2(sinA)2 as sin⁡2A\sin^2 Asin2A and reorder. Hence proved.

  2. Check: at A=30°A = 30°A=30°: (12)2+(32)2=14+34=1\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 = \dfrac{1}{4} + \dfrac{3}{4} = 1(21​)2+(23​​)2=41​+43​=1 ✔.

Which of the following is not equal to 1 for every acute angle A?

Common mistakes!

  • Misusing the identities is one of the four most common reasons for lost marks in this chapter. Each line below is a real "identity" students invent.

  • sin⁡A+cos⁡A=1\sin A + \cos A = 1sinA+cosA=1

    Only the squares add to 1. At 45° the sum is 2\sqrt{2}2​.

    sin⁡2A+cos⁡2A=1\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1sin2A+cos2A=1.

  • 1−sin⁡2A=cos⁡A1 - \sin^2 A = \cos A1−sin2A=cosA

    The identity gives cos⁡2A\cos^2 Acos2A.

    1−sin⁡2A=cos⁡2A1 - \sin^2 A = \cos^2 A1−sin2A=cos2A; take a square root only if you really need cos⁡A\cos AcosA.

  • sec⁡2A+tan⁡2A=1\sec^2 A + \tan^2 A = 1sec2A+tan2A=1

    It is the difference that is 1.

    sec⁡2A−tan⁡2A=1\sec^2 A - \tan^2 A = 1sec2A−tan2A=1 (and cosec⁡2A−cot⁡2A=1\operatorname{cosec}^2 A - \cot^2 A = 1cosec2A−cot2A=1).

  • 1+tan⁡2A=cosec⁡2A1 + \tan^2 A = \operatorname{cosec}^2 A1+tan2A=cosec2A

    Wrong partner: tan goes with sec.

    Pairs: sin–cos, tan–sec, cot–cosec.

  • (sin⁡A+cos⁡A)2=1(\sin A + \cos A)^2 = 1(sinA+cosA)2=1

    The middle term 2sin⁡Acos⁡A2\sin A\cos A2sinAcosA is missing.

    (sin⁡A+cos⁡A)2=1+2sin⁡Acos⁡A(\sin A + \cos A)^2 = 1 + 2\sin A\cos A(sinA+cosA)2=1+2sinAcosA.

  • 1+tan⁡2A=1+tan⁡A\sqrt{1 + \tan^2 A} = 1 + \tan A1+tan2A​=1+tanA

    A square root does not split over a sum.

    Leave it as 1+tan⁡2A\sqrt{1 + \tan^2 A}1+tan2A​, or write it as sec⁡A\sec AsecA.

Key points

  • An identity is true for every angle for which both sides are defined.
  • sin⁡2A+cos⁡2A=1\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1sin2A+cos2A=1 is Pythagoras divided by hyp². Divide by cos⁡2A\cos^2 Acos2A → 1+tan⁡2A=sec⁡2A1 + \tan^2 A = \sec^2 A1+tan2A=sec2A; divide by sin⁡2A\sin^2 Asin2A → 1+cot⁡2A=cosec⁡2A1 + \cot^2 A = \operatorname{cosec}^2 A1+cot2A=cosec2A.
  • Use the rearranged forms: 1−sin⁡2A=cos⁡2A1 - \sin^2 A = \cos^2 A1−sin2A=cos2A, sec⁡2A−tan⁡2A=1\sec^2 A - \tan^2 A = 1sec2A−tan2A=1, cosec⁡2A−1=cot⁡2A\operatorname{cosec}^2 A - 1 = \cot^2 Acosec2A−1=cot2A, and the bracket forms.
  • One ratio in terms of another: e.g. cos⁡A=1−sin⁡2A\cos A = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 A}cosA=1−sin2A​ (positive root for acute A).
  • The identities are about squares — sin⁡A+cos⁡A≠1\sin A + \cos A \neq 1sinA+cosA=1.
