Toolkit: the coordinate plane, squares and roots

About 30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Plot points in all four quadrants and on the axes, and read the coordinates of a point.
  • Use Pythagoras' theorem for a right triangle drawn on a grid.
  • Square negative numbers correctly and simplify square roots (for example, √50 = 5√2).
Why this lesson exists

Every question in this chapter starts with points on a grid and ends with a square root. When an answer goes wrong, the slip is very often here: a point plotted in the wrong quadrant, (−5)2(-5)^2(−5)2 written as −25-25−25, or 50\sqrt{50}50​ left unsimplified. If you already have these skills, prove it below and go straight to Topic 2.

Your seat in the exam hall is "Row 4, Seat 7". Two numbers, in a fixed order, tell the invigilator exactly where you are — swap them and you are in someone else's seat. A map app does the same with every shop and bus stop. In this chapter you will use such pairs of numbers to find how far apart two places are and where to stand between them, without measuring anything. Half an hour here makes the rest of the chapter much easier.

  1. The coordinate plane

Two number lines cross at right angles at the origin O(0, 0): the horizontal x-axis and the vertical y-axis. A point P is written (x, y):

  • the x-coordinate (abscissa) says how far to go right (+) or left (−) from the y-axis;
  • the y-coordinate (ordinate) says how far to go up (+) or down (−) from the x-axis.

Order matters: (2,5)(2, 5)(2,5) and (5,2)(5, 2)(5,2) are different points.

The axes cut the plane into four quadrants, numbered anticlockwise from the top right:

Quadrant Sign of x Sign of y Example
I + + (3,2)(3, 2)(3,2)
II − + (−3,2)(-3, 2)(−3,2)
III − − (−3,−2)(-3, -2)(−3,−2)
IV + − (3,−2)(3, -2)(3,−2)

Points on the axes belong to no quadrant. A point on the x-axis has y=0y = 0y=0: it is (x,0)(x, 0)(x,0). A point on the y-axis has x=0x = 0x=0: it is (0,y)(0, y)(0,y).

  1. Pythagoras' theorem on a grid

In a right triangle, (hypotenuse)² = (one shorter side)² + (other shorter side)². On squared paper, a slanting segment is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose two other sides run along the grid lines. You get their lengths by counting squares — or, faster, by subtracting coordinates: the horizontal side is the difference of the x-coordinates, the vertical side is the difference of the y-coordinates.

  1. Squaring negatives

  • A negative number times itself is positive: (−6)2=(−6)×(−6)=36(-6)^2 = (-6) \times (-6) = 36(−6)2=(−6)×(−6)=36.
  • Work out the bracket first, then square: (−2−6)2=(−8)2=64(-2 - 6)^2 = (-8)^2 = 64(−2−6)2=(−8)2=64.
  • Subtracting a negative means adding: 3−(−5)=3+5=83 - (-5) = 3 + 5 = 83−(−5)=3+5=8.
  • Watch the brackets: (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9, but −32-3^2−32 means −(32)=−9-(3^2) = -9−(32)=−9. In this chapter every difference is squared inside brackets, so the result is never negative.

  1. Simplifying square roots

Split the number into a perfect square times what is left, and take the root of the perfect square: 50=25×2=52\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \times 2} = 5\sqrt{2}50​=25×2​=52​. Use the largest perfect square you can find (4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, …).

A root of a sum is not a sum of roots: 9+16=25=5\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 59+16​=25​=5, but 9+16=3+4=7\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 79​+16​=3+4=7. Always add first, then take the root.

Worked example 1

Plotting points and naming where they lie

Question. Plot the points A(3, −2), B(−4, 1), C(0, 5), D(−2, 0), E(−3, −3) and F(2.5, 4). For each point, name its quadrant, or the axis it lies on.
Print graph paper

Given: six points. Asked: where each one lies.

Concept used: the signs of x and y decide the quadrant; a zero coordinate puts the point on an axis. Why it applies: each point is given by its two coordinates.

  1. 1

    A(3, −2): go 3 right, then 2 down. x is +, y is −: quadrant IV.

  2. 2

    B(−4, 1): go 4 left, then 1 up. x is −, y is +: quadrant II.

  3. 3

    C(0, 5): do not move sideways; go 5 up. x=0x = 0x=0: on the y-axis.

  4. 4

    D(−2, 0): go 2 left; do not move up or down. y=0y = 0y=0: on the x-axis.

  5. 5

    E(−3, −3): go 3 left, then 3 down. Both negative: quadrant III.

  6. 6

    F(2.5, 4): go 2.5 right (half-way between 2 and 3), then 4 up. Both positive: quadrant I.

  7. Check: read each plotted dot back from the grid: the number under it on the x-axis and the number level with it on the y-axis must be the given pair ✔.

  8. Answer A — IV; B — II; C — y-axis; D — x-axis; E — III; F — I.

    Common trap: Plotting D(−2, 0) on the y-axis at "−2". The first number is always the sideways move: (−2,0)(-2, 0)(−2,0) is on the x-axis, while (0,−2)(0, -2)(0,−2) would be on the y-axis.

Worked example 2

Pythagoras on a grid

Question. Find the length of (a) PQ, where P(1, 2) and Q(5, 5); (b) RS, where R(−4, −1) and S(2, 7). Draw a right triangle on the grid for each.
Print graph paper

Given: two pairs of points. Asked: the length of each segment.

Concept used: Pythagoras' theorem in a right triangle whose two shorter sides run along the grid lines. Why it applies: from any slanting segment you can go across and then up to make a right angle.

  1. 1

    (a)

    From P go across to the corner K(5, 2), then up to Q. The angle at K is 90°.

  2. 2

    Horizontal side: PK=5−1=4PK = 5 - 1 = 4PK=5−1=4 units.

  3. 3

    Vertical side: KQ=5−2=3KQ = 5 - 2 = 3KQ=5−2=3 units.

  4. 4

    Pythagoras.

    PQ2=42+32=16+9=25PQ^2 = 4^2 + 3^2 = 16 + 9 = 25PQ2=42+32=16+9=25Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Take the positive root: PQ=5PQ = 5PQ=5 units.

  6. 6

    (b)

    Corner L(2, −1). Horizontal side: RL=2−(−4)=6RL = 2 - (-4) = 6RL=2−(−4)=6 units. Vertical side: LS=7−(−1)=8LS = 7 - (-1) = 8LS=7−(−1)=8 units.

  7. 7

    Pythagoras.

    RS2=62+82=36+64=100RS^2 = 6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100RS2=62+82=36+64=100Swipe to see the whole equation →
  8. 8

    RS=10RS = 10RS=10 units.

  9. Check: count the squares on the grid: 4 across and 3 up for (a), 6 across and 8 up for (b) ✔. (3, 4, 5 and 6, 8, 10 are the well-known right-triangle triples.)

  10. Answer (a) PQ=5PQ = 5PQ=5 units (b) RS=10RS = 10RS=10 units

    Common trap: In (b), writing the horizontal side as 2−4=−22 - 4 = -22−4=−2. Moving from −4 to 2 crosses the y-axis: the gap is 2−(−4)=62 - (-4) = 62−(−4)=6.

Worked example 3

Squaring negatives

Question. Find (a) (−6)2(-6)^2(−6)2 (b) (−2−6)2(-2 - 6)^2(−2−6)2 (c) (3−(−5))2(3 - (-5))^2(3−(−5))2 (d) (−7)2+(4−9)2(-7)^2 + (4 - 9)^2(−7)2+(4−9)2.

Given: four expressions. Asked: their values.

Concept used: work out the bracket first; a negative number times itself is positive. Why it applies: every expression is a bracket squared.

  1. 1

    (a) (−6)2=(−6)×(−6)=36(-6)^2 = (-6) \times (-6) = 36(−6)2=(−6)×(−6)=36.

  2. 2

    (b) Bracket first: −2−6=−8-2 - 6 = -8−2−6=−8. Then (−8)2=64(-8)^2 = 64(−8)2=64.

  3. 3

    (c) Subtracting −5 means adding 5: 3−(−5)=3+5=83 - (-5) = 3 + 5 = 83−(−5)=3+5=8. Then 82=648^2 = 6482=64.

  4. 4

    (d) (−7)2=49(-7)^2 = 49(−7)2=49 and 4−9=−54 - 9 = -54−9=−5, so (−5)2=25(-5)^2 = 25(−5)2=25. Add: 49+25=7449 + 25 = 7449+25=74.

  5. Check: a square can never be negative, and all four answers are positive ✔.

  6. Answer (a) 36 (b) 64 (c) 64 (d) 74

    Common trap: In (b), writing −2−6-2 - 6−2−6 as −4-4−4 (that is −2+6-2 + 6−2+6 with the wrong sign) or as 444; in (c), writing 3−(−5)=−23 - (-5) = -23−(−5)=−2. One sign slip changes the whole answer.

Worked example 4

Simplifying square roots

Question. Simplify (a) 45\sqrt{45}45​ (b) 200\sqrt{200}200​ (c) 16+9\sqrt{16 + 9}16+9​.

Given: three roots. Asked: their simplest form.

Concept used: a×b=a×b\sqrt{a \times b} = \sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b}a×b​=a​×b​; take out the largest perfect square. Why it applies: 45 and 200 each have a perfect-square factor.

  1. 1

    (a) 45=9×545 = 9 \times 545=9×5, so 45=9×5=35\sqrt{45} = \sqrt{9} \times \sqrt{5} = 3\sqrt{5}45​=9​×5​=35​.

  2. 2

    (b) 200=100×2200 = 100 \times 2200=100×2, so 200=100×2=102\sqrt{200} = \sqrt{100} \times \sqrt{2} = 10\sqrt{2}200​=100​×2​=102​.

  3. 3

    (c) Add first: 16+9=2516 + 9 = 2516+9=25, so 16+9=25=5\sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} = 516+9​=25​=5.

  4. Check: square each answer: (35)2=9×5=45(3\sqrt{5})^2 = 9 \times 5 = 45(35​)2=9×5=45 ✔; (102)2=100×2=200(10\sqrt{2})^2 = 100 \times 2 = 200(102​)2=100×2=200 ✔; 52=255^2 = 2552=25 ✔.

  5. Answer (a) 353\sqrt{5}35​ (b) 10210\sqrt{2}102​ (c) 5

    Common trap: In (b), using a smaller square: 200=4×50=250\sqrt{200} = \sqrt{4 \times 50} = 2\sqrt{50}200​=4×50​=250​ is true but not finished. In (c), writing 16+9=7\sqrt{16} + \sqrt{9} = 716​+9​=7.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers name sign errors when squaring among the most common reasons for lost marks in this chapter. They start here.

  • (−5)2=−25(-5)^2 = -25(−5)2=−25

    A negative times a negative is positive.

    (−5)2=(−5)×(−5)=25(-5)^2 = (-5) \times (-5) = 25(−5)2=(−5)×(−5)=25.

  • (−3−2)2=(−1)2(-3 - 2)^2 = (-1)^2(−3−2)2=(−1)2

    −3−2-3 - 2−3−2 is −5-5−5, not −1-1−1.

    Work out the bracket on its own line first: −3−2=−5-3 - 2 = -5−3−2=−5.

  • 4−(−2)=24 - (-2) = 24−(−2)=2

    Subtracting a negative means adding.

    4−(−2)=4+2=64 - (-2) = 4 + 2 = 64−(−2)=4+2=6.

  • (0,4)(0, 4)(0,4) for a point on the x-axis

    On the x-axis, y=0y = 0y=0.

    x-axis: (x,0)(x, 0)(x,0); y-axis: (0,y)(0, y)(0,y).

  • 9+16=3+4\sqrt{9 + 16} = 3 + 49+16​=3+4

    The root of a sum is not the sum of the roots.

    Add first: 25=5\sqrt{25} = 525​=5.

  • 72=218\sqrt{72} = 2\sqrt{18}72​=218​ left as the answer

    Not fully simplified.

    Use the largest square: 36×2=62\sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6\sqrt{2}36×2​=62​.

Key points

  • A point is (x,y)(x, y)(x,y): x is the sideways move (right +, left −), y is the up-down move (up +, down −).
  • Quadrants I to IV go anticlockwise: (+,+)(+, +)(+,+), (−,+)(-, +)(−,+), (−,−)(-, -)(−,−), (+,−)(+, -)(+,−). Points on an axis are in no quadrant.
  • On the x-axis a point is (x,0)(x, 0)(x,0); on the y-axis it is (0,y)(0, y)(0,y).
  • A slanting segment on a grid is the hypotenuse of a right triangle; its other sides are the differences of the coordinates.
  • Bracket first, then square; a square is never negative.
  • Simplify roots with the largest perfect square; add before taking a root.
