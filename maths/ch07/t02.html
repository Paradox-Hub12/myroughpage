Topic 2 of 5

The distance formula

About 60 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Derive the distance formula from Pythagoras' theorem; find the distance of a point from the origin and from an axis.
  • Find the distance between two points (including points with letters for coordinates) and find an unknown coordinate from a given distance (two values).
  • Find the condition for a point to be equidistant from two points (a relation between x and y), and find such a point on an axis.

A delivery app knows where the shop is and where your house is, as two pairs of numbers. To show the straight-line ("as the crow flies") distance, it does not need to measure anything: over short distances a formula built from Pythagoras' theorem is enough. In this topic you learn that formula and use it in every direction: to find a distance, to find a missing coordinate, and to find the points that are the same distance from two places.

The formula

Take any two points P(x1,y1)P(x_1, y_1)P(x1​,y1​) and Q(x2,y2)Q(x_2, y_2)Q(x2​,y2​). From P go across until you are straight below (or above) Q, at the corner T(x2,y1)T(x_2, y_1)T(x2​,y1​). Then go up (or down) to Q. The angle at T is 90°, so:

  • the horizontal side is PT=x2−x1PT = x_2 - x_1PT=x2​−x1​ (or its opposite, if it comes out negative);
  • the vertical side is TQ=y2−y1TQ = y_2 - y_1TQ=y2​−y1​ (likewise);
  • by Pythagoras, PQ2=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2PQ^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2PQ2=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2.

A length is never negative, so we take the positive root:

PQ=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}PQ=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​Swipe to see the whole equation →

This is the distance formula. Squaring removes any minus sign, so the order of the points does not matter: (x1−x2)2(x_1 - x_2)^2(x1​−x2​)2 is the same as (x2−x1)2(x_2 - x_1)^2(x2​−x1​)2.

The fixed routine (use it every time):

  1. Label the points: (x1,y1)=…(x_1, y_1) = \ldots(x1​,y1​)=…, (x2,y2)=…(x_2, y_2) = \ldots(x2​,y2​)=…
  2. Substitute in brackets, signs included: (x2−x1)=(…−(…))(x_2 - x_1) = (\ldots - (\ldots))(x2​−x1​)=(…−(…)).
  3. Work out each bracket, then square it.
  4. Add.
  5. Take the root and simplify it.

Special cases

  • From the origin: the distance of P(x,y)P(x, y)P(x,y) from O(0,0)O(0, 0)O(0,0) is x2+y2\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}x2+y2​.
  • Same y-coordinate (a horizontal segment): the distance is just the difference of the x-coordinates; same x-coordinate: the difference of the y-coordinates.
  • Distance from an axis. The distance of P(x,y)P(x, y)P(x,y) from the x-axis is the length of the perpendicular from P to the x-axis, which is ∣y∣\lvert y \rvert∣y∣ (the size of y, without its sign). The distance from the y-axis is ∣x∣\lvert x \rvert∣x∣. So (2,3)(2, 3)(2,3) is 3 units from the x-axis and 2 units from the y-axis.
  • Mirror image in an axis. The image of P(x,y)P(x, y)P(x,y) in the x-axis is the point the same distance on the other side of the x-axis: (x,−y)(x, -y)(x,−y) — x stays, y changes sign. The image in the y-axis is (−x,y)(-x, y)(−x,y). For example, the image of (4,−7)(4, -7)(4,−7) in the x-axis is (4,7)(4, 7)(4,7), and in the y-axis it is (−4,−7)(-4, -7)(−4,−7). (Check: the mid-point of a point and its image lies on the mirror.)

Finding an unknown coordinate

If a distance is given and one coordinate is unknown, write the distance formula, square both sides to remove the root, and solve. You usually get a quadratic of the form (y−3)2=64(y - 3)^2 = 64(y−3)2=64, which has two answers, y−3=8y - 3 = 8y−3=8 or y−3=−8y - 3 = -8y−3=−8. Both are correct unless the question rules one out (for example, "the point is in the fourth quadrant"). Check each value by putting it back into the distance formula.

Points equidistant from two points

P is equidistant from A and B when PA=PBPA = PBPA=PB. It is easier to use PA2=PB2PA^2 = PB^2PA2=PB2 (no roots). Expand both sides; the x2x^2x2 and y2y^2y2 terms cancel and a linear equation in x and y is left. Every point on that line is equidistant from A and B — the line is the perpendicular bisector of AB, and it passes through the mid-point of AB.

If the point must lie on an axis, use the axis card: on the x-axis write the point as (x,0)(x, 0)(x,0); on the y-axis as (0,y)(0, y)(0,y). Then there is only one unknown.

Worked example 1

The distance between two points

Question. Find the distance between A(2, −1) and B(7, 3).

Given: A(2, −1), B(7, 3). Asked: AB.

Concept used: the distance formula. Why it applies: both points are given by their coordinates.

  1. 1

    Label: (x1,y1)=(2,−1)(x_1, y_1) = (2, -1)(x1​,y1​)=(2,−1) and (x2,y2)=(7,3)(x_2, y_2) = (7, 3)(x2​,y2​)=(7,3).

  2. 2

    Write the formula.

    AB=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2AB = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}AB=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Substitute in brackets.

    AB=(7−2)2+(3−(−1))2AB = \sqrt{(7 - 2)^2 + (3 - (-1))^2}AB=(7−2)2+(3−(−1))2​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Work out each bracket: 7−2=57 - 2 = 57−2=5 and 3−(−1)=3+1=43 - (-1) = 3 + 1 = 43−(−1)=3+1=4.

    AB=52+42AB = \sqrt{5^2 + 4^2}AB=52+42​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Square and add.

    AB=25+16=41AB = \sqrt{25 + 16} = \sqrt{41}AB=25+16​=41​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    41 has no perfect-square factor, so 41\sqrt{41}41​ is already simplest.

  7. Check: take the points the other way round: (2−7)2+(−1−3)2=25+16=41\sqrt{(2 - 7)^2 + (-1 - 3)^2} = \sqrt{25 + 16} = \sqrt{41}(2−7)2+(−1−3)2​=25+16​=41​ ✔.

  8. Answer AB=41AB = \sqrt{41}AB=41​ units (about 6.4 units)

    Common trap: Writing 3−(−1)3 - (-1)3−(−1) as 3−1=23 - 1 = 23−1=2, which gives 25+4=29\sqrt{25 + 4} = \sqrt{29}25+4​=29​. Keep the bracket round the negative coordinate.

  3. 3

    Brackets: 5−(−3)=5+3=85 - (-3) = 5 + 3 = 85−(−3)=5+3=8 and −2−4=−6-2 - 4 = -6−2−4=−6.

Worked example 4

From the origin and from the axes

Question. For the point M(−8, 15), find its distance (a) from the origin (b) from the x-axis (c) from the y-axis.

Given: M(−8, 15). Asked: three distances.

Concept used: distance from O is x2+y2\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}x2+y2​; distance from the x-axis is ∣y∣\lvert y \rvert∣y∣; from the y-axis is ∣x∣\lvert x \rvert∣x∣. Why it applies: the perpendicular from M to an axis is a straight horizontal or vertical segment.

  1. 1

    (a) OM=(−8)2+152=64+225=289=17OM = \sqrt{(-8)^2 + 15^2} = \sqrt{64 + 225} = \sqrt{289} = 17OM=(−8)2+152​=64+225​=289​=17.

  2. 2

    (b) The perpendicular from M to the x-axis is vertical and goes from height 15 to height 0: its length is ∣15∣=15\lvert 15 \rvert = 15∣15∣=15.

  3. 3

    (c) The perpendicular from M to the y-axis is horizontal and goes from −8 to 0: its length is ∣−8∣=8\lvert -8 \rvert = 8∣−8∣=8.

  4. Check: O, M and the foot X(−8, 0) form a right triangle with legs OX = 8 and XM = 15, and 82+152=64+225=289=1728^2 + 15^2 = 64 + 225 = 289 = 17^282+152=64+225=289=172, and 82+152=64+225=289=1728^2 + 15^2 = 64 + 225 = 289 = 17^282+152=64+225=289=172 ✔.

  5. Answer (a) 17 units (b) 15 units (c) 8 units

    Common trap: Mixing up the axes: the distance from the x-axis is the y-coordinate's size, and the distance from the y-axis is the x-coordinate's size. Another slip is answering "−8": a distance is never negative.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers name sign errors when squaring, points on an axis and keeping only one value among the commonest reasons for lost marks in this chapter.

  • (−3−2)2(-3 - 2)^2(−3−2)2 written as (−1)2(-1)^2(−1)2, or (−5)2=−25(-5)^2 = -25(−5)2=−25

    Substitute in brackets; work out each bracket on its own; a square is never negative.
