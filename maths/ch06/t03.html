Topic 3 of 5

The Basic Proportionality Theorem and its converse

About 70 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • State and prove the Basic Proportionality Theorem (BPT).
  • State the converse of BPT (without proof) and use it to decide whether a line is parallel to a side.
  • Solve problems with BPT, including the AD/AB = AE/AC form and trapezium and quadrilateral problems.

Take a triangle of card and slide a ruler across it, always keeping the ruler parallel to the bottom edge. Wherever you stop, the ruler cuts the left side and the right side in the same ratio — if it is one-third of the way down the left side, it is one-third of the way down the right side too. This is the one theorem in the chapter that you must be able to prove, and all four recent CBSE sample papers ask for its proof as a 5-mark question.

The theorem

Theorem 6.1 (Basic Proportionality Theorem)

"If a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides in distinct points, the other two sides are divided in the same ratio."

In △ABC, if a line meets AB at D and AC at E, with DE∥BCDE \parallel BCDE∥BC, then

ADDB=AEEC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}DBAD​=ECAE​Swipe to see the whole equation →

The Greek mathematician Thales is believed to have used this result, so it is also called Thales' theorem. "Distinct points" means D is not A or B and E is not A or C — otherwise a piece would have length 0.

Three facts the proof uses

Quick reminder (Topic 1 and Class 9).

  1. Area of a triangle =12×base×height= \dfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}=21​×base×height, with the height perpendicular to the line of the base.
  2. Two triangles with the same height have areas in the ratio of their bases.
  3. Triangles on the same base and between the same parallels have equal areas.

Worked example 1 below builds the proof from these three facts. Learn the construction (join BE and CD; draw DM⊥ACDM \perp ACDM⊥AC and EN⊥ABEN \perp ABEN⊥AB), the three area ratios, and the reason for the equal areas — each of these carries marks.

Other forms of the same result

From ADDB=AEEC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}DBAD​=ECAE​ you also get (turn both sides upside down, add 1, and turn back)

ADAB=AEACandDBAB=ECAC\dfrac{AD}{AB} = \dfrac{AE}{AC} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \dfrac{DB}{AB} = \dfrac{EC}{AC}ABAD​=ACAE​andABDB​=ACEC​Swipe to see the whole equation →

Use whichever form matches the lengths you are given — but never mix a part-to-part ratio on one side with a part-to-whole ratio on the other.

The converse

Theorem 6.2

"If a line divides any two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, then the line is parallel to the third side."

So if ADDB=AEEC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}DBAD​=ECAE​, then DE∥BCDE \parallel BCDE∥BC; and if the two ratios are not equal, DE is not parallel to BC. The syllabus asks you to state and use the converse, not to prove it. Never assume a line is parallel because it looks parallel: test the ratios.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Proving the Basic Proportionality Theorem

Question. Prove that if a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides in distinct points, the other two sides are divided in the same ratio.

Given: In △ABC, a line parallel to BC meets AB at D and AC at E (D, E distinct from the vertices), so DE∥BCDE \parallel BCDE∥BC.

  1. To prove: ADDB=AEEC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}DBAD​=ECAE​.

    Construction: Join BE and CD. Draw DM⊥ACDM \perp ACDM⊥AC (M on AC) and EN⊥ABEN \perp ABEN⊥AB (N on AB).

    Concept used: areas of triangles with a common height; triangles on the same base and between the same parallels. Why it applies: the two ratios we want are ratios of bases, and the parallel lines give two equal areas.

    Statement Reason
    1. ar(ADE)=12×AD×EN\text{ar}(ADE) = \dfrac{1}{2} \times AD \times ENar(ADE)=21​×AD×EN Area =12×= \dfrac{1}{2} \times=21​× base ×\times× height; base AD lies on AB and EN⊥ABEN \perp ABEN⊥AB
    2. ar(BDE)=12×DB×EN\text{ar}(BDE) = \dfrac{1}{2} \times DB \times ENar(BDE)=21​×DB×EN Base DB lies on the same line AB; the height from E is the same EN
    3. ar(ADE)ar(BDE)=ADDB\dfrac{\text{ar}(ADE)}{\text{ar}(BDE)} = \dfrac{AD}{DB}ar(BDE)ar(ADE)​=DBAD​ … (1) Divide step 1 by step 2; 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​ and EN cancel
    4. ar(ADE)=12×AE×DM\text{ar}(ADE) = \dfrac{1}{2} \times AE \times DMar(ADE)=21​×AE×DM Now take base AE on AC; DM⊥ACDM \perp ACDM⊥AC
    5. ar(DEC)=12×EC×DM\text{ar}(DEC) = \dfrac{1}{2} \times EC \times DMar(DEC)=21​×EC×DM Base EC lies on the same line AC; same height DM
    6. ar(ADE)ar(DEC)=AEEC\dfrac{\text{ar}(ADE)}{\text{ar}(DEC)} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}ar(DEC)ar(ADE)​=ECAE​ … (2) Divide step 4 by step 5
    7. ar(BDE)=ar(DEC)\text{ar}(BDE) = \text{ar}(DEC)ar(BDE)=ar(DEC) … (3) △BDE and △DEC are on the same base DE and between the same parallels DE and BC
    8. ADDB=AEEC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}DBAD​=ECAE​ From (1), (2) and (3): the left sides of (1) and (2) are now equal, so the right sides are equal. Hence proved.

    In plain words: the triangle ADE is compared with two different triangles that have the same area. Measured with base on AB it gives ADDB\dfrac{AD}{DB}DBAD​; measured with base on AC it gives AEEC\dfrac{AE}{EC}ECAE​. Same comparison, so same ratio.

  2. Check (with numbers): draw A (3, 8), B (0, 0), C (11, 0) on squared paper and take D on AB with AD : DB = 2 : 3. The line through D parallel to BC is the line y = 4.8; it meets AC at E (6.2, 4.8), and AE : EC = 2 : 3 too. △BDE and △DEC both have base DE = 4.4 and height 4.8, so both areas are 10.56 square units ✔. (If the triangle has an obtuse angle, a foot N or M can fall on a side produced; the heights and the proof are unchanged.)

  3. Answer ADDB=AEEC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}DBAD​=ECAE​ — proved using the construction and steps (1), (2), (3).

    Common trap: Writing "ar(BDE) = ar(DEC)" with no reason, or with the reason "they look equal". The reason that earns the mark is: same base DE and between the same parallels DE and BC.

Worked example 2

Finding a piece directly

Question. In △ABC, DE∥BCDE \parallel BCDE∥BC with D on AB and E on AC. AD=2.4AD = 2.4AD=2.4 cm, DB=3.6DB = 3.6DB=3.6 cm and AE=2AE = 2AE=2 cm. Find EC.

Given: DE∥BCDE \parallel BCDE∥BC, AD=2.4AD = 2.4AD=2.4, DB=3.6DB = 3.6DB=3.6, AE=2AE = 2AE=2 (cm). Asked: EC.

Concept used: BPT, ADDB=AEEC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}DBAD​=ECAE​. Why it applies: DE is parallel to BC and meets the other two sides.

  1. 1

    Write BPT.

    ADDB=AEEC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}DBAD​=ECAE​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Substitute.

    2.43.6=2EC\dfrac{2.4}{3.6} = \dfrac{2}{EC}3.62.4​=EC2​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Simplify the left side.

    23=2EC\dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{2}{EC}32​=EC2​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Cross-multiply: 2×EC=2×32 \times EC = 2 \times 32×EC=2×3.

    EC=3 cmEC = 3 \text{ cm}EC=3 cmSwipe to see the whole equation →

  5. Check: AEEC=23\dfrac{AE}{EC} = \dfrac{2}{3}ECAE​=32​, the same as ADDB\dfrac{AD}{DB}DBAD​ ✔.

  6. Answer EC=3EC = 3EC=3 cm

    Common trap: Writing ADDB=ECAE\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{EC}{AE}DBAD​=AEEC​ (pieces from the wrong ends), which gives EC=43EC = \dfrac{4}{3}EC=34​ cm. Both ratios must go "vertex piece ÷ base piece".

Worked example 3

Using the part-to-whole form

Question. In △ABC, DE∥BCDE \parallel BCDE∥BC with D on AB and E on AC. AD=4AD = 4AD=4 cm, AB=10AB = 10AB=10 cm and AC=15AC = 15AC=15 cm. Find AE and EC.

Given: DE∥BCDE \parallel BCDE∥BC, AD=4AD = 4AD=4, AB=10AB = 10AB=10, AC=15AC = 15AC=15 (cm). Asked: AE and EC.

Concept used: BPT in the form ADAB=AEAC\dfrac{AD}{AB} = \dfrac{AE}{AC}ABAD​=ACAE​. Why it applies: we know a whole side (AB), so part-to-whole on both sides is quickest.

  1. 1

    Write the part-to-whole form.

    ADAB=AEAC\dfrac{AD}{AB} = \dfrac{AE}{AC}ABAD​=ACAE​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Substitute.

    410=AE15\dfrac{4}{10} = \dfrac{AE}{15}104​=15AE​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Multiply both sides by 15.

    AE=4×1510=6 cmAE = \dfrac{4 \times 15}{10} = 6 \text{ cm}AE=104×15​=6 cmSwipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    EC=AC−AE=15−6=9EC = AC - AE = 15 - 6 = 9EC=AC−AE=15−6=9 cm.

  5. Check: DB=10−4=6DB = 10 - 4 = 6DB=10−4=6; ADDB=46=23\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{4}{6} = \dfrac{2}{3}DBAD​=64​=32​ and AEEC=69=23\dfrac{AE}{EC} = \dfrac{6}{9} = \dfrac{2}{3}ECAE​=96​=32​ ✔.

  6. Answer AE=6AE = 6AE=6 cm, EC=9EC = 9EC=9 cm

    Common trap: Mixing forms: ADDB=AEAC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{AC}DBAD​=ACAE​, i.e. 46=AE15\dfrac{4}{6} = \dfrac{AE}{15}64​=15AE​, gives AE=10AE = 10AE=10 cm — and then EC would be only 5 cm. One side part-to-part, the other part-to-whole, is always wrong.

Worked example 4

Finding x

Question. In △ABC, DE∥BCDE \parallel BCDE∥BC with D on AB and E on AC. AD=x+1AD = x + 1AD=x+1, DB=3x−1DB = 3x - 1DB=3x−1, AE=x+3AE = x + 3AE=x+3 and EC=3x+4EC = 3x + 4EC=3x+4 (all in cm). Find x and the four lengths.

Given: DE∥BCDE \parallel BCDE∥BC and the four lengths in x. Asked: x.

Concept used: BPT, then cross-multiplication (Topic 1). Why it applies: the four pieces of the two sides are all in terms of x.

  1. 1

    Write BPT and substitute.

    x+13x−1=x+33x+4\dfrac{x + 1}{3x - 1} = \dfrac{x + 3}{3x + 4}3x−1x+1​=3x+4x+3​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Cross-multiply.

    (x+1)(3x+4)=(x+3)(3x−1)(x + 1)(3x + 4) = (x + 3)(3x - 1)(x+1)(3x+4)=(x+3)(3x−1)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Expand the left side.

    3x2+7x+4=(x+3)(3x−1)3x^2 + 7x + 4 = (x + 3)(3x - 1)3x2+7x+4=(x+3)(3x−1)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Expand the right side.

    3x2+7x+4=3x2+8x−33x^2 + 7x + 4 = 3x^2 + 8x - 33x2+7x+4=3x2+8x−3Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Subtract 3x2+7x3x^2 + 7x3x2+7x from both sides.

    4=x−34 = x - 34=x−3Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    Add 3 to both sides.

    x=7x = 7x=7Swipe to see the whole equation →
  7. 7

    Lengths: AD=8AD = 8AD=8 cm, DB=20DB = 20DB=20 cm, AE=10AE = 10AE=10 cm, EC=25EC = 25EC=25 cm.

  8. Check: 820=25\dfrac{8}{20} = \dfrac{2}{5}208​=52​ and 1025=25\dfrac{10}{25} = \dfrac{2}{5}2510​=52​ ✔; every length is positive ✔.

  9. Answer x=7x = 7x=7; AD=8AD = 8AD=8 cm, DB=20DB = 20DB=20 cm, AE=10AE = 10AE=10 cm, EC=25EC = 25EC=25 cm

    Common trap: Stopping at x. Check that every length is positive — a value of x that makes a length 0 or negative must be rejected.

Worked example 5

Is the line parallel?

Question. In △XYZ, M is on XY and N is on XZ. Is MN∥YZMN \parallel YZMN∥YZ when (a) XM=2.8XM = 2.8XM=2.8 cm, MY=4.2MY = 4.2MY=4.2 cm, XN=3.6XN = 3.6XN=3.6 cm, NZ=5.4NZ = 5.4NZ=5.4 cm (b) XM=3XM = 3XM=3 cm, MY=5MY = 5MY=5 cm, XN=4XN = 4XN=4 cm, NZ=6NZ = 6NZ=6 cm?

Given: four pieces in each case. Asked: parallel or not.

Concept used: the converse of BPT (equal ratios → parallel); and BPT itself (parallel → equal ratios), so unequal ratios → not parallel. Why it applies: we can compute both ratios.

  1. 1

    (a)

    XMMY=2.84.2=23\dfrac{XM}{MY} = \dfrac{2.8}{4.2} = \dfrac{2}{3}MYXM​=4.22.8​=32​.

  2. 2

    XNNZ=3.65.4=23\dfrac{XN}{NZ} = \dfrac{3.6}{5.4} = \dfrac{2}{3}NZXN​=5.43.6​=32​.

  3. 3

    The ratios are equal, so by the converse of BPT, MN∥YZMN \parallel YZMN∥YZ.

  4. 4

    (b)

    XMMY=35\dfrac{XM}{MY} = \dfrac{3}{5}MYXM​=53​ and XNNZ=46=23\dfrac{XN}{NZ} = \dfrac{4}{6} = \dfrac{2}{3}NZXN​=64​=32​.

  5. 5

    35≠23\dfrac{3}{5} \neq \dfrac{2}{3}53​=32​ (cross-multiply: 9≠109 \neq 109=10). If MN were parallel to YZ, BPT would make the ratios equal — so MN is not parallel to YZ.

  6. Check: (a) part-to-whole: 2.87=3.69=0.4\dfrac{2.8}{7} = \dfrac{3.6}{9} = 0.472.8​=93.6​=0.4 ✔.

  7. Answer (a) Yes, MN∥YZMN \parallel YZMN∥YZ (b) No

    Common trap: In (b), answering "yes" because the numbers are close, or because MN "looks parallel" in a sketch. Only equal ratios prove it.

Worked example 6

A trapezium: prove, then find

Question. ABCD is a trapezium with AB∥DCAB \parallel DCAB∥DC. A line parallel to AB meets AD at P and BC at Q. (a) Prove that APPD=BQQC\dfrac{AP}{PD} = \dfrac{BQ}{QC}PDAP​=QCBQ​. (b) Find BQ if AP=3AP = 3AP=3 cm, PD=4.5PD = 4.5PD=4.5 cm and BC=10BC = 10BC=10 cm.

Given: AB∥DCAB \parallel DCAB∥DC, PQ∥ABPQ \parallel ABPQ∥AB. To prove: APPD=BQQC\dfrac{AP}{PD} = \dfrac{BQ}{QC}PDAP​=QCBQ​; then find BQ.

  1. Construction: Join the diagonal AC; let it meet PQ at O.

    Concept used: BPT, used twice — once in △ADC and once in △CAB. Why it applies: BPT needs a triangle; the diagonal AC cuts the trapezium into two triangles, each with a side parallel to PQ.

    Statement Reason
    1. PQ∥DCPQ \parallel DCPQ∥DC PQ∥ABPQ \parallel ABPQ∥AB and AB∥DCAB \parallel DCAB∥DC; lines parallel to the same line are parallel to each other
    2. In △ADC, PO∥DCPO \parallel DCPO∥DC PO is part of PQ (step 1)
    3. APPD=AOOC\dfrac{AP}{PD} = \dfrac{AO}{OC}PDAP​=OCAO​ … (1) BPT in △ADC
    4. In △CAB, OQ∥ABOQ \parallel ABOQ∥AB OQ is part of PQ (given)
    5. COOA=CQQB\dfrac{CO}{OA} = \dfrac{CQ}{QB}OACO​=QBCQ​, so AOOC=BQQC\dfrac{AO}{OC} = \dfrac{BQ}{QC}OCAO​=QCBQ​ … (2) BPT in △CAB; then turn both fractions upside down
    6. APPD=BQQC\dfrac{AP}{PD} = \dfrac{BQ}{QC}PDAP​=QCBQ​ From (1) and (2). Hence proved.
  2. 7

    (b)

    APPD=34.5=23\dfrac{AP}{PD} = \dfrac{3}{4.5} = \dfrac{2}{3}PDAP​=4.53​=32​, so BQ : QC = 2 : 3.

  3. 8

    BC has 2+3=52 + 3 = 52+3=5 parts; one part is 10÷5=210 \div 5 = 210÷5=2 cm.

  4. 9

    BQ=2×2=4BQ = 2 \times 2 = 4BQ=2×2=4 cm (and QC=6QC = 6QC=6 cm).

  5. Check: BQQC=46=23=APPD\dfrac{BQ}{QC} = \dfrac{4}{6} = \dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{AP}{PD}QCBQ​=64​=32​=PDAP​ ✔.

  6. Answer (a) proved (b) BQ=4BQ = 4BQ=4 cm

    Common trap: Using BPT in the trapezium directly ("PQ∥ABPQ \parallel ABPQ∥AB, so APPD=BQQC\dfrac{AP}{PD} = \dfrac{BQ}{QC}PDAP​=QCBQ​ by BPT"). A trapezium is not a triangle — the diagonal is the step that earns the marks.

Quick check · 4 of 4MCQ1 mark

In the proof of BPT, the step "ar(BDE) = ar(DEC)" earns its mark only with the reason:

Half the reason: two triangles on one base can have very different heights.
Half the reason: between the same parallels but on different bases, the areas can differ.
Same base and same parallels together give the same height, so the same area.
Not true in general, and not what the areas depend on.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers name the BPT proof and ratio set-up among the four commonest reasons for lost marks in this chapter.

  • No construction written (or no figure)

    The schemes give marks for the figure, given, to prove and construction.

    Draw the figure; write "Construction: join BE and CD; draw DM⊥ACDM \perp ACDM⊥AC, EN⊥ABEN \perp ABEN⊥AB".

  • Steps with no reasons

    Each area line needs "12×\dfrac{1}{2} \times21​× base ×\times× height" and the height named.

    Write the reason beside every line.

  • "ar(BDE) = ar(DEC)" with no reason, or "same base" only

    Same base alone does not fix the height.

    "Same base DE and between the same parallels DE and BC."

  • ADDB=AEAC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{AC}DBAD​=ACAE​

    Part-to-part on one side, part-to-whole on the other.

    ADDB=AEEC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}DBAD​=ECAE​ or ADAB=AEAC\dfrac{AD}{AB} = \dfrac{AE}{AC}ABAD​=ACAE​ — the same kind on both sides.

  • ADDB=ECAE\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{EC}{AE}DBAD​=AEEC​

    The second ratio is upside down.

    Both ratios: vertex piece on top.

  • Saying "DE ∥ BC" because it looks parallel

    A sketch proves nothing.

    Compare the ratios; quote the converse of BPT.

  • BPT used straight on a trapezium

    BPT is about a triangle.

    Draw a diagonal and use BPT in each triangle.

Key points

  • BPT: in △ABC, if DE∥BCDE \parallel BCDE∥BC (D on AB, E on AC, distinct points), then ADDB=AEEC\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}DBAD​=ECAE​ — also ADAB=AEAC\dfrac{AD}{AB} = \dfrac{AE}{AC}ABAD​=ACAE​.
  • Proof: join BE, CD; draw DM⊥ACDM \perp ACDM⊥AC, EN⊥ABEN \perp ABEN⊥AB; two area ratios; ar(BDE) = ar(DEC) — same base DE, between the same parallels DE and BC.
  • Converse: if a line divides two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, it is parallel to the third side. Unequal ratios → not parallel.
  • Never mix part-to-part with part-to-whole.
  • In a trapezium, draw a diagonal and use BPT twice.
