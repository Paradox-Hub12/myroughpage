Topic 2 of 5

Similar figures

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Define similar figures and similar polygons (corresponding angles equal and corresponding sides proportional — both needed); explain that congruent figures are similar but not conversely.
  • Give examples and counter-examples of similar figures.
  • Find the scale factor and missing sides of similar polygons.

Open a map app and pinch to zoom: the roads get longer, but every bend keeps its angle and the city keeps its shape. If the app stretched only the width and not the height, the city would look squashed — no longer the "same shape". What exactly must stay the same for two figures to have the same shape? This topic gives the precise answer.

Same shape, any size

Two figures are similar if one is an exact enlargement (or reduction) of the other: the shape is kept, while the size may change. All circles are similar; all squares are similar; all equilateral triangles are similar.

Congruent figures match in shape and in size. So every pair of congruent figures is similar (the "enlargement" has factor 1) — but similar figures need not be congruent.

Similar polygons: two conditions

Two polygons with an equal number of sides are similar when

  1. their corresponding angles are equal, and
  2. each side of one, divided by the matching side of the other, gives one and the same ratio (the sides are proportional).

That common ratio is the scale factor. For a quadrilateral ABCD similar to PQRS, the scale factor from ABCD to PQRS is

k=PQAB=QRBC=RSCD=SPDAk = \dfrac{PQ}{AB} = \dfrac{QR}{BC} = \dfrac{RS}{CD} = \dfrac{SP}{DA}k=ABPQ​=BCQR​=CDRS​=DASP​Swipe to see the whole equation →

If k>1k > 1k>1 the second figure is an enlargement; if k<1k < 1k<1, a reduction; if k=1k = 1k=1, the figures are congruent.

For polygons, one condition alone is not enough:

  • A square and a (non-square) rectangle have all angles equal (90°), but their sides are not in one ratio — not similar.
  • A square and a rhombus whose angles are not 90° have all sides in one ratio, but their angles are not equal — not similar.

(Topic 4 shows that triangles are special: for triangles, either condition brings the other with it.)

Writing similarity in the right order

"ABCD ~ PQRS" (read "is similar to") is a matching statement: A matches P, B matches Q, C matches R, D matches S. The order tells you which angles are equal (∠A=∠P\angle A = \angle P∠A=∠P, …) and which sides make the ratios (ABPQ\dfrac{AB}{PQ}PQAB​, …). Changing the order of one name changes the meaning.

Quick reminder (Class 9). Congruent triangles are written in matching order too: △ABC ≅ △PQR means AB=PQAB = PQAB=PQ, ∠A=∠P\angle A = \angle P∠A=∠P, and so on.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

A square and a rectangle

Question. Is a square of side 4 cm similar to a rectangle measuring 4 cm by 6 cm? Give a reason.

Given: square 4, 4, 4, 4 (cm), all angles 90°; rectangle 4, 6, 4, 6 (cm), all angles 90°. Asked: similar or not.

Concept used: similar polygons need equal angles and proportional sides. Why it applies: we can test both conditions.

  1. 1

    Angles: every angle of both figures is 90°, so condition (i) holds.

  2. 2

    Sides: match the square's sides in order with 4, 6, 4, 6. The ratios are 44=1\dfrac{4}{4} = 144​=1 and 64=32\dfrac{6}{4} = \dfrac{3}{2}46​=23​.

  3. 3

    The ratios are not all equal, so condition (ii) fails (and any other matching also pairs 4 with both 4 and 6).

  4. Check: if they were similar, the rectangle's sides would all be k times 4 — all equal — but 4 ≠ 6 ✔.

  5. Answer Not similar — the angles are equal but the sides are not in one ratio.

    Common trap: "All angles are 90°, so they are similar." Equal angles alone are not enough for quadrilaterals.

Worked example 2

A square and a rhombus

Question. A square has side 5 cm. A rhombus has side 7.5 cm and one angle of 60°. Are they similar?

Given: square 5 cm (angles 90°); rhombus 7.5 cm (angles 60°, 120°, 60°, 120°). Asked: similar or not.

Concept used: both conditions for similar polygons. Why it applies: the sides look proportional, so the angles decide.

  1. 1

    Sides: every ratio is 7.55=1.5\dfrac{7.5}{5} = 1.557.5​=1.5, so the sides are in one ratio.

  2. 2

    Angles: the square's angles are 90°; the rhombus has 60° and 120°. No matching makes them equal.

  3. 3

    Condition (i) fails.

  4. Check: a rhombus with a 60° angle is "pushed over"; no enlargement of a square can tilt its corners ✔.

  5. Answer Not similar — the sides are in one ratio, but the angles are not equal.

    Common trap: Stopping after the sides. For polygons with four or more sides, check the angles too.

Worked example 3

Always, sometimes or never similar?

Question. Say whether each pair is always similar, and give a reason or a counter-example: (a) two rectangles, one 6 cm × 4 cm and the other 9 cm × 6 cm (b) any two equilateral triangles (c) any two isosceles triangles.

Given: three pairs. Asked: similar or not, with a reason.

Concept used: both conditions; one counter-example is enough to show "not always". Why it applies: (b) and (c) are claims about all pairs.

  1. 1

    (a)

    All angles are 90° in both.

  2. 2

    Sides: 96=1.5\dfrac{9}{6} = 1.569​=1.5 and 64=1.5\dfrac{6}{4} = 1.546​=1.5 — one ratio. So they are similar, scale factor 1.5.

  3. 3

    (b)

    Every angle of an equilateral triangle is 60°, so the angles always match.

  4. 4

    Sides a, a, a and b, b, b: every ratio is ba\dfrac{b}{a}ab​. So they are always similar.

  5. 5

    (c)

    Counter-example: sides 5, 5, 6 and sides 5, 5, 8 (both isosceles).

  6. 6

    The equal sides give 55=1\dfrac{5}{5} = 155​=1 but the bases give 86=43\dfrac{8}{6} = \dfrac{4}{3}68​=34​. The sides are not in one ratio, so these two are not similar.

  7. Check: (c) both triangles exist: 5+5>65 + 5 > 65+5>6 and 5+5>85 + 5 > 85+5>8 ✔.

  8. Answer (a) similar (scale factor 1.5) (b) always similar (c) not always — 5, 5, 6 and 5, 5, 8 are not similar.

    Common trap: In (a), pairing 9 with 4 and 6 with 6 (94≠1\dfrac{9}{4} \neq 149​=1) and saying "not similar". Pair long side with long side, short with short.

Worked example 4

Missing sides of similar quadrilaterals

Question. Quadrilateral ABCD ~ quadrilateral PQRS. AB=4AB = 4AB=4 cm, BC=6BC = 6BC=6 cm, CD=5CD = 5CD=5 cm, DA=3DA = 3DA=3 cm, ∠B=90°\angle B = 90°∠B=90° and PQ=6PQ = 6PQ=6 cm. Find the scale factor, the other sides of PQRS and ∠Q\angle Q∠Q.

Given: ABCD ~ PQRS and the measurements above. Asked: k, QR, RS, SP, ∠Q\angle Q∠Q.

Concept used: in similar polygons, corresponding sides are in one ratio and corresponding angles are equal. Why it applies: the order ABCD ~ PQRS tells us which side matches which.

  1. 1

    Read the matching: A ↔ P, B ↔ Q, C ↔ R, D ↔ S. So PQ matches AB.

  2. 2

    Scale factor.

    k=PQAB=64=1.5k = \dfrac{PQ}{AB} = \dfrac{6}{4} = 1.5k=ABPQ​=46​=1.5Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    QR=k×BC=1.5×6=9QR = k \times BC = 1.5 \times 6 = 9QR=k×BC=1.5×6=9 cm.

  4. 4

    RS=k×CD=1.5×5=7.5RS = k \times CD = 1.5 \times 5 = 7.5RS=k×CD=1.5×5=7.5 cm.

  5. 5

    SP=k×DA=1.5×3=4.5SP = k \times DA = 1.5 \times 3 = 4.5SP=k×DA=1.5×3=4.5 cm.

  6. 6

    ∠Q=∠B=90°\angle Q = \angle B = 90°∠Q=∠B=90° (corresponding angles of similar polygons).

  7. Check: 96=7.55=4.53=1.5\dfrac{9}{6} = \dfrac{7.5}{5} = \dfrac{4.5}{3} = 1.569​=57.5​=34.5​=1.5 ✔.

  8. Answer k=1.5k = 1.5k=1.5; QR=9QR = 9QR=9 cm, RS=7.5RS = 7.5RS=7.5 cm, SP=4.5SP = 4.5SP=4.5 cm; ∠Q=90°\angle Q = 90°∠Q=90°

    Common trap: Using 46\dfrac{4}{6}64​ as the multiplier for PQRS. That is the scale factor from PQRS back to ABCD; it would make PQRS smaller, but PQ=6PQ = 6PQ=6 is longer than AB=4AB = 4AB=4.

Worked example 5

Enlarging a photograph

Question. A photograph 10 cm wide and 15 cm tall is enlarged, without changing its shape, so that its height becomes 24 cm. Find the scale factor and the new width.

Given: old 10 cm × 15 cm; new height 24 cm. Asked: k and the new width.

Concept used: an enlargement "without changing shape" makes a similar rectangle. Why it applies: every length is multiplied by the same k.

  1. 1

    Heights match heights.

    k=2415=85=1.6k = \dfrac{24}{15} = \dfrac{8}{5} = 1.6k=1524​=58​=1.6Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    New width.

    1.6×10=16 cm1.6 \times 10 = 16 \text{ cm}1.6×10=16 cmSwipe to see the whole equation →

  3. Check: 1610=2415=1.6\dfrac{16}{10} = \dfrac{24}{15} = 1.61016​=1524​=1.6 ✔.

  4. Answer k=1.6k = 1.6k=1.6; the new width is 16 cm.

    Common trap: Adding instead of multiplying: "the height grew by 9 cm, so the width grows by 9 cm too" gives 19 cm, and 1910≠2415\dfrac{19}{10} \neq \dfrac{24}{15}1019​=1524​ — the shape changes.

Quick check · 3 of 3True/False with justification1 mark

True or false? "Two quadrilaterals are similar if their corresponding angles are equal." Justify.

Answer: False. A 2 cm square and a 2 cm × 3 cm rectangle have all angles 90°, but their sides are not in one ratio (22≠32\dfrac{2}{2} \neq \dfrac{3}{2}22​=23​), so they are not similar.

For quadrilaterals both conditions are needed; one counter-example is enough to show "false".

Common mistakes!

  • "All angles are equal, so the quadrilaterals are similar"

    A square and a rectangle break this.

    Check angles and side ratios for polygons.

  • "All sides are in one ratio, so they are similar"

    A square and a 60° rhombus break this.

    Check the angles too.

  • "Similar means congruent" (or the other way round)

    Similar figures may differ in size.

    Congruent = similar with scale factor 1.

  • Writing ABCD ~ PQRS but pairing AB with QR

    The order of letters fixes the matching.

    A ↔ P, B ↔ Q, …: AB ↔ PQ, BC ↔ QR.

  • Using oldnew\dfrac{\text{old}}{\text{new}}newold​ to enlarge

    That ratio shrinks.

    k=newoldk = \dfrac{\text{new}}{\text{old}}k=oldnew​ for "old → new".

Key points

  • Similar = same shape, not necessarily the same size; congruent figures are similar (k = 1), not conversely.
  • Two polygons with the same number of sides are similar when corresponding angles are equal and corresponding sides are proportional.
  • Square vs rectangle (angles only) and square vs rhombus (sides only) show that one condition is not enough for polygons.
  • The scale factor is the common ratio of corresponding sides; multiply by it to find missing sides.
  • The order of the letters in "ABCD ~ PQRS" fixes which parts match.
