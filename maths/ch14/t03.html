Topic 3 of 5

The rules: 0 to 1, complements and the words of an event

About 45 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Use 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1, P(impossible event) = 0 and P(sure event) = 1, and decide whether a number can be a probability.
  • Use "the probabilities of all the elementary events add to 1" and P(not E) = 1 − P(E).
  • Read "at least", "at most", "less than", "more than" and "not" correctly, and work back from a probability to a count or an unknown.

A weather app says "chance of rain: 37%". What is the chance of no rain? You do not need to know anything about clouds: it is 100%−37%=63%100\% - 37\% = 63\%100%−37%=63%. This topic gives you the few rules that every probability obeys — and the exact meaning of words like "at least" and "at most", where many marks are lost.

  1. Every probability is between 0 and 1

The favourable outcomes are some of all the outcomes, so the numerator is never more than the denominator:

0≤P(E)≤10 \le P(E) \le 10≤P(E)≤1Swipe to see the whole equation →
  • An event that cannot happen (getting 7 on a die) has no favourable outcome: P=0P = 0P=0. It is an impossible event.
  • An event that must happen (getting a number less than 7 on a die) has every outcome favourable: P=1P = 1P=1. It is a sure (certain) event.

A probability can be written as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage: 14=0.25=25%\dfrac{1}{4} = 0.25 = 25\%41​=0.25=25%. A negative number, or a number greater than 1 (or greater than 100%), cannot be a probability.

  1. Elementary events add to 1

An event with only one outcome is an elementary event (for example "the die shows 5"). The probabilities of all the elementary events of an experiment add up to 1 — together they are every outcome.

  1. The complement: "not E"

The event not E, written Eˉ\bar{E}Eˉ, happens exactly when E does not. Every outcome is in E or in Eˉ\bar{E}Eˉ, never both. So

P(E)+P(Eˉ)=1,P(Eˉ)=1−P(E)P(E) + P(\bar{E}) = 1, \qquad P(\bar{E}) = 1 - P(E)P(E)+P(Eˉ)=1,P(Eˉ)=1−P(E)Swipe to see the whole equation →

E and Eˉ\bar{E}Eˉ are complementary events. Use this when "not E" is easier to count, or when only P(E)P(E)P(E) is given.

  1. The words of an event

On a spinner numbered 1 to 10:

Words Means Numbers
at least 7 7 or more 7, 8, 9, 10
more than 7 bigger than 7 (7 not included) 8, 9, 10
at most 7 7 or less 1, 2, …, 7
less than 7 smaller than 7 (7 not included) 1, 2, …, 6
not 7 every number except 7 1–6, 8–10

"At least 7" and "less than 7" are complements; so are "at most 7" and "more than 7".

  1. Working back from a probability

P(E)=favourableallP(E) = \dfrac{\text{favourable}}{\text{all}}P(E)=allfavourable​, so favourable = P(E) × all. If a box of 60 sweets has P(mango)=0.35P(\text{mango}) = 0.35P(mango)=0.35, there are 0.35×60=210.35 \times 60 = 210.35×60=21 mango sweets. If a number is unknown, write the probability with the unknown and solve the equation.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Can it be a probability?

Question. Which of these can be the probability of an event? 75\dfrac{7}{5}57​, −0.2, 0, 112%, 0.999, 1. Give a reason for each.

Given: six numbers. Asked: which can be probabilities.

Concept used: 0≤P(E)≤10 \le P(E) \le 10≤P(E)≤1. Why it applies: favourable outcomes can never be more than all outcomes, and a count cannot be negative.

  1. 1

    75=1.4>1\dfrac{7}{5} = 1.4 > 157​=1.4>1 → no.

  2. 2

    −0.2<0-0.2 < 0−0.2<0 → no.

  3. 3

    0 → yes (an impossible event).

  4. 4

    112%=112100=1.12>1112\% = \dfrac{112}{100} = 1.12 > 1112%=100112​=1.12>1 → no.

  5. 5

    0.999 → yes (between 0 and 1; a very likely event).

  6. 6

    1 → yes (a sure event).

  7. Check: each "yes" can be written as favourableall\dfrac{\text{favourable}}{\text{all}}allfavourable​ — for example 0.999=99910000.999 = \dfrac{999}{1000}0.999=1000999​ (999 winning tickets out of 1000) — and each "no" cannot ✔.

  8. Answer 0, 0.999 and 1 can be probabilities; 75\dfrac{7}{5}57​, −0.2 and 112% cannot.

    Common trap: Rejecting 0 and 1. They are probabilities — of an impossible and a sure event.

Worked example 2

The complement

Question. The probability that it will rain tomorrow is 0.37. What is the probability that it will not rain tomorrow?

Given: P(rain)=0.37P(\text{rain}) = 0.37P(rain)=0.37. Asked: P(no rain)P(\text{no rain})P(no rain).

Concept used: P(Eˉ)=1−P(E)P(\bar{E}) = 1 - P(E)P(Eˉ)=1−P(E). Why it applies: "rain" and "no rain" are complementary — exactly one of them happens.

  1. 1

    P(no rain)=1−0.37=0.63P(\text{no rain}) = 1 - 0.37 = 0.63P(no rain)=1−0.37=0.63.

  2. Check: 0.37+0.63=10.37 + 0.63 = 10.37+0.63=1 ✔.

  3. Answer 0.63

    Common trap: Writing 1−0.37=0.731 - 0.37 = 0.731−0.37=0.73 — subtract carefully: 1.00−0.37=0.631.00 - 0.37 = 0.631.00−0.37=0.63.

Worked example 3

Elementary events add to 1

Question. A pencil box has 12 pencils of the same shape: 5 HB, 4 2B and 3 4B. One pencil is taken at random. Find the probability of each type and show that they add to 1.

Given: 5 + 4 + 3 pencils. Asked: three probabilities and their sum.

Concept used: P=favourableallP = \dfrac{\text{favourable}}{\text{all}}P=allfavourable​; every pencil is one of the three types. Why it applies: the three events cover every outcome once.

  1. 1

    All outcomes: 5+4+3=125 + 4 + 3 = 125+4+3=12.

  2. 2

    P(HB)=512P(\text{HB}) = \dfrac{5}{12}P(HB)=125​, P(2B)=412=13P(\text{2B}) = \dfrac{4}{12} = \dfrac{1}{3}P(2B)=124​=31​, P(4B)=312=14P(\text{4B}) = \dfrac{3}{12} = \dfrac{1}{4}P(4B)=123​=41​.

  3. 3

    Sum: 512+412+312=1212=1\dfrac{5}{12} + \dfrac{4}{12} + \dfrac{3}{12} = \dfrac{12}{12} = 1125​+124​+123​=1212​=1.

  4. Check: with the simplified fractions: 512+13+14=5+4+312=1\dfrac{5}{12} + \dfrac{1}{3} + \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{5 + 4 + 3}{12} = 1125​+31​+41​=125+4+3​=1 ✔.

  5. Answer 512\dfrac{5}{12}125​, 13\dfrac{1}{3}31​, 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​; their sum is 1.

    Common trap: Adding 512+13+14\dfrac{5}{12} + \dfrac{1}{3} + \dfrac{1}{4}125​+31​+41​ as 5+1+112+3+4=719\dfrac{5 + 1 + 1}{12 + 3 + 4} = \dfrac{7}{19}12+3+45+1+1​=197​. Fractions are added over a common denominator.

Worked example 4

At least, at most, less than, more than

Question. A spinner has 10 equal sectors numbered 1 to 10. Find the probability that the arrow stops on a number (a) at least 7, (b) at most 7, (c) less than 7, (d) more than 7.

Given: 10 equally likely numbers. Asked: four probabilities.

Concept used: read each phrase as a list (visual above). Why it applies: "at least" and "at most" include 7; "less than" and "more than" do not.

  1. 1

    (a) At least 7: 7, 8, 9, 10 → 410=25\dfrac{4}{10} = \dfrac{2}{5}104​=52​.

  2. 2

    (b) At most 7: 1 to 7 → 710\dfrac{7}{10}107​.

  3. 3

    (c) Less than 7: 1 to 6 → 610=35\dfrac{6}{10} = \dfrac{3}{5}106​=53​.

  4. 4

    (d) More than 7: 8, 9, 10 → 310\dfrac{3}{10}103​.

  5. Check: complements add to 1: (a) + (c) =25+35=1= \dfrac{2}{5} + \dfrac{3}{5} = 1=52​+53​=1 and (b) + (d) =710+310=1= \dfrac{7}{10} + \dfrac{3}{10} = 1=107​+103​=1 ✔.

  6. Answer (a) 25\dfrac{2}{5}52​ (b) 710\dfrac{7}{10}107​ (c) 35\dfrac{3}{5}53​ (d) 310\dfrac{3}{10}103​

    Common trap: Treating "at least 7" as "more than 7" (giving 310\dfrac{3}{10}103​), or "at most 7" as "less than 7" (giving 35\dfrac{3}{5}53​).

Worked example 5

From a probability to a number

Question. A jar has 60 sweets. A sweet is taken at random; the probability that it is a mango sweet is 0.35. How many mango sweets are in the jar, and what is the probability that the sweet is not mango?

Given: 60 sweets; P(mango)=0.35P(\text{mango}) = 0.35P(mango)=0.35. Asked: the number of mango sweets; P(not mango)P(\text{not mango})P(not mango).

Concept used: favourable = P × all; P(Eˉ)=1−P(E)P(\bar{E}) = 1 - P(E)P(Eˉ)=1−P(E). Why it applies: P=mango sweets60P = \dfrac{\text{mango sweets}}{60}P=60mango sweets​.

  1. 1

    Mango sweets =0.35×60=21= 0.35 \times 60 = 21=0.35×60=21.

  2. 2

    P(not mango)=1−0.35=0.65P(\text{not mango}) = 1 - 0.35 = 0.65P(not mango)=1−0.35=0.65.

  3. Check: the other sweets are 60−21=3960 - 21 = 3960−21=39, and 3960=0.65\dfrac{39}{60} = 0.656039​=0.65 ✔.

  4. Answer 21 mango sweets; P(not mango)=0.65P(\text{not mango}) = 0.65P(not mango)=0.65.

    Common trap: Dividing instead of multiplying: 60÷0.35≈17160 \div 0.35 \approx 17160÷0.35≈171 — more sweets than the jar holds, a sign of the wrong operation.

Worked example 6

An unknown in a probability

Question. A bag has 7 white counters and some black counters. A counter is drawn at random. The probability that it is black is 34\dfrac{3}{4}43​. How many black counters are there?

Given: 7 white, x black; P(black)=34P(\text{black}) = \dfrac{3}{4}P(black)=43​. Asked: x.

Concept used: P(black)=x7+xP(\text{black}) = \dfrac{x}{7 + x}P(black)=7+xx​. Why it applies: the total is 7+x7 + x7+x counters.

  1. 1

    x7+x=34\dfrac{x}{7 + x} = \dfrac{3}{4}7+xx​=43​.

  2. 2

    Cross-multiply: 4x=3(7+x)=21+3x4x = 3(7 + x) = 21 + 3x4x=3(7+x)=21+3x.

  3. 3

    x=21x = 21x=21.

  4. Check: with 21 black counters the total is 28, and P(black)=2128=34P(\text{black}) = \dfrac{21}{28} = \dfrac{3}{4}P(black)=2821​=43​ ✔.

  5. Answer 21 black counters.

    Common trap: Writing x7=34\dfrac{x}{7} = \dfrac{3}{4}7x​=43​ (black against white). The denominator must be the total 7+x7 + x7+x.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers say that misreading "at least" and "at most", using 1 − P for the wrong event and accepting a probability greater than 1 are among the most common reasons for lost marks in this topic.

  • "At least 7" counted as 8, 9, 10

    "At least" includes 7.

    At least = that number or more.

  • "At most 7" counted as 1 to 6

    "At most" includes 7.

    At most = that number or less.

  • P(not E)P(\text{not } E)P(not E) written as P(E)P(E)P(E)

    The question asked for the opposite event.

    P(Eˉ)=1−P(E)P(\bar{E}) = 1 - P(E)P(Eˉ)=1−P(E).

  • An answer greater than 1 (or negative) accepted

    0≤P(E)≤10 \le P(E) \le 10≤P(E)≤1 always.

    Recount; the numerator can never exceed the denominator.

  • 37% used as 37 in 1−P1 - P1−P

    37% =0.37= 0.37=0.37.

    Change the percentage to a decimal or fraction first.

  • Favourable =all÷P= \text{all} \div P=all÷P

    P=favourableallP = \dfrac{\text{favourable}}{\text{all}}P=allfavourable​.

    Favourable =P×all= P \times \text{all}=P×all.

Key points

  • 0≤P(E)≤10 \le P(E) \le 10≤P(E)≤1; impossible event 0; sure event 1.
  • The probabilities of all the elementary events of an experiment add up to 1.
  • P(Eˉ)=1−P(E)P(\bar{E}) = 1 - P(E)P(Eˉ)=1−P(E) — E and not E are complementary.
  • At least = that number or more; at most = that number or less; less than / more than leave the number out.
  • Favourable outcomes =P(E)×= P(E) \times=P(E)× all outcomes; for an unknown, write PPP with the unknown and solve.
