Wrap-up

The recipe, the words of an event, sample-space sizes and mixed problems

About 30 minutes

Every question in this chapter follows the same three moves: count all the equally likely outcomes, count the favourable ones after reading the words of the event exactly, and write the fraction in lowest terms (and check that it lies between 0 and 1). This page puts three cards on one sheet and then gives four mixed problems in the styles of the sample papers — a fair-day spinner case, a deck with cards removed, numbered cards with an "either … or" event, and a three-coin game.

The probability recipe card

Mixed worked examples

Mixed example 1

Case study: the fair-day spinner

Question. At a school fair, a game uses a spinner with 12 equal sectors numbered 1 to 12. A player spins once. (i) Find the probability that the arrow stops on a prime number. (1 mark) (ii) Find the probability that it stops on a multiple of 4. (1 mark) (iii) (A) Find the probability that it stops on a number that is not a factor of 12. (2 marks) OR (iii) (B) A player wins a prize if the number is at least 9. Find the probability of winning a prize. (2 marks)

Given: 12 equally likely numbers. Asked: four probabilities.

Concept used: count the favourable numbers in 1 to 12; the complement for "not". Why it applies: the sectors are equal.

  1. 1

    (i) Primes: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 → 512\dfrac{5}{12}125​. (1 is not a prime.)

  2. 2

    (ii) Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12 → 312=14\dfrac{3}{12} = \dfrac{1}{4}123​=41​.

  3. 3

    (iii) (A) Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 → 6; not factors: 12−6=612 - 6 = 612−6=6 → 612=12\dfrac{6}{12} = \dfrac{1}{2}126​=21​.

  4. 4

    (iii) (B) At least 9: 9, 10, 11, 12 → 412=13\dfrac{4}{12} = \dfrac{1}{3}124​=31​.

  5. Check: (iii) (A) list the numbers that are not factors: 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 — six numbers ✔. (iii) (B) "less than 9" is 1 to 8, and 812+412=1\dfrac{8}{12} + \dfrac{4}{12} = 1128​+124​=1 ✔.

  6. Answer (i) 512\dfrac{5}{12}125​ (ii) 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​ (iii) (A) 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​ (B) 13\dfrac{1}{3}31​

    Common trap: In (iii) (B), counting 10, 11, 12 only ("more than 9") and getting 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​.

Mixed example 2

A deck with cards removed

Question. From a deck of 52 cards, all 13 spades and the two red queens are removed. One card is drawn at random from the remaining cards. (Face cards are kings, queens and jacks; aces are not face cards.) Find the probability that it is (a) a face card, (b) a black card, (c) a queen.

Given: 15 cards removed. Asked: three probabilities.

Concept used: new total and new counts. Why it applies: the removed cards cannot be drawn.

  1. 1

    Total: 52−13−2=3752 - 13 - 2 = 3752−13−2=37.

  2. 2

    (a) Face cards in clubs, hearts and diamonds: 3×3=93 \times 3 = 93×3=9; the two red queens are gone: 9−2=79 - 2 = 79−2=7. P=737P = \dfrac{7}{37}P=377​.

  3. 3

    (b) Black cards left: the 13 clubs only. P=1337P = \dfrac{13}{37}P=3713​.

  4. 4

    (c) Queens left: only the queen of clubs. P=137P = \dfrac{1}{37}P=371​.

  5. Check: (b) the red cards left are 26−2=2426 - 2 = 2426−2=24, and 13+24=3713 + 24 = 3713+24=37 ✔.

  6. Answer (a) 737\dfrac{7}{37}377​ (b) 1337\dfrac{13}{37}3713​ (c) 137\dfrac{1}{37}371​

    Common trap: Removing the spades but forgetting the two queens (total 39), or forgetting that the spade face cards have gone (12 face cards).

Mixed example 3

Numbered cards: "either … or" and "neither … nor"

Question. Fifty cards numbered 11 to 60 are put in a box and one is drawn at random. Find the probability that the number on it is (a) a multiple of 5 or a perfect square, (b) neither a multiple of 5 nor a perfect square.

Given: the numbers 11 to 60. Asked: two probabilities.

Concept used: A + B − both; neither = complement. Why it applies: 25 is in both lists.

  1. 1

    All outcomes: 60−11+1=5060 - 11 + 1 = 5060−11+1=50.

  2. 2

    Multiples of 5: 15, 20, 25, …, 60 → 10. Perfect squares: 16, 25, 36, 49 → 4. Both: 25 → 1.

  3. 3

    (a) 10+4−1=1310 + 4 - 1 = 1310+4−1=13 → 1350\dfrac{13}{50}5013​.

  4. 4

    (b) 1−1350=37501 - \dfrac{13}{50} = \dfrac{37}{50}1−5013​=5037​.

  5. Check: (a) list them: 15, 16, 20, 25, 30, 35, 36, 40, 45, 49, 50, 55, 60 — 13 numbers ✔.

  6. Answer (a) 1350\dfrac{13}{50}5013​ (b) 3750\dfrac{37}{50}5037​

    Common trap: Taking the total as 60−11=4960 - 11 = 4960−11=49, or counting 25 twice (1450\dfrac{14}{50}5014​).

Mixed example 4

A three-coin game

Question. Ravi and Mona play a game: a coin is tossed three times. Ravi wins if there are exactly two heads. Mona wins if the first and the last tosses are both tails. Otherwise nobody wins. (a) Who is more likely to win? (b) What is the probability that nobody wins?

Given: three tosses. Asked: compare two probabilities; the probability of no winner.

Concept used: list the 8 outcomes and mark each player's. Why it applies: both events are sets of these 8 equally likely outcomes.

  1. 1

    Outcomes: HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT.

  2. 2

    Ravi (exactly two heads): HHT, HTH, THH → 38\dfrac{3}{8}83​.

  3. 3

    Mona (first and last tails): THT, TTT → 28=14\dfrac{2}{8} = \dfrac{1}{4}82​=41​.

  4. 4

    (a) 38>28\dfrac{3}{8} > \dfrac{2}{8}83​>82​, so Ravi is more likely to win.

  5. 5

    (b) No outcome is in both lists, so 3+2=53 + 2 = 53+2=5 outcomes give a winner; nobody wins in 8−5=38 - 5 = 38−5=3: HHH, HTT, TTH → 38\dfrac{3}{8}83​.

  6. Check: 38+28+38=1\dfrac{3}{8} + \dfrac{2}{8} + \dfrac{3}{8} = 183​+82​+83​=1 ✔.

  7. Answer (a) Ravi (38\dfrac{3}{8}83​ against 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​) (b) 38\dfrac{3}{8}83​

    Common trap: Comparing "two heads" with "two tails" as if each were one outcome — the lists show 3 outcomes against 2.
