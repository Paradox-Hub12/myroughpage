Exercise 14.1, Q1Complete the statements2 marks
  1. Complete the following statements: (i) Probability of an event E + Probability of the event 'not E' = …… . (ii) The probability of an event that cannot happen is …… . Such an event is called …… . (iii) The probability of an event that is certain to happen is …… . Such an event is called …… . (iv) The sum of the probabilities of all the elementary events of an experiment is …… . (v) The probability of an event is greater than or equal to …… and less than or equal to …… .

Given: five statements about probability. Asked: the missing words and numbers.

Concept used: the rules of probability (Topic 3). Why it applies: each blank is one of those rules.

  1. 1

    (i) E and not E together are every outcome, with no overlap: the sum is 1.

  2. 2

    (ii) No outcome is favourable: 0; an impossible event.

  3. 3

    (iii) Every outcome is favourable: 1; a sure (certain) event.

  4. 4

    (iv) The elementary events together are all the outcomes: 1.

  5. 5

    (v) 0 and 1.

  6. Check: with a die: P(a number less than 4) + P(not less than 4) =36+36=1= \dfrac{3}{6} + \dfrac{3}{6} = 1=63​+63​=1; P(7) =06=0= \dfrac{0}{6} = 0=60​=0; P(less than 7) =66=1= \dfrac{6}{6} = 1=66​=1 ✔.

  7. Answer (i) 1 (ii) 0, impossible event (iii) 1, sure (certain) event (iv) 1 (v) 0, 1

    Common trap: Writing "100" in (v) — a probability written as a fraction or decimal is at most 1 (100% only as a percentage).
Exercise 14.1, Q2Equally likely outcomes?2 marks
  1. Which of the following experiments have equally likely outcomes? Explain. (i) A driver attempts to start a car. The car starts or does not start. (ii) A player attempts to shoot a basketball. She/he shoots or misses the shot. (iii) A trial is made to answer a true-false question. The answer is right or wrong. (iv) A baby is born. It is a boy or a girl.

Given: four experiments with two outcomes each. Asked: which have equally likely outcomes, with reasons.

Concept used: outcomes are equally likely only when nothing makes one more likely than the other (Topic 2). Why it applies: two outcomes do not automatically mean equal chances.

  1. 1

    (i) Not equally likely — a car in good condition usually starts; it depends on the car's condition.

  2. 2

    (ii) Not equally likely — it depends on the player's skill.

  3. 3

    (iii) Equally likely — when the answer is a guess, right and wrong are equally likely (one of two choices).

  4. 4

    (iv) Equally likely — a boy or a girl is taken to be equally likely, as NCERT's answer does.

  5. Check: in (i) and (ii) a reason other than chance (the car's condition, the player's skill) decides the result; in (iii) and (iv) nothing favours either outcome ✔.

  6. Answer (iii) and (iv) have equally likely outcomes; (i) and (ii) do not.

    Common trap: Answering "all four" because each has two outcomes.
Exercise 14.1, Q3Why a coin toss is fair1 mark
  1. Why is tossing a coin considered to be a fair way of deciding which team should get the ball at the beginning of a football game?

Given: a coin toss before a game. Asked: why it is fair.

Concept used: equally likely outcomes (Topic 2). Why it applies: each team is given one face of the coin.

  1. 1

    A fair coin is equally likely to show heads or tails, P(H)=P(T)=12P(\text{H}) = P(\text{T}) = \dfrac{1}{2}P(H)=P(T)=21​.

  2. 2

    The result of a toss cannot be predicted or controlled, so neither team has an advantage.

  3. Check: P(H)+P(T)=12+12=1P(\text{H}) + P(\text{T}) = \dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{2} = 1P(H)+P(T)=21​+21​=1, and the two chances are equal ✔.

  4. Answer Because heads and tails are equally likely and the result of one toss is unpredictable, each team has the same chance (12\dfrac{1}{2}21​) of getting the ball.

    Common trap: Saying "because the coin always lands heads half the time" — any one toss is unpredictable; the point is the equal chance.

Exercise 14.1, Q5P(not E)1 mark
  1. If P(E) = 0.05, what is the probability of 'not E'?

Given: P(E)=0.05P(E) = 0.05P(E)=0.05. Asked: P(Eˉ)P(\bar{E})P(Eˉ).

Concept used: P(Eˉ)=1−P(E)P(\bar{E}) = 1 - P(E)P(Eˉ)=1−P(E) (Topic 3). Why it applies: E and not E are complementary.

  1. 1

    P(Eˉ)=1−0.05=0.95P(\bar{E}) = 1 - 0.05 = 0.95P(Eˉ)=1−0.05=0.95.

  2. Check: 0.05+0.95=10.05 + 0.95 = 10.05+0.95=1 ✔.

  3. Answer 0.95

    Common trap: Writing 0.5 or 0.995 — subtract 0.05 from 1.00 carefully.

Concept used: impossible event (0) and sure event (1) (Topic 3). Why it applies: no candy is orange; every candy is lemon.

    All outcomes: 3+5=83 + 5 = 83+5=8.

    (i) P(red)=38P(\text{red}) = \dfrac{3}{8}P(red)=83​.

  1. A piggy bank contains hundred 50p coins, fifty ₹ 1 coins, twenty ₹ 2 coins and ten ₹ 5 coins. If it is equally likely that one of the coins will fall out when the bank is turned upside down, what is the probability that the coin (i) will be a 50 p coin ? (ii) will not be a ₹ 5 coin?

Exercise 14.1, Q25Correct or not?2 marks
  1. Which of the following arguments are correct and which are not correct? Give reasons for your answer. (i) If two coins are tossed simultaneously there are three possible outcomes—two heads, two tails or one of each. Therefore, for each of these outcomes, the probability is 13\dfrac{1}{3}31​ (ii) If a die is thrown, there are two possible outcomes— an odd number or an even number. Therefore, the probability of getting an odd number is 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​.

Given: two arguments. Asked: correct or not, with reasons.

Concept used: equally likely outcomes; two coins give 4 ordered outcomes (Topics 2, 5). Why it applies: the formula needs equally likely outcomes.

  1. 1

    (i) The equally likely outcomes are HH, HT, TH, TT. "One of each" is HT or TH — 2 of the 4 — so P(one of each)=24=12P(\text{one of each}) = \dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}P(one of each)=42​=21​, while P(two heads)=P(two tails)=14P(\text{two heads}) = P(\text{two tails}) = \dfrac{1}{4}P(two heads)=P(two tails)=41​. The three "outcomes" are not equally likely: not correct.

  2. 2

    (ii) Odd (1, 3, 5) and even (2, 4, 6) have 3 faces each, so they are equally likely: P(odd)=36=12P(\text{odd}) = \dfrac{3}{6} = \dfrac{1}{2}P(odd)=63​=21​ — correct.

  3. Check: (i) 14+12+14=1\dfrac{1}{4} + \dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{4} = 141​+21​+41​=1, while the argument's 13\dfrac{1}{3}31​ each would need "one of each" to be no more likely than two heads ✔.

  4. Answer (i) Not correct — "one of each" is twice as likely as two heads (12\dfrac{1}{2}21​ against 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​). (ii) Correct — odd and even are equally likely.
