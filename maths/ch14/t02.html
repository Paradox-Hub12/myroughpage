Topic 2 of 5

What probability means: outcomes, events and P(E)

About 45 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Name the outcomes of an experiment and decide whether they are equally likely.
  • Find P(E) = (number of outcomes favourable to E) ÷ (number of all possible outcomes) by listing and counting.
  • Explain that this chapter uses theoretical (classical) probability, and that past results do not change it.

Before a cricket match, the umpire tosses a coin. Nobody complains that the toss is unfair. Why not? Because a fair coin is as likely to land heads as tails — each team has the same chance. In this topic you turn "the same chance" into a number, and learn the one formula the whole chapter uses.

  1. Experiment and outcomes

An experiment is an action whose result we cannot predict, such as tossing a coin, throwing a die or drawing a card. Each possible result is an outcome.

Experiment All possible outcomes How many
toss a coin H, T 2
throw a die 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 6
draw a card from a deck the 52 cards 52
pick one ball from a bag of 16 balls the 16 balls 16

  1. Equally likely outcomes

Outcomes are equally likely when none of them has a better chance than another. A fair coin, a fair die and a well-shuffled deck give equally likely outcomes. Words such as "at random", "well-shuffled" and "without looking" tell you the outcomes are equally likely.

Careful: in a bag with 7 green and 4 yellow balls, the two colours are not equally likely — green is more likely. But each ball is equally likely to be picked. So we count balls, not colours.

  1. Events and favourable outcomes

An event is something that may or may not happen, such as "the die shows a multiple of 3". The outcomes that make it happen are the outcomes favourable to the event: here 3 and 6.

  1. The probability of an event

When all the outcomes are equally likely, the theoretical (classical) probability of an event E is

P(E)=favourable outcomesall possible outcomesP(E) = \dfrac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{all possible outcomes}}P(E)=all possible outcomesfavourable outcomes​Swipe to see the whole equation →

In full: the number of outcomes favourable to E, divided by the number of all possible outcomes of the experiment.

So for "a multiple of 3" on a die: P=26=13P = \dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac{1}{3}P=62​=31​.

  1. Theoretical probability only

The NCERT chapter reminds you that probability can also be found by experiment — repeat an experiment many times and divide the number of times the event happened by the number of trials. That experimental probability is not in the 2026-27 syllabus, and it is not taught here. In this chapter "probability" always means the theoretical probability above, worked out by counting equally likely outcomes. One consequence: past results do not change the probability. A fair coin has no memory — after four tails in a row, the next toss is still heads with probability 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​.

Optional (not for the exam): NCERT also shows two starred examples where the outcomes are points on a line or in a region, so the probability is a ratio of lengths or areas. These are marked "Not from the examination point of view".

Worked examples

Worked example 1

One die

Question. A die is thrown once. Find the probability of getting (a) a multiple of 3, (b) a factor of 6.

Given: one fair die, faces 1 to 6. Asked: two probabilities.

Concept used: P(E)=favourableallP(E) = \dfrac{\text{favourable}}{\text{all}}P(E)=allfavourable​. Why it applies: the six faces are equally likely.

  1. 1

    All outcomes: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 — that is 6.

  2. 2

    (a) Multiples of 3: 3, 6 — 2 outcomes. P=26=13P = \dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac{1}{3}P=62​=31​.

  3. 3

    (b) Factors of 6: 1, 2, 3, 6 — 4 outcomes. P=46=23P = \dfrac{4}{6} = \dfrac{2}{3}P=64​=32​.

  4. Check: (b) the faces that are not factors of 6 are 4 and 5 — 2 outcomes, and 4+2=64 + 2 = 64+2=6 ✔.

  5. Answer (a) 13\dfrac{1}{3}31​ (b) 23\dfrac{2}{3}32​

    Common trap: Leaving out 1 and 6 as factors of 6. Every number is a factor of itself, and 1 is a factor of every number.

Worked example 2

A bag of balls

Question. A bag has 7 green, 4 yellow and 5 orange balls of the same size. One ball is taken out at random. Find the probability that it is (a) yellow, (b) green, (c) green or yellow.

Given: 7 + 4 + 5 balls. Asked: three probabilities.

Concept used: count balls, not colours. Why it applies: each ball is equally likely to be taken.

  1. 1

    All outcomes: 7+4+5=167 + 4 + 5 = 167+4+5=16 balls.

  2. 2

    (a) Yellow: 4. P=416=14P = \dfrac{4}{16} = \dfrac{1}{4}P=164​=41​.

  3. 3

    (b) Green: 7. P=716P = \dfrac{7}{16}P=167​.

  4. 4

    (c) Green or yellow: 7+4=117 + 4 = 117+4=11. P=1116P = \dfrac{11}{16}P=1611​.

  5. Check: (c) the other balls are the 5 orange ones, and 11+5=1611 + 5 = 1611+5=16 ✔.

  6. Answer (a) 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​ (b) 716\dfrac{7}{16}167​ (c) 1116\dfrac{11}{16}1611​

    Common trap: Saying "three colours, so each colour has probability 13\dfrac{1}{3}31​". The colours are not equally likely; the balls are.

Worked example 3

Equally likely or not?

Question. Are the outcomes equally likely? Give a reason. (a) A seed is planted: it grows or it does not grow. (b) A card is picked at random from ten identical cards numbered 1 to 10: the number is odd or even. (c) A spinner has one large red sector (three-quarters of the circle) and one small blue sector: it stops on red or on blue. (d) A die is thrown: the number is odd or even.

Given: four experiments, each with two outcomes. Asked: equally likely or not, with a reason.

Concept used: outcomes are equally likely only when nothing makes one more likely. Why it applies: "two outcomes" does not by itself mean "each 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​".

  1. 1

    (a) Not equally likely — good seed, soil and water make growing much more likely; it depends on conditions.

  2. 2

    (b) Equally likely — 5 odd cards (1, 3, 5, 7, 9) and 5 even cards (2, 4, 6, 8, 10).

  3. 3

    (c) Not equally likely — red covers three-quarters of the circle, so the arrow stops on red more often.

  4. 4

    (d) Equally likely — 3 odd faces (1, 3, 5) and 3 even faces (2, 4, 6).

  5. Check: in (b) and (d) the counts on the two sides are equal (5 and 5; 3 and 3); in (a) and (c) something other than chance tips the balance ✔.

  6. Answer (a) No (b) Yes (c) No (d) Yes — with the reasons above.

    Common trap: "There are two outcomes, so each has probability 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​." That is true only when the two outcomes are equally likely.

Worked example 4

Picking a student

Question. A class has 32 students; 14 of them wear glasses. The class monitor is chosen by drawing one name at random. Find the probability that the monitor (a) wears glasses, (b) does not wear glasses.

Given: 32 students, 14 with glasses. Asked: two probabilities.

Concept used: each name is one equally likely outcome. Why it applies: the name is drawn at random.

  1. 1

    All outcomes: 32.

  2. 2

    (a) Favourable: 14. P=1432=716P = \dfrac{14}{32} = \dfrac{7}{16}P=3214​=167​.

  3. 3

    (b) Favourable: 32−14=1832 - 14 = 1832−14=18. P=1832=916P = \dfrac{18}{32} = \dfrac{9}{16}P=3218​=169​.

  4. Check: 716+916=1616=1\dfrac{7}{16} + \dfrac{9}{16} = \dfrac{16}{16} = 1167​+169​=1616​=1 — every student either wears glasses or does not ✔.

  5. Answer (a) 716\dfrac{7}{16}167​ (b) 916\dfrac{9}{16}169​

    Common trap: Writing 1418\dfrac{14}{18}1814​ — glasses against no glasses. The denominator is always all the outcomes (32).

Worked example 5

Does the past matter?

Question. A fair coin has shown tails on each of its last four tosses. Rohan says the next toss is "sure to be heads". Is he right? What is the probability of heads on the next toss?

Given: a fair coin; four tails in a row. Asked: P(head) on the next toss.

Concept used: theoretical probability counts the equally likely outcomes of this toss. Why it applies: the coin does not remember earlier tosses.

  1. 1

    The next toss has two outcomes, H and T, and they are still equally likely.

  2. 2

    P(head)=12P(\text{head}) = \dfrac{1}{2}P(head)=21​.

  3. 3

    Rohan is wrong: heads is not "sure" (that would be probability 1), and not even more likely than tails.

  4. Check: P(head)+P(tail)=12+12=1P(\text{head}) + P(\text{tail}) = \dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{2} = 1P(head)+P(tail)=21​+21​=1, and the coin, its two faces and the way it is tossed are exactly the same as before ✔.

  5. Answer No. P(head)=12P(\text{head}) = \dfrac{1}{2}P(head)=21​, as on every toss.

    Common trap: Thinking a result is "due" after a long run of the other result.

Quick check · 4 of 4MCQ1 mark

A fair coin has landed heads on 5 tosses in a row. The probability that it lands heads on the 6th toss is:

Heads is not "used up" — a coin has no memory.
The 6th toss still has two equally likely outcomes, H and T.
Five heads in a row do not make heads certain.
This treats "1 head out of 6 tosses" as the probability; earlier tosses do not count.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers say that treating outcomes as equally likely when they are not, using a wrong total and thinking past results change the chance are among the most common reasons for lost marks in this topic.

  • "Three colours, so each is 13\dfrac{1}{3}31​"

    The colours have different numbers of balls.

    Count the balls: balls of that colourall balls\dfrac{\text{balls of that colour}}{\text{all balls}}all ballsballs of that colour​.

  • "Two outcomes, so 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​" for a seed, a match or an exam

    These outcomes are not equally likely.

    Use the formula only for equally likely outcomes.

  • Denominator = the other outcomes (1418\dfrac{14}{18}1814​)

    The denominator is all the outcomes.

    1432\dfrac{14}{32}3214​.

  • "After four tails, heads is due"

    A fair coin has no memory.

    Each toss: P(head)=12P(\text{head}) = \dfrac{1}{2}P(head)=21​.

  • Answer left as 46\dfrac{4}{6}64​

    Answers are given in lowest terms.

    23\dfrac{2}{3}32​.

Key points

  • An outcome is one possible result; outcomes are equally likely when none has a better chance.
  • P(E)=number of outcomes favourable to Enumber of all possible outcomesP(E) = \dfrac{\text{number of outcomes favourable to } E}{\text{number of all possible outcomes}}P(E)=number of all possible outcomesnumber of outcomes favourable to E​, for equally likely outcomes.
  • Count objects (balls, cards, students), not kinds (colours).
  • This chapter uses theoretical probability only; past results do not change it.
  • Write every answer in lowest terms.
