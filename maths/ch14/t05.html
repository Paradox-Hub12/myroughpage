Topic 5 of 5

Two coins, three coins and two dice

About 55 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • List the outcomes of tossing two or three coins (HHT, HTH and THH are different outcomes) and find probabilities.
  • Use the 6 × 6 grid for two dice, or one die thrown twice, for sums, products, differences and doubles.
  • Solve multi-part and case-study questions and compare the chances of players in a game.

Toss two coins. Are "two heads", "one head and one tail" and "two tails" equally likely? Most people say yes — and most people are wrong. One head and one tail can happen in two ways (HT and TH), so it is twice as likely as two heads. When two or three things happen together, the safe way is to list every outcome in order. This topic shows you how, for coins and for two dice.

  1. Two coins: 4 outcomes

Think of the coins as "first" and "second" (a ₹1 coin and a ₹2 coin, say). Each has H or T:

HH,HT,TH,TT\text{HH},\quad \text{HT},\quad \text{TH},\quad \text{TT}HH,HT,TH,TTSwipe to see the whole equation →

HT (head on the first, tail on the second) and TH are different outcomes. So P(two heads)=14P(\text{two heads}) = \dfrac{1}{4}P(two heads)=41​, but P(one head and one tail)=24=12P(\text{one head and one tail}) = \dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}P(one head and one tail)=42​=21​.

  1. Three coins: 8 outcomes

Each new coin doubles the list: 2×2×2=82 \times 2 \times 2 = 82×2×2=8. List them by the number of heads so you miss none:

Heads Outcomes How many
3 HHH 1
2 HHT, HTH, THH 3
1 HTT, THT, TTH 3
0 TTT 1

HHT, HTH and THH are three different outcomes — the tail is on the third, the second or the first toss. Tossing one coin three times gives exactly the same 8 outcomes.

  1. Two dice: 36 outcomes

Write each outcome as an ordered pair (first die, second die). The first die has 6 faces and the second has 6, so there are 6×6=366 \times 6 = 366×6=36 outcomes. (1, 4) and (4, 1) are different outcomes; a double such as (3, 3) is one outcome. Throwing one die twice is the same experiment.

The grid below shows the sum in each cell: the rows are the first die and the columns the second die. Equal sums lie on slanting lines.

The sums 2 to 12 are not equally likely: 7 appears 6 times, 2 and 12 only once each. For a product or a difference, write the products or differences in the grid instead.

  1. "At least one" — use the complement

"At least one six", "a head at least once" — count the opposite ("no six on either die", "no head at all"), which is a single block, and subtract: P(at least one)=1−P(none)P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})P(at least one)=1−P(none).

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Two coins

Question. A ₹1 coin and a ₹2 coin are tossed together. Find the probability of getting (a) a head on the ₹1 coin, (b) a head on the ₹1 coin and a tail on the ₹2 coin.

Given: two fair coins. Asked: two probabilities.

Concept used: list the 4 ordered outcomes (₹1 coin first). Why it applies: HT and TH are different outcomes.

  1. 1

    All outcomes: HH, HT, TH, TT — 4.

  2. 2

    (a) Head on the ₹1 coin: HH, HT → 24=12\dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}42​=21​.

  3. 3

    (b) Head on the ₹1 coin and tail on the ₹2 coin: HT only → 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​.

  4. Check: (a) the ₹1 coin alone has two equally likely faces, so its head must have probability 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​ whatever the ₹2 coin does ✔; (b) is one of the 4 outcomes ✔.

  5. Answer (a) 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​ (b) 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​

    Common trap: In (b), counting TH as well ("one head and one tail" in any order) and answering 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​ — the question fixes which coin shows the head.

Worked example 2

Three coins

Question. A coin is tossed three times. List all the outcomes, and find the probability of getting (a) exactly two heads, (b) a head on the first toss and a tail on the last toss, (c) the same result on the first and the last toss.

Given: three tosses of a fair coin. Asked: the list and three probabilities.

Concept used: 2×2×2=82 \times 2 \times 2 = 82×2×2=8 ordered outcomes. Why it applies: each toss has two equally likely results.

  1. 1

    Outcomes: HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT — 8.

  2. 2

    (a) Exactly two heads: HHT, HTH, THH → 38\dfrac{3}{8}83​.

  3. 3

    (b) First H and last T: HHT, HTT → 28=14\dfrac{2}{8} = \dfrac{1}{4}82​=41​.

  4. 4

    (c) First and last the same: HHH, HTH, THT, TTT → 48=12\dfrac{4}{8} = \dfrac{1}{2}84​=21​.

  5. Check: by numbers of heads the 8 outcomes split as 1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8, and the "2 heads" group has 3 ✔.

  6. Answer (a) 38\dfrac{3}{8}83​ (b) 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​ (c) 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​

    Common trap: Taking "two heads" as a single outcome among "0, 1, 2, 3 heads" and answering 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​ for (a).

Worked example 3

Two dice: sums

Question. Two dice are thrown together. Find the probability that the sum of the numbers on top is (a) a multiple of 3, (b) at least 10.

Given: two fair dice. Asked: two probabilities.

Concept used: the 6 × 6 grid of sums (above). Why it applies: the 36 ordered pairs are equally likely.

  1. 1

    All outcomes: 6×6=366 \times 6 = 366×6=36.

  2. 2

    (a) Sums that are multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12. From the grid: sum 3 — 2 cells, 6 — 5, 9 — 4, 12 — 1. Favourable: 2+5+4+1=122 + 5 + 4 + 1 = 122+5+4+1=12. P=1236=13P = \dfrac{12}{36} = \dfrac{1}{3}P=3612​=31​.

  3. 3

    (b) At least 10: sums 10, 11, 12 — (4, 6), (5, 5), (6, 4), (5, 6), (6, 5), (6, 6) → 6. P=636=16P = \dfrac{6}{36} = \dfrac{1}{6}P=366​=61​.

  4. Check: (a) count row by row: in each row of the grid exactly 2 of the 6 sums are multiples of 3 (row 1: 3, 6; row 2: 3, 6; …), so 6×2=126 \times 2 = 126×2=12 ✔.

  5. Answer (a) 13\dfrac{1}{3}31​ (b) 16\dfrac{1}{6}61​

    Common trap: Taking the 11 sums 2, 3, …, 12 as equally likely: (a) would then be 411\dfrac{4}{11}114​ — wrong.

Worked example 4

Two dice: products and differences

Question. Two dice are thrown. Find the probability that (a) the product of the two numbers is odd, (b) the product is even, (c) the two numbers differ by at least 3.

Given: 36 ordered pairs. Asked: three probabilities.

Concept used: a product is odd only when both numbers are odd; complement; count differences. Why it applies: each event is a block or a pattern in the grid.

  1. 1

    (a) Both odd: first die 1, 3 or 5 and second die 1, 3 or 5: 3×3=93 \times 3 = 93×3=9. P=936=14P = \dfrac{9}{36} = \dfrac{1}{4}P=369​=41​.

  2. 2

    (b) Even product = not odd: P=1−14=34P = 1 - \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{3}{4}P=1−41​=43​.

  3. 3

    (c) Difference 3: (1, 4), (2, 5), (3, 6) and the reverse pairs → 6; difference 4: (1, 5), (2, 6) and reverses → 4; difference 5: (1, 6), (6, 1) → 2. Favourable: 6+4+2=126 + 4 + 2 = 126+4+2=12. P=1236=13P = \dfrac{12}{36} = \dfrac{1}{3}P=3612​=31​.

  4. Check: (c) count the other way: differences 0, 1, 2 give 6+10+8=246 + 10 + 8 = 246+10+8=24 pairs, and 24+12=3624 + 12 = 3624+12=36 ✔.

  5. Answer (a) 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​ (b) 34\dfrac{3}{4}43​ (c) 13\dfrac{1}{3}31​

    Common trap: In (c), counting only (1, 4), (2, 5), (3, 6), (1, 5), (2, 6), (1, 6) — 6 pairs — and forgetting the reverse pairs: 636=16\dfrac{6}{36} = \dfrac{1}{6}366​=61​.

Worked example 5

One die thrown twice: "at least once"

Question. A die is thrown twice. Find the probability that a prime number comes up at least once.

Given: two throws — the same 36 ordered pairs as two dice. Asked: P(a prime at least once).

Concept used: P(at least once)=1−P(never)P(\text{at least once}) = 1 - P(\text{never})P(at least once)=1−P(never). Why it applies: "never" is one block of the grid; "at least once" is an L-shape that is easy to miscount.

  1. 1

    All outcomes: 36. The primes on a die are 2, 3, 5; the faces that are not prime are 1, 4, 6.

  2. 2

    Never a prime: both throws 1, 4 or 6: 3×3=93 \times 3 = 93×3=9. P(never)=936=14P(\text{never}) = \dfrac{9}{36} = \dfrac{1}{4}P(never)=369​=41​.

  3. 3

    P(at least once)=1−14=34P(\text{at least once}) = 1 - \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{3}{4}P(at least once)=1−41​=43​.

  4. Check: count directly: first throw prime (any second throw): 3×6=183 \times 6 = 183×6=18; first throw not prime and second prime: 3×3=93 \times 3 = 93×3=9; 18+9=2718 + 9 = 2718+9=27, and 2736=34\dfrac{27}{36} = \dfrac{3}{4}3627​=43​ ✔.

  5. Answer 34\dfrac{3}{4}43​

    Common trap: Adding 12+12=1\dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{2} = 121​+21​=1 — this counts the 9 pairs with a prime on both throws twice, and gives a "sure" event that is clearly not sure (for example (1, 4) has no prime).

Worked example 6

A die with unusual faces

Question. Two dice are thrown: one is an ordinary die (1 to 6) and the other has faces marked 2, 2, 4, 4, 6, 6. Find the probability that the sum is (a) odd, (b) 8.

Given: 6 faces × 6 faces. Asked: two probabilities.

Concept used: each face is one outcome, even when two faces show the same number. Why it applies: the 36 face-pairs are equally likely.

  1. 1

    All outcomes: 6×6=366 \times 6 = 366×6=36.

  2. 2

    (a) The second die always shows an even number, so the sum is odd only when the ordinary die is odd (1, 3 or 5): 3×6=183 \times 6 = 183×6=18. P=1836=12P = \dfrac{18}{36} = \dfrac{1}{2}P=3618​=21​.

  3. 3

    (b) Sum 8: (2, 6), (4, 4), (6, 2), and each of 2, 4, 6 is on two faces of the second die: 3×2=63 \times 2 = 63×2=6. P=636=16P = \dfrac{6}{36} = \dfrac{1}{6}P=366​=61​.

  4. Check: (b) list by the second die's faces: face 2 (twice) needs a 6; face 4 (twice) needs a 4; face 6 (twice) needs a 2 — 2+2+2=62 + 2 + 2 = 62+2+2=6 pairs ✔.

  5. Answer (a) 12\dfrac{1}{2}21​ (b) 16\dfrac{1}{6}61​

    Common trap: Treating the second die as having only 3 faces (2, 4, 6), giving 18 outcomes in all and 318\dfrac{3}{18}183​ for (b) — it happens to simplify to the same 16\dfrac{1}{6}61​ here, but the method is wrong, and it breaks as soon as the faces are not repeated equally.

Common mistakes!

  • Teachers say that treating HHT, HTH and THH as one outcome, counting (1, 4) and (4, 1) once and using the 11 sums as equally likely are among the most common reasons for lost marks in this topic.

  • Two coins: 3 outcomes (2 heads, 1 head, 0 heads)

    One head can be HT or TH.

    List HH, HT, TH, TT.

  • Three coins: "two heads" as one outcome

    HHT, HTH, THH are three outcomes.

    List all 8; group by number of heads (1, 3, 3, 1).

  • Two dice: 12 or 11 outcomes

    Each die has 6 faces: 6×66 \times 66×6.

    36 ordered pairs.

  • (1, 4) and (4, 1) counted once

    The dice are different.

    Count both.

  • A double such as (3, 3) counted twice

    (3, 3) is one cell of the grid.

    Count it once.

  • "At least once" = 16+16\dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{6}61​+61​

    The pair where both happen is counted twice.

    1−P(never)1 - P(\text{never})1−P(never).

Key points

  • Two coins: 4 outcomes; three coins: 8 outcomes (1, 3, 3, 1 by number of heads).
  • Two dice (or one die thrown twice): 36 ordered pairs; (a, b) and (b, a) are different; doubles count once.
  • Write the sum (or product, or difference) in each cell of the grid, then shade and count.
  • The sums 2 to 12 are not equally likely.
  • At least one = 1 − none.
