Chapter 14 · Unit VII

Probability

5 topicsAbout 8 hoursBoard exam
Why this chapter matters

Who bats first? Which ticket wins the raffle? How likely is it that a bulb picked from a box is faulty? Probability turns "how likely?" into an exact number between 0 and 1. The idea is simple: when every outcome is equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of outcomes that suit you divided by the number of all possible outcomes. The skill is in the counting — listing all the outcomes without missing or repeating any, and reading the words of the question ("not", "at least", "at most", "between", "either … or") exactly.

In the board exam this chapter is part of Unit VII, Statistics and Probability (11 marks together with Chapter 13, Statistics), with the same content in Mathematics Standard and Mathematics Basic. In the four sample papers for 2025-26 and 2026-27 it carries 5 to 6 marks: 1-mark MCQs and assertion–reason items (cards, dice, letters of a word, "can this be a probability?"), a 2- or 3-mark question (two dice, three coins) and, in the Basic papers, a 4-mark case study (a lot of T-shirts; blood groups). The syllabus asks for the classical definition of probability and simple problems on finding the probability of an event.

Not in the syllabus: experimental (empirical) probability — probability found by repeating an experiment many times. The NCERT chapter mentions it only to explain why we need the theoretical definition, and every question in the chapter is theoretical. So this chapter uses theoretical (classical) probability only. NCERT also marks two examples and Exercise 14.1 Q20, which use lengths and areas, as "Not from the examination point of view"; Q20 is solved here with that label.

Conventions used in every question (as in NCERT): a die is fair, with faces 1 to 6 unless the question says otherwise; a coin is fair; a deck has 52 cards in 4 suits of 13 — spades and clubs are black, hearts and diamonds are red; the face cards are the kings, queens and jacks only (12 cards) — aces are not face cards. "At random" and "well-shuffled" mean that every outcome is equally likely.
