How this chapter is asked

The board papers of 2024–2026 asked this chapter 61 times: most often on the types of farming (features of primitive subsistence, intensive subsistence, commercial and plantation farming, and the differences between them), then on crops and their conditions (rice, wheat, tea, coffee, cotton, rubber, jute, sugarcane, millets), the cropping seasons, the government's reforms and measures, and four map items on the leading state for a crop. The 2026-27 sample paper gives the chapter a 5-mark question (tea or coffee; rice or wheat); the 2025-26 sample paper a 2-mark question on rice.

CBSE Sample Question Paper 2026-27, Section B, Q17A5 marks

Question: A farmer plans to shift from food crops to plantation crops and is to choose between tea and coffee. Highlight the difference in the conditions associated with their growth.

Asked: how the conditions for tea and for coffee differ.

Concept used: Tea; coffee (Topic 5).

  1. 1

    What your textbook gives for tea: tropical and sub-tropical climates; warm and moist frost-free climate all through the year; frequent showers evenly distributed over the year; deep and fertile well-drained soil, rich in humus and organic matter; labour-intensive (abundant, cheap and skilled labour).

  2. 2

    What your textbook gives for coffee: the Arabica variety from Yemen, first on the Baba Budan Hills, now confined to the Nilgiri in Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu — but no temperature, rainfall or soil.

  3. 3

    So one difference is fully in your textbook: tea grows in many states, while coffee is confined to a small area in the south (the marking scheme makes the same point).

  4. 4

    The marking scheme adds coffee points that are not in your textbook — Beyond your textbook: coffee "prefers seasonal rainfall", suits "moderate temperatures and well-drained slopes, such as the Western Ghats", and "requires comparatively less labour".

  5. 5

    Write each difference as tea (textbook) vs coffee, and label the coffee halves that come from the marking scheme.

  6. Check: five points, each a matched pair; every coffee condition that is not in the textbook carries its label.

    Common trap: writing coffee's conditions as if your textbook gave them. It gives only coffee's variety, history and area.
  1. Tea needs rainfall all year — frequent showers evenly distributed; Beyond your textbook: coffee prefers seasonal rainfall. (1)
  1. Tea needs a warm, moist, frost-free climate all through the year; Beyond your textbook: coffee suits moderate temperatures. (1)
  1. Tea needs deep, fertile, well-drained soil rich in humus; Beyond your textbook: coffee grows on well-drained slopes such as the Western Ghats. (1)
  1. Tea is labour-intensive, needing abundant, cheap and skilled labour; Beyond your textbook: coffee needs comparatively less labour. (1)
  1. Tea grows in many states — Assam, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Kerala; coffee is confined to the Nilgiri in Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu. (1)

Marking scheme: "Tea is more suitable in areas with high rainfall distributed throughout the year, unlike coffee which prefers seasonal rainfall. · Tea requires a frost-free climate, making it ideal for tropical and sub-tropical regions. · Coffee (Arabica) is suitable for regions with moderate temperatures and well-drained slopes, such as the Western Ghats. · Tea plantations demand large-scale labour availability, while coffee requires comparatively less labour. · Coffee cultivation is geographically more restricted, whereas tea can be grown in several regions of India." — 1 mark per point. Note on the official text: your textbook gives growing conditions for tea only; the scheme's points on coffee's rainfall, temperature, slopes and labour are beyond the textbook and are labelled above.

CBSE Sample Question Paper 2026-27, Section B, Q17B5 marks

Question: Farmers in a region experiencing moderate rainfall, cool winters, and assured irrigation plan to move from mixed cropping to the cultivation of a single cereal crop. Should they opt for rice or wheat? Support your answer with appropriate reasons based on crop requirements.

Asked: a choice between rice and wheat, with reasons from each crop's needs.

Concept used: Rice and wheat compared (Topic 4); rabi crops (Topic 3).

  1. 1

    Cool winters match wheat: a rabi crop needing a cool growing season.

  2. 2

    Moderate rainfall matches wheat's 50 to 75 cm, evenly distributed over the growing season; rice needs above 100 cm.

  3. 3

    Ripening: wheat needs bright sunshine at the time of ripening.

  4. 4

    Rice does not fit: it needs a high temperature above 25°C and high humidity; irrigation can make up for less rain, but not for a cool climate.

  5. 5

    Where such conditions are: wheat is the main food crop in the north and north-west, on the Ganga-Satluj plains — winter rain there comes from the western temperate cyclones.

  6. Check: every reason links a condition in the question (moderate rain, cool winters, irrigation) to a textbook requirement; one point shows why rice fails.

    Common trap: choosing rice because "assured irrigation" is given. Irrigation helps rice where rain is short, but rice also needs heat and humidity.
  1. They should grow wheat, a rabi crop that needs a cool growing season, as in their cool winters. (1)
  1. Wheat needs 50–75 cm of rain evenly spread over the growing season, which moderate rainfall can give; rice needs above 100 cm. (1)
  1. Wheat needs bright sunshine at the time of ripening. (1)
  1. Rice needs a high temperature above 25°C and high humidity, which a cool winter cannot give even with irrigation. (1)
  1. Wheat is the main food crop of the north and north-west, where winter rain from western temperate cyclones helps it. (1)

Marking scheme: "Wheat is more suitable as it grows well in cool temperatures during the growing season. · The region's moderate rainfall matches wheat's requirement of 50–75 cm. · Wheat requires bright sunshine at the time of ripening, which is common in winter months. · Availability of irrigation supports wheat cultivation in areas with limited rainfall. · Wheat is traditionally grown in north and north-western regions, matching the given conditions." — 1 mark per point. Note on the official text: the scheme's "common in winter months" and its irrigation point are not your textbook's words; our points 4 and 5 use the textbook's rice requirements and its western temperate cyclones instead.

CBSE Sample Question Paper 2025-26, Section B, Q162 marks

Question: Rice is grown as a commercial crop in Haryana and Punjab, but as a subsistence crop in Odisha. Using your understanding of geographical factors and economic practices, explain why rice cultivation differs in these regions.

Asked: why the same crop is commercial in one region and subsistence in another — one geographical reason and one economic reason.

Concept used: Commercial farming; one crop, two roles (Topic 2); rice (Topic 4).

  1. 1

    Geographical factor (irrigation): Haryana and Punjab are areas of less rainfall for rice, but a dense network of canal irrigation and tubewells has made rice possible there; your textbook says rice is a commercial crop in Haryana and Punjab.

  2. 2

    Economic practice: commercial farming uses higher doses of modern inputs — HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers, insecticides and pesticides — for higher productivity, and grows for the market.

  3. 3

    Odisha: rice is grown mainly to feed the family (subsistence); the textbook says the degree of commercialisation varies from one region to another.

  4. 4

    The marking scheme adds examples that are not in your textbook — Beyond your textbook: the Sutlej-Yamuna Link Canal, nearness to markets, government procurement and machinery in Punjab and Haryana; rain-fed rice and fewer large irrigation systems in Odisha.

  5. Check: the scheme asks for one point on climate or irrigation and one on economics; both points are there.

    Common trap: writing "Odisha cannot grow rice". Coastal Odisha is one of the textbook's important rice regions; there it is mainly a subsistence crop.
  1. Canals and tubewells in Haryana and Punjab let rice grow despite less rainfall; there it is a commercial crop. (1)
  1. Farmers there use HYV seeds and fertilisers for higher productivity to sell; in Odisha rice mainly feeds the family. (1)

Marking scheme: "Climate and Irrigation … Economic Factors (Market Access) … Farming Practices … Or any other relevant point(s) (Any 2 point to be considered out of which at least one should be related to climate and one economic)" — 1 mark each. Note on the official text: the scheme's examples (the Sutlej-Yamuna Link Canal, market access, procurement, machinery, rain-fed farming in Odisha) are beyond your textbook; the two points above use the textbook's own facts.
