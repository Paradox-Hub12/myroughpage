Topic 6 of 8

Non-food crops: rubber, cotton and jute

About 35 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the conditions, states and uses of rubber, cotton and jute.
  • Classify the four fibre crops and explain sericulture.

Look around you: a bicycle tyre, a school bag's rubber grip, a cotton shirt, a jute sack of rice, a silk scarf. None of these is food, yet all of them begin on a farm. This topic is about India's non-food crops — rubber and the fibre crops (cotton, jute, hemp and silk).

Quick reminder (Chapter 1)

Black soil of the Deccan holds moisture well and is good for cotton — that is why it is also called black cotton soil. Alluvial soil is laid down by rivers on the plains and deltas.

  1. Rubber

Your textbook says

Where mainly Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, the Andaman and Nicobar islands and the Garo hills of Meghalaya

Rubber needs the most rain of all the crops in this chapter: more than 200 cm.

  1. Fibre crops

Your textbook names four major fibre crops of India: cotton, jute, hemp and natural silk.

From the soil From silkworms
Cotton, jute, hemp — derived from crops grown in the soil Natural silk — obtained from cocoons of silkworms fed on green leaves, specially mulberry

Sericulture = the rearing of silkworms for the production of silk fibre.

  1. Cotton

Your textbook says

Worked example 1

Rubber or cotton?

Question. An imagined district, Velamala, receives about 240 cm of rain a year, stays humid, and its temperature rarely falls below 26°C. Another imagined district, Kalimati, has dark soil on a dry plateau, little rain, canals for irrigation and long sunny seasons without frost. Which of rubber and cotton suits each? Use your textbook's conditions.

  1. (The districts and their numbers are imagined; the conditions are from your textbook.)

    Asked: the crop for each district, with reasons.

    Concept used: Rubber; cotton (sections 1 and 3); the routine (section 5).

  2. 1

    Velamala: 240 cm is more than 200 cm; 26°C is above 25°C; moist and humid → rubber.

  3. 2

    Kalimati: dark soil on a dry plateau fits the drier parts of the black cotton soil; light rainfall or irrigation; bright sunshine and frost-free days → cotton.

  4. Check: swap them and both fail: Kalimati's little rain is far below rubber's 200 cm, and cotton needs light rainfall, not 240 cm.

  5. Answer Velamala — rubber; Kalimati — cotton.

    Common trap: choosing cotton for Velamala because "it is hot". Cotton needs light rainfall; 240 cm is rubber country.

Worked example 2

Sort the fibres

Question. A shop sells a cotton shirt, a jute bag, a hemp rope and a silk scarf. (a) Which three fibres come from crops grown in the soil? (b) Where does the fourth come from? (c) What is the rearing of silkworms called?

Asked: a sort, a source and a term.

Concept used: Fibre crops (section 2).

  1. 1

    (a) Cotton, jute and hemp — derived from crops grown in the soil.

  2. 2

    (b) Silk — from the cocoons of silkworms fed on green leaves, specially mulberry.

  3. 3

    (c) Sericulture.

  4. Check: your textbook names exactly four fibre crops; three from the soil plus one from silkworms makes four ✔.

  5. Answer (a) cotton, jute, hemp; (b) silk, from silkworm cocoons; (c) sericulture.

    Common trap: writing "silk is grown on black soil". Silk comes from silkworms; only their food, mulberry, grows in the soil.

Worked example 3

Why jute grows in the flood plains

Question. Explain two geographical conditions your textbook gives for jute, and name two of its major states. (2 marks)

Asked: two conditions and two states.

Concept used: Jute (section 4).

  1. 1

    Soil: well-drained fertile soils in the flood plains, where soils are renewed every year.

  2. 2

    Temperature: high temperature during the time of growth.

  3. 3

    States: two of West Bengal, Bihar, Assam, Odisha, Meghalaya.

  4. Check: the answer has both parts asked — conditions and states.

  5. Answer Flood-plain soils renewed every year; high temperature during growth; e.g. West Bengal and Assam.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Jute grows on well-drained fertile flood-plain soils that are renewed every year, with high temperature during growth. (1)
    2. Major states: West Bengal and Assam (also Bihar, Odisha, Meghalaya). (1)
    Common trap: writing cotton's "black soil" for jute. Jute needs renewed flood-plain soil.

Common mistakes!

  • Rubber given tea's conditions ("frost-free all year").

    Rubber: equatorial, moist and humid, more than 200 cm, above 25°C.

    Learn "over 200, over 25".

  • Cotton placed in West Bengal; jute placed in Punjab.

    Cotton: black cotton soil of the Deccan, Maharashtra first in your textbook's list; jute: flood plains, West Bengal first in your textbook's list.

    Use the cotton–jute table.

  • "Silk is grown in black soil."

    Silk comes from silkworm cocoons; rearing them is sericulture.

    Fibres: three from the soil, one from silkworms.

  • Cotton called a rabi crop.

    Cotton is kharif and needs 6 to 8 months to mature.

    "Cotton: 210 days, 6–8 months, kharif."

Key points

  • Rubber: equatorial (also tropical and sub-tropical under special conditions); moist and humid; more than 200 cm; above 25°C; industrial raw material; Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andaman and Nicobar islands, Garo hills of Meghalaya.
  • Fibre crops: cotton, jute, hemp (from the soil) and natural silk (from silkworms fed on mulberry); sericulture = rearing silkworms for silk.
  • Cotton: India believed its original home; raw material for the cotton textile industry; second after China (2017); drier parts of the black cotton soil of the Deccan; high temperature, light rainfall or irrigation, 210 frost-free days, bright sunshine; kharif, 6–8 months; Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh …
  • Jute: the golden fibre; flood-plain soils renewed every year; high temperature; West Bengal, Bihar, Assam, Odisha, Meghalaya; gunny bags, mats, ropes, yarn, carpets.
