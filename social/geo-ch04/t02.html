Topic 2 of 8

Commercial and plantation farming

About 35 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe commercial farming and explain why the same crop can be commercial in one region and subsistence in another.
  • Describe plantation farming and compare the four types of farming.

A truck leaves a farm loaded with sacks of grain for a market far away. Another truck leaves a tea garden for a factory that will sort and pack the leaves. In both places the farmers grow for sale, not mainly for the family. This topic is about the two kinds of farming that feed markets and industries: commercial and plantation farming.

  1. Commercial farming

Feature What your textbook says
Main characteristic the use of higher doses of modern inputs
Examples of inputs high yielding variety (HYV) seeds, chemical fertilisers, insecticides and pesticides
Aim higher productivity
Varies by region the degree of commercialisation varies from one region to another

One crop, two roles. Your textbook's example: rice is a commercial crop in Haryana and Punjab, but in Odisha it is a subsistence crop. The crop is the same; what changes is why it is grown — for the market, or mainly to feed the family.

  1. Plantation farming — a type of commercial farming

Feature What your textbook says
Crop a single crop grown on a large area
Link with industry the plantation has an interface of agriculture and industry
Land and money large tracts of land, capital intensive inputs
Labour migrant labourers
Produce all the produce is used as raw material in the respective industries
Needs a well-developed network of transport and communication connecting plantation areas, processing industries and markets — because production is mainly for market
Crops in India tea, coffee, rubber, sugarcane, banana, etc.
Examples tea in Assam and North Bengal; coffee in Karnataka

Note on the textbook's wording: the line is printed "Tea in Assam and North Bengal coffee in Karnataka". A comma is missing: tea in Assam and North Bengal; coffee in Karnataka.

NCERT Fig. 4.2 shows a banana plantation in southern India and Fig. 4.3 a bamboo plantation in the north-east.

Why transport matters — a chain. Single crop for the market → it must reach a processing industry (tea is processed within the garden; Topic 5) → it must then reach markets → so plantations develop only where transport and communication link garden, factory and market.

  1. The four types side by side

Type Grown for · inputs Labour · land
Primitive subsistence the family · no fertilisers or modern inputs family or community labour · small patches, shifted
Intensive subsistence the family · biochemical inputs and irrigation labour-intensive · small, uneconomical holdings
Commercial the market · HYV seeds, fertilisers, pesticides not described in your textbook
Plantation industry and market · capital intensive inputs migrant labourers · large tracts, one crop

(The textbook says nothing about labour or land size in commercial farming, so that cell is left as "not described".)

Memory hook: "Family–Family–Market–Factory." Read the four types left to right: who the crop is mainly for.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Sort the farms

Question. Name the type of farming in each imagined farm. (a) A company grows only rubber on 900 hectares; workers from other states tap the trees, and all the latex goes to factories. (b) A family on the crowded plains grows wheat and vegetables on one acre, using fertilisers, pesticides and canal water to feed the family. (c) A farmer sows HYV wheat seeds with chemical fertilisers and sells the whole harvest at the market. (d) A hill family burns a patch of forest, grows millets for a few years, then moves on.

Asked: four types of farming.

Concept used: The four types side by side (section 3).

  1. 1

    (a) One crop, large area, migrant labourers, all produce to industry → plantation farming.

  2. 2

    (b) Tiny plot, high doses of inputs and irrigation, food for the family → intensive subsistence farming.

  3. 3

    (c) HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers, grown for sale → commercial farming.

  4. 4

    (d) Slash and burn, then shifting → primitive subsistence farming.

  5. Check: (b) and (c) both use fertilisers; the difference is who the crop is for — the family (b) or the market (c).

  6. Answer (a) plantation; (b) intensive subsistence; (c) commercial; (d) primitive subsistence.

    Common trap: calling (b) commercial because it uses fertilisers. Intensive subsistence farming also uses high doses of biochemical inputs.

Worked example 2

Why plantations need roads

Question. An imagined company plans a tea estate on a remote hillside with no road. Using your textbook, explain three reasons why the estate needs a good transport and communication network. (3 marks)

Asked: three reasons linked to plantation farming.

Concept used: Plantation farming (section 2).

  1. 1

    Production is mainly for market: a plantation grows a single crop to sell, not to eat.

  2. 2

    Raw material for industry: all the produce is used as raw material in its industry, so it must reach processing industries.

  3. 3

    Plantation areas, processing industries and markets must be connected: your textbook says a well-developed network of transport and communication "plays an important role in the development of plantations".

  4. Check: each point is a different link — market, industry, the network joining them.

  5. Answer The produce is for the market, it goes to processing industries, and the network joins estate, factory and market.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. A plantation's production is mainly for the market, not for the farmer's family. (1)
    2. All its produce is raw material for industries, so it must reach processing industries. (1)
    3. A transport and communication network connecting plantations, processing industries and markets plays an important role in their development. (1)
    Common trap: writing "because the workers need buses". Link the network to the produce, the industry and the market.

Worked example 3

Subsistence or commercial?

Question. "The same crop can be grown for the family in one region and for the market in another." Explain with your textbook's example, and say what decides the difference. (2 marks)

Asked: the textbook example and the deciding factor.

Concept used: One crop, two roles (section 1).

  1. 1

    Example: rice is a commercial crop in Haryana and Punjab, but a subsistence crop in Odisha.

  2. 2

    What decides it: the degree of commercialisation, which varies from region to region — whether the crop is grown mainly for sale or mainly to feed the family.

  3. Check: both parts asked are answered — the example and the deciding factor.

  4. Answer Rice: commercial in Haryana and Punjab, subsistence in Odisha; the degree of commercialisation varies by region.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Rice is a commercial crop in Haryana and Punjab, but a subsistence crop in Odisha. (1)
    2. The degree of commercialisation varies from region to region: grown mainly for sale, or mainly for the family. (1)
    Common trap: saying "Odisha cannot grow rice". Odisha grows a lot of rice; there it is mainly a subsistence crop.

Common mistakes!

  • "Plantation is different from commercial farming."

    Your textbook: plantation is also a type of commercial farming.

    Say "a type of commercial farming in which a single crop is grown on a large area".

  • "Plantations grow food grains for the family."

    Plantation produce is raw material for industries; the crops are tea, coffee, rubber, sugarcane, banana.

    Link plantation to industry and market.

  • "Rice is always a commercial crop."

    It is commercial in Haryana and Punjab but subsistence in Odisha.

    Say the degree of commercialisation varies by region.

  • Using "uses fertilisers" alone to call a farm commercial.

    Intensive subsistence farming also uses high doses of biochemical inputs.

    Ask who the crop is for: the family or the market.

Key points

  • Commercial farming: higher doses of modern inputs — HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers, insecticides, pesticides — for higher productivity; the degree of commercialisation varies (rice: commercial in Haryana and Punjab, subsistence in Odisha).
  • Plantation farming is a type of commercial farming: a single crop on a large area; an interface of agriculture and industry; large tracts, capital intensive inputs, migrant labourers; all produce is raw material for industry.
  • Plantation crops: tea, coffee, rubber, sugarcane, banana; tea in Assam and North Bengal, coffee in Karnataka.
  • Plantations need a transport and communication network linking estates, processing industries and markets.
