Topic 1 of 8

Subsistence farming: primitive and intensive

About 40 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why agriculture matters to India, and describe primitive subsistence ("slash and burn") farming with its names in different regions.
  • Describe intensive subsistence farming and explain why there is enormous pressure on agricultural land.

On a hillside in the north-east, a family clears a patch of forest, burns the cut plants and sows grain in the ash. A few years later they walk away and clear a new patch. Far away, on the crowded plains, another family farms a field so small that you can cross it in twenty steps — and still squeezes out as much grain as it can. Both families farm mainly to feed themselves. This topic is about these two kinds of subsistence farming.

  1. Why agriculture matters

  • India is an agriculturally important country: two-thirds of its population is engaged in agricultural activities.
  • Agriculture is a primary activity. It produces most of the food that we consume.
  • Besides food grains, it produces raw material for various industries (for example, cotton for the cotton textile industry — Topic 6).
  • Some agricultural products, like tea, coffee and spices, are also exported.
Two wordings in your textbook

The opening says "two-thirds of its population is engaged in agricultural activities"; the section on reforms (Topic 7) says agriculture "provides livelihood for more than 60 per cent of its population". They agree: two-thirds (about 67 per cent) is more than 60 per cent. Quote the wording the question uses.

  1. Farming varies

Farming methods have changed over the years, depending on the physical environment, technological know-how and socio-cultural practices. Farming varies from subsistence (growing food mainly for the family) to commercial (growing for the market). Your textbook describes four farming systems:

  1. Primitive subsistence farming

It is still practised in a few pockets of India.

Feature What your textbook says
Land small patches of land
Tools primitive tools like the hoe, dao and digging sticks
Labour family or community labour
Depends on the monsoon, the natural fertility of the soil and other environmental conditions
Method "slash and burn": clear a patch, grow cereals and other food crops to sustain the family
When soil fertility falls the farmers shift and clear a fresh patch
Why shifting helps it allows Nature to replenish the fertility of the soil through natural processes
Productivity low, because the farmer does not use fertilisers or other modern inputs

Its many names. The same practice has different names in different places.

Name Where (your textbook)
Jhumming north-eastern states — Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland
Pamlou Manipur
Dipa Bastar district of Chhattisgarh, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands

  1. Intensive subsistence farming

Feature What your textbook says
Where areas of high population pressure on land
Labour labour-intensive farming
Inputs high doses of biochemical inputs and irrigation, for higher production
Size of holdings the right of inheritance divides land among successive generations, making holdings uneconomical
Why farmers keep at it they take maximum output from the limited land in the absence of an alternative source of livelihood
Result enormous pressure on agricultural land

Cause → effect chain: more people on the land → land divided at each generation (right of inheritance) → tiny, uneconomical plots → no other way to earn → farmers push for maximum output with labour, biochemical inputs and irrigation → enormous pressure on agricultural land.

  1. Primitive and intensive side by side

Primitive subsistence Intensive subsistence
Inputs no fertilisers or modern inputs high doses of biochemical inputs and irrigation
Tools and labour hoe, dao, digging sticks; family or community labour labour-intensive
Land small patches, shifted when fertile soil is used up small, uneconomical holdings
Output low land productivity maximum output from limited land

Memory hook: "Primitive moves, intensive squeezes." Primitive farmers move to a fresh patch; intensive farmers squeeze the most out of the same small plot.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Which farming, and what is it called?

Question. Lalsang's family lives in the hills of Mizoram. Each year they cut the bushes on a new hillside, burn them and sow rice and maize with digging sticks. After two or three harvests the crops grow poorly, so they move to another slope. (a) Name the type of farming. (b) What does your textbook call it in Mizoram? (c) Why does the old slope recover? (Lalsang's family is imagined; the facts you need are in your textbook.)

Asked: the type, its local name, and why the land recovers.

Concept used: Primitive subsistence farming and its names (section 3).

  1. 1

    (a) Cutting, burning, simple tools, food for the family, moving on → primitive subsistence farming ("slash and burn").

  2. 2

    (b) Mizoram is one of the north-eastern states where it is called jhumming.

  3. 3

    (c) When the family shifts, the old patch is left alone, and Nature replenishes the fertility of the soil through natural processes.

  4. Check: the clue "move to another slope" fits only primitive subsistence farming; intensive farmers stay on the same plot.

  5. Answer (a) Primitive subsistence (slash and burn) farming; (b) jhumming; (c) shifting lets Nature replenish the soil's fertility.

    Common trap: writing "Pamlou" for Mizoram. Pamlou is the name in Manipur.

Worked example 2

Small plots, big output

Question. In an imagined village on the crowded plains, a farmer's grandfather had 6 acres. It was divided equally among his three sons, and each son's share was divided equally between two children. (a) How much land does each grandchild get? (b) Using your textbook, explain why such farmers still try to get maximum output from their land. (3 marks)

  1. (The village and its numbers are imagined for practice; the reasons are from your textbook.)

    Asked: the size of each holding; the textbook's reasons for intensive farming.

    Concept used: Intensive subsistence farming (section 4).

  2. 1

    (a) 6 acres ÷ 3 sons = 2 acres each; 2 acres ÷ 2 children = 1 acre each.

  3. 2

    Why holdings shrink: the right of inheritance divides land among successive generations, making holdings uneconomical.

  4. 3

    Why they still farm hard: there is no alternative source of livelihood, so farmers take maximum output from the limited land.

  5. 4

    How they do it: labour-intensive farming with high doses of biochemical inputs and irrigation.

  6. Check: 1 acre × 2 children × 3 sons = 6 acres ✔. Each reason is a different textbook point (inheritance, no other livelihood, inputs).

  7. Answer (a) 1 acre each; (b) inheritance divides land; with no other livelihood, farmers use labour, biochemical inputs and irrigation to get maximum output.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. The right of inheritance divides land among successive generations, so holdings become small and uneconomical. (1)
    2. Having no alternative source of livelihood, farmers try to take maximum output from their limited land. (1)
    3. So they use much labour and high doses of biochemical inputs and irrigation; there is enormous pressure on agricultural land. (1)
    Common trap: describing slash and burn here. Intensive farmers do not move; they farm the same small plot harder.

Worked example 3

Reading the textbook's numbers

Question. Your textbook says that two-thirds of India's population is engaged in agricultural activities, and later that agriculture gives a livelihood to more than 60 per cent. (a) Write two-thirds as a per cent. (b) Do the two statements disagree? (c) Give one other reason your textbook gives for agriculture's importance.

Asked: a conversion, a comparison and one more reason.

Concept used: Why agriculture matters (section 1).

  1. 1

    (a) two-thirds = 2 ÷ 3 ≈ 0.667 → about 67 per cent.

  2. 2

    (b) "About 67 per cent" is more than 60 per cent, so the two statements do not disagree; both say about two in three people depend on agriculture.

  3. 3

    (c) It produces most of the food we consume (or: raw material for industries; exports such as tea, coffee and spices).

  4. Check: 67 > 60 ✔ — "more than 60 per cent" is true of two-thirds.

  5. Answer (a) about 67 per cent; (b) no — both mean about two in three people; (c) it produces most of our food.

    Common trap: saying "two-thirds means 60 per cent". Two-thirds is about 67 per cent.

Common mistakes!

  • "Primitive farmers use fertilisers; intensive farmers use digging sticks."

    It is the other way round: primitive farming uses no fertilisers and primitive tools; intensive farming uses high doses of biochemical inputs and irrigation.

    Primitive moves, intensive squeezes.

  • "Farmers shift because the land is ruined for ever."

    They shift when soil fertility decreases; shifting allows Nature to replenish the fertility.

    Write "shifting lets Nature replenish the soil".

  • Jhumming placed in Madhya Pradesh, or Pamlou in Mizoram.

    Jhumming: north-eastern states; Pamlou: Manipur; Dipa: Bastar and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands; Bewar/Dahiya: Madhya Pradesh.

    Learn each name with its region from the table.

  • One point for a 3-mark "features" question.

    Each feature earns one mark.

    Give three different features: land and tools, method, productivity.

Key points

  • Two-thirds of India's people are engaged in agriculture; it gives most of our food, raw material for industries, and exports (tea, coffee, spices).
  • Primitive subsistence farming: small patches, hoe, dao, digging sticks, family/community labour; depends on monsoon and natural fertility; slash and burn; shift when fertility falls (Nature replenishes the soil); low productivity (no fertilisers or modern inputs).
  • Names: jhumming (Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland), Pamlou (Manipur), Dipa (Bastar, Chhattisgarh; Andaman and Nicobar Islands).
  • Intensive subsistence farming: high population pressure, labour-intensive, high doses of biochemical inputs and irrigation; inheritance makes holdings uneconomical; with no other livelihood, farmers take maximum output → enormous pressure on land.
