Topic 4 of 8

Food grains: rice, wheat, millets, maize and pulses

About 45 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the geographical conditions and the areas of rice and wheat.
  • Describe the features, conditions and major states of jowar, bajra, ragi, maize and pulses.

A plate of rice and dal in the east, rotis and dal in the north-west, ragi balls in the south: the grain changes with the region, because each crop needs its own temperature, rainfall and soil. This topic gives the conditions for India's food grains and where they grow. Learn them as a set: crop → season → temperature → rainfall → soil → states.

Quick reminder (Chapter 1 and Chapter 3)

Alluvial soil covers the northern plains and the coastal deltas; black soil covers the Deccan plateau of Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh; red soil covers much of the southern and eastern plateau. Canals and tube-wells bring irrigation where rain is short. Temperatures are in degrees Celsius (°C); yearly rainfall is in centimetres (cm).

Your textbook names the major crops of India: rice, wheat, millets, pulses, tea, coffee, sugarcane, oil seeds, cotton and jute. They are grown in different parts of the country depending upon the variations in soil, climate and cultivation practices.

  1. Rice

Rice (your textbook)
Importance the staple food crop of a majority of the people in India; India is the second largest producer of rice in the world, after China
Season kharif
Temperature high, above 25°C
Humidity and rain high humidity; annual rainfall above 100 cm; in areas of less rainfall it grows with the help of irrigation
Where the plains of north and north-eastern India, coastal areas and the deltaic regions
Irrigation areas a dense network of canal irrigation and tubewells has made rice possible in areas of less rainfall — Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh and parts of Rajasthan

The most important rice-growing regions are listed in Topic 3: Assam, West Bengal, coastal Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Maharashtra (Konkan coast), Uttar Pradesh and Bihar; recently also Punjab and Haryana. Your textbook's map "India: Distribution of Rice" (p. 33) shades the major areas: the eastern plains and the east and south-east coasts. NCERT Fig. 4.4 shows rice growing and ready for harvest.

  1. Wheat

Wheat (your textbook)
Importance the second most important cereal crop; the main food crop in the north and north-west
Season rabi
Temperature and sun a cool growing season and bright sunshine at the time of ripening
Rain 50 to 75 cm of annual rainfall, evenly distributed over the growing season
Two zones the Ganga-Satluj plains in the north-west and the black soil region of the Deccan
Major states Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar and Rajasthan

Your textbook's map "India: Distribution of Wheat" (p. 35) shades the major area from Punjab and Haryana through western Uttar Pradesh into Madhya Pradesh. NCERT Fig. 4.5 shows a wheat field.

Rice and wheat compared (the pair most often asked):

Rice Wheat

Rainfall above 100 cm (irrigation where less) 50–75 cm, evenly distributed

  1. Millets — jowar, bajra and ragi

They are coarse grains, but they have very high nutritional value.

Millet Your textbook says Major states
Jowar the third most important food crop with respect to area and production; rain-fed, grown mostly in moist areas; hardly needs irrigation Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh
Bajra grows well on sandy soils and shallow black soil Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Haryana
Ragi a crop of dry regions; grows on red, black, sandy, loamy and shallow black soils; very rich in iron, calcium, other micro nutrients and roughage Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Jharkhand, Arunachal Pradesh

NCERT Fig. 4.6 shows bajra growing.

  1. Maize

  • Used both as food and fodder.
  • A kharif crop; needs a temperature between 21°C and 27°C; grows well in old alluvial soil.
  • In some states, like Bihar, maize is grown in the rabi season also.
  • high yielding variety (HYV) seeds, fertilisers and irrigation have increased its production.
  • Major states: Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. NCERT Fig. 4.7 shows maize.

  1. Pulses

  • India is the largest producer as well as the consumer of pulses in the world.
  • Pulses are the major source of protein in a vegetarian diet.
  • Major pulses: tur (arhar), urad, moong, masur, peas and gram.
  • They need less moisture and survive even in dry conditions.
  • Your textbook: "Being leguminous crops, all these crops except arhar help in restoring soil fertility by fixing nitrogen from the air." So they are mostly grown in rotation with other crops.
Note on the textbook's wording

In the exam, write your textbook's words, "all these crops except arhar". Beyond your textbook: arhar (tur, pigeon pea) is also a legume, and it too is usually said to fix nitrogen.

  • Major states: Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh and Karnataka.

Cause → effect: leguminous → fixes nitrogen from the air → restores soil fertility → grown in rotation with other crops.

Memory hook — the rainfall ladder: "Wheat 50–75 · Rice above 100 (cm)" — and in Topics 5–6 you add Sugarcane 75–100 and Rubber above 200.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Which grain suits which place?

Question. Two imagined districts: Nadipur is on a river delta with 150 cm of rain a year, humid air and summer temperatures of about 30°C. Sukhagarh is on a black-soil plateau in central India, with about 65 cm of rain spread evenly over a cool growing season and clear, sunny weeks before the harvest. Which of rice, wheat and sugarcane suits each? Give the textbook conditions that decide.

  1. (The districts and their numbers are imagined; the conditions are from your textbook.)

    Asked: the crop for each district and the deciding conditions.

    Concept used: Rice; wheat (sections 1 and 2); sugarcane (Topic 5).

  2. 1

    Nadipur: 150 cm is above 100 cm; 30°C is above 25°C; humid; a delta → rice.

  3. 2

    Sukhagarh: 65 cm lies between 50 and 75 cm, evenly distributed over the growing season; a cool growing season; bright sunshine at the time of ripening; the black soil region of the Deccan is one of the textbook's two wheat zones → wheat.

  4. 3

    Not sugarcane for Sukhagarh: sugarcane needs a hot and humid climate, 21°C to 27°C and 75 cm to 100 cm of rain — Sukhagarh is cool, and its rain is too little.

  5. Check: swap them and both fail: Sukhagarh's rain is far below rice's 100 cm, and Nadipur is not cool.

  6. Answer Nadipur — rice (above 25°C, above 100 cm, humid delta); Sukhagarh — wheat (cool season, 50–75 cm evenly spread, bright sunshine at ripening).

    Common trap: choosing sugarcane for Sukhagarh because both grow on the plateau. Sugarcane wants heat and 75–100 cm; a cool season with 50–75 cm describes wheat.

Worked example 2

The millets in one answer

Question. "Coarse grains are not poor grains." Support the statement with three points about millets from your textbook. (3 marks)

Asked: three points that show millets are valuable.

Concept used: Millets (section 3).

  1. 1

    Nutrition: jowar, bajra and ragi are coarse grains but have very high nutritional value; ragi is very rich in iron, calcium, other micro nutrients and roughage.

  2. 2

    Importance: jowar is the third most important food crop with respect to area and production.

  3. 3

    Hardy: jowar is rain-fed and hardly needs irrigation; ragi grows in dry regions; bajra grows on sandy soils and shallow black soil.

  4. Check: three different ideas — nutrition, importance, hardiness — each from the textbook.

  5. Answer Millets are highly nutritious (ragi: iron, calcium), jowar is the third most important food crop, and millets grow in dry or rain-fed conditions.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Millets are coarse grains but have very high nutritional value; ragi is very rich in iron, calcium and roughage. (1)
    2. Jowar is the third most important food crop with respect to area and production. (1)
    3. They suit hard conditions: jowar is rain-fed, ragi grows in dry regions and bajra on sandy soils. (1)
    Common trap: saying "coarse means low nutrition". Your textbook says the opposite.

Worked example 3

A rotation plan

Question. An imagined farmer grows wheat on her field every rabi season. She wants to grow a pulse in the kharif season, between two wheat crops, to help the soil. Using your textbook, name one suitable pulse and explain how it helps. (2 marks)

Asked: a kharif pulse, and how pulses help the soil.

Concept used: Pulses (section 5); the kharif list (Topic 3).

  1. 1

    Season first: your textbook's kharif list includes moong and urad (and tur). Gram and peas are rabi crops — the same season as her wheat.

  2. 2

    How it helps: pulses are leguminous crops that help in restoring soil fertility by fixing nitrogen from the air, so they are grown in rotation with other crops. (Your textbook does not count arhar (tur) here; see the note in section 5.)

  3. Check: both parts are answered — a pulse of the right season, and the reason.

  4. Answer Moong or urad (kharif pulses); pulses fix nitrogen from the air and restore soil fertility.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Moong or urad, which your textbook lists among kharif crops. (1)
    2. Being leguminous, pulses restore soil fertility by fixing nitrogen from the air, so they are grown in rotation. (1)
    Common trap: naming gram or peas. They are rabi crops, so they would compete with her wheat.

Common mistakes!

  • "Rice needs 50–75 cm; wheat needs above 100 cm."

    Swapped: rice above 100 cm, wheat 50–75 cm.

    Use the rainfall ladder.

  • "Rice grows only where it rains heavily."

    Canals and tubewells let rice grow in Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh and parts of Rajasthan.

    Say "with the help of irrigation where rainfall is less".

  • Only one wheat zone written.

    There are two: the Ganga-Satluj plains and the black soil region of the Deccan.

    Name both zones.

  • "Coarse grains have little food value."

    Millets have very high nutritional value; ragi is rich in iron and calcium.

    Quote the textbook's nutrition point.

  • Jowar, bajra and ragi facts swapped.

    Jowar: third most important food crop, rain-fed; bajra: sandy and shallow black soil; ragi: dry regions, iron and calcium.

    Learn one key fact for each.

  • "Maize is a rabi crop needing 50–75 cm."

    Maize is kharif (also rabi in Bihar), needs 21°C–27°C and old alluvial soil, and is food and fodder; 50–75 cm is wheat's.

    Learn maize with sugarcane's temperature range.

  • "Pulses need a lot of water."

    They need less moisture and survive even in dry conditions.

    Learn pulses with the dry-land crops, not with rice.

Key points

  • Rice: kharif; above 25°C, high humidity, above 100 cm (irrigation where less); plains of north and north-east India, coasts and deltas; with canals and tubewells — Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh, parts of Rajasthan; India second after China.
  • Wheat: rabi; cool growing season, bright sunshine at ripening, 50–75 cm evenly distributed; zones: Ganga-Satluj plains and black soil region of the Deccan; Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, Rajasthan.
  • Millets (high nutrition): jowar — third most important food crop, rain-fed; bajra — sandy and shallow black soil; ragi — dry regions, iron and calcium.
  • Maize: food and fodder; kharif (rabi too in Bihar); 21°C–27°C; old alluvial soil.
  • Pulses: India the largest producer and consumer; protein; less moisture; leguminous, fix nitrogen (your textbook: "all these crops except arhar") → grown in rotation.
