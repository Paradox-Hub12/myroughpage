Topic 7 of 8

Technological and institutional reforms

About 40 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why reforms were needed, and describe the institutional reforms after Independence, the Green Revolution and the White Revolution.
  • Describe the comprehensive land development programme and the schemes for farmers, and the Bhoodan–Gramdan movement.

Imagine a farmer whose land has been split among brothers until each plot is tiny, who waits for the monsoon every year, who borrows money at a high rate and sells his crop to a middleman at whatever price is offered. What would help him? Better seeds and water (technological change), or fairer rules about land, loans and prices (institutional change)? Your textbook says India needed both.

  1. Why reforms were needed

  • Agriculture has been practised in India for thousands of years. Sustained uses of land without compatible techno-institutional changes have hindered the pace of agricultural development.
  • In spite of new sources of irrigation, most farmers in large parts of the country still depend upon the monsoon and natural fertility.
  • For a growing population, this poses a serious challenge.
  • Agriculture provides livelihood for more than 60 per cent of the population, so it needs serious technical and institutional reforms.

  1. Two kinds of reform

Technological (technical) reforms Institutional reforms
change how farming is done: high yielding variety (HYV) seeds, fertilisers, irrigation (Beyond your textbook: the usual meaning of the Green Revolution's "package technology") change the rules and organisations around farming: collectivisation, consolidation of holdings, cooperation, abolition of zamindari (your textbook's institutional reforms); crop insurance, Grameen banks, support prices (Beyond your textbook: our sorting)

(The words "technological" and "institutional" are your textbook's. It names collectivisation, consolidation of holdings, cooperation and abolition of zamindari as institutional reforms; it does not sort the other reforms one by one.)

  1. Reforms through time

Period What your textbook says
After Independence collectivisation, consolidation of holdings, cooperation and abolition of zamindari were given priority to bring about institutional reforms. 'Land reform' was the main focus of the First Five Year Plan. The right of inheritance had already led to fragmentation of land holdings, making consolidation necessary. Land-reform laws were enacted, but the implementation was lacking or lukewarm.
1960s and 1970s the Government of India introduced agricultural reforms: the Green Revolution, based on package technology, and the White Revolution (Operation Flood). But these too led to the concentration of development in few selected areas.
1980s and 1990s a comprehensive land development programme with both institutional and technical reforms (section 4).

(Your textbook does not explain "package technology". In an exam, write it as your textbook does: "the Green Revolution based on the use of package technology". Beyond your textbook: it usually means HYV seeds, fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation used together.)

Memory hook: "Land first, then Green and White, then the big programme of the 80s–90s."

  1. The comprehensive land development programme (1980s–1990s)

Measure What it does (your textbook)
Crop insurance protects against drought, flood, cyclone, fire and disease
Grameen banks, cooperative societies and banks give loan facilities to farmers at lower rates of interest
Kissan Credit Card (KCC) a scheme of the Government of India for the benefit of farmers
Personal Accident Insurance Scheme (PAIS) a scheme of the Government of India for the benefit of farmers
Special weather bulletins and agricultural programmes for farmers, on radio and television
Minimum support price, remunerative and procurement prices announced for important crops to check the exploitation of farmers by speculators and middlemen

Spelling note: your textbook prints "Kissan Credit Card"; board papers write "Kisan Card" (Beyond your textbook: the usual spelling is Kisan). Your textbook names KCC and PAIS but does not describe how they work — write only their names and that they are "for the benefit of the farmers".

Kissan Credit Card (KCC) and PAIS: your textbook names them only as "schemes introduced by the Government of India for the benefit of the farmers". List them by name; do not give them as the cure for a problem.

  1. Bhoodan – Gramdan (your textbook's box)

  1. Mahatma Gandhi declared Vinoba Bhave his spiritual heir. Vinoba took part in Satyagraha as one of the foremost satyagrahis and believed in Gandhiji's idea of gram swarajya.
  2. After Gandhiji's martyrdom, Vinoba Bhave went on a padyatra to spread Gandhiji's message, covering almost the entire country.
  3. At Pochampalli in Andhra Pradesh, some poor landless villagers asked him for land. He could not promise it, but said he would talk to the Government of India if they undertook cooperative farming.
  4. Shri Ram Chandra Reddy then offered 80 acres of land to be distributed among 80 landless villagers. This act was known as 'Bhoodan'.
  5. Later, some zamindars, owners of many villages, offered to distribute some villages among the landless. This was known as 'Gramdan'.
  6. Many land-owners gave part of their land to poor farmers due to the fear of the land ceiling act.
  7. The Bhoodan–Gramdan movement, initiated by Vinoba Bhave, is also known as the Blood-less Revolution.

Bhoodan = a gift of land; Gramdan = a gift of villages.

Worked example 1

Sort the reforms

Question. Sort these into "institutional" and "technological" reforms, as we sort them in section 2 (your textbook does not sort each reform): (a) abolition of zamindari; (b) HYV seeds and fertilisers; (c) Grameen banks; (d) consolidation of holdings; (e) irrigation; (f) minimum support price.

Asked: a sort of six reforms.

Concept used: Two kinds of reform (section 2).

  1. 1

    Institutional (rules, ownership, credit, prices): (a), (c), (d), (f).

  2. 2

    Technological (inputs and methods): (b), (e).

  3. Check: the institutional ones change who owns land, who lends, at what price — not the seeds or water.

  4. Answer Institutional: (a), (c), (d), (f). Technological: (b), (e).

    Common trap: putting "minimum support price" under technology. A price rule is institutional.

Worked example 2

A small farmer's five problems

Question. An imagined small farmer, Ramdeen, (1) has three scattered strips of land, (2) loses his crop to a flood, (3) pays a high interest rate to a lender, (4) sells his wheat at a low price to a middleman, and (5) never hears the weather forecast. Suggest one measure from your textbook for each problem. (5 marks)

Asked: five measures, one per problem.

Concept used: Problem → measure (section 5).

  1. 1

    Scattered strips → consolidation of holdings.

  2. 2

    Flood loss → crop insurance (against drought, flood, cyclone, fire and disease).

  3. 3

    High interest → loans at lower rates of interest from Grameen banks, cooperative societies and banks.

  4. 4

    Low price from a middleman → minimum support, remunerative and procurement prices to check exploitation by speculators and middlemen.

  5. 5

    No forecast → special weather bulletins and agricultural programmes on radio and television.

  6. Check: five problems, five measures — not one of them repeats the problem.

  7. Answer Consolidation of holdings; crop insurance; Grameen and cooperative banks; minimum support price; weather bulletins on radio and TV.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Consolidation of holdings would join his scattered strips into one holding. (1)
    2. Crop insurance protects against loss from drought, flood, cyclone, fire and disease. (1)
    3. Grameen banks, cooperative societies and banks give him loans at lower rates of interest. (1)
    4. Minimum support and procurement prices check exploitation by speculators and middlemen. (1)
    5. Special weather bulletins and agricultural programmes on radio and television keep him informed. (1)
    Common trap: writing the problems again ("he is poor", "he has small land"). "Suggest measures" needs cures.

Worked example 3

Both sides of the Green Revolution

Question. "The Green Revolution and the White Revolution improved Indian agriculture, but they did not solve every problem." Explain with your textbook. (3 marks)

Asked: what they were, the benefit, and the limit.

Concept used: Reforms through time (section 3).

  1. 1

    What: in the 1960s and 1970s, the government introduced the Green Revolution (based on package technology) and the White Revolution (Operation Flood).

  2. 2

    Benefit: they were among the strategies to improve the lot of Indian agriculture; the green revolution's success in Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh and parts of Rajasthan helped rabi crops (Topic 3).

  3. 3

    Limit: they led to the concentration of development in few selected areas, so in the 1980s and 1990s a comprehensive land development programme was started.

  4. Check: both sides are in the textbook's own words — "to improve the lot" and "concentration of development in few selected areas".

  5. Answer They improved agriculture (green revolution regions grew more rabi crops), but development was concentrated in a few areas, so a wider programme followed.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. In the 1960s–70s, the Green Revolution (package technology) and White Revolution (Operation Flood) were started to improve agriculture. (1)
    2. The green revolution's success in Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh and parts of Rajasthan helped rabi crops grow. (1)
    3. But development was concentrated in few selected areas, so a comprehensive land development programme followed in the 1980s–90s. (1)
    Common trap: writing "the White Revolution increased wheat". Your textbook calls the White Revolution Operation Flood and links the green revolution to rabi crops. Beyond your textbook: Operation Flood was about milk.

Common mistakes!

  • Institutional and technological reforms mixed.

    Institutional: land, ownership, cooperation, credit, insurance, prices; technological: HYV seeds, fertilisers, irrigation.

    Ask: does it change the rules or the method?

  • "The Green Revolution spread development evenly."

    It led to the concentration of development in few selected areas.

    Give both sides.

  • "Land reform laws worked well everywhere."

    Laws were enacted but implementation was lacking or lukewarm.

    Quote the textbook's limit.

  • "MSP is a cheap loan."

    MSP and procurement prices check exploitation by speculators and middlemen; loans come from Grameen and cooperative banks.

    Match each scheme to its purpose.

  • Bhoodan and Gramdan swapped.

    Bhoodan: 80 acres by Ram Chandra Reddy at Pochampalli; Gramdan: zamindars giving villages.

    Bhoodan = land; Gramdan = villages.

  • Writing problems for "measures taken by the government".

    A cause is not a cure.

    List the government's measures.

Key points

  • Reforms were needed because land was used for centuries without matching techno-institutional changes, most farmers still depend on the monsoon and natural fertility, and agriculture supports more than 60 per cent of the people.
  • After Independence: collectivisation, consolidation of holdings, cooperation, abolition of zamindari; land reform — main focus of the First Five Year Plan; implementation lacking or lukewarm.
  • 1960s–70s: Green Revolution (package technology) and White Revolution (Operation Flood) — but development was concentrated in few selected areas.
  • 1980s–90s: a comprehensive land development programme: crop insurance; Grameen banks, cooperative societies and banks (loans at lower interest); KCC, PAIS; weather bulletins on radio and TV; minimum support, remunerative and procurement prices against speculators and middlemen.
  • Bhoodan–Gramdan: Vinoba Bhave; Pochampalli (Andhra Pradesh); 80 acres for 80 landless villagers (Bhoodan); zamindars gave villages (Gramdan); the Blood-less Revolution.
