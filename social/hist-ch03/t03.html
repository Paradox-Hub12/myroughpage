Topic 3 of 6

Food travels: spaghetti and potato

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain how food shows long-distance cultural exchange — noodles and spaghetti, Arab traders and fifth-century Sicily — and why the truth about some origins may never be known.
  • Name the common foods that reached Europe and Asia from America after Columbus, and the people they came from.
  • Explain how new crops could make the difference between life and death — the potato in Europe and the Irish famine of the 1840s (NCERT Fig. 4).

Think of the last plate of food you enjoyed: perhaps aloo-tamatar sabzi with green chillies, or a packet of masala peanuts. It feels completely Indian. Yet your textbook says that potatoes, tomatoes, chillies and groundnuts were not known to our ancestors until about five centuries ago. They crossed an ocean to reach your plate. Food is one of the best clues to how far people and ideas travelled.

The chain in one line: traders and travellers carry crops and recipes → new foods settle in distant lands → sometimes a new crop changes how long people live — and how they die when it fails.

  1. Food as long-distance cultural exchange

Your textbook says food offers many examples of long-distance cultural exchange:

  • Traders and travellers introduced new crops to the lands they travelled.
  • Even "ready" foodstuff in distant parts of the world might share common origins.

The noodle puzzle. Take spaghetti and noodles:

  1. It is believed that noodles travelled west from China to become spaghetti.
  2. Or, perhaps, Arab traders took pasta to fifth-century Sicily, an island now in Italy.
  3. Similar foods were also known in India and Japan — so the truth about their origins may never be known.

So why does the textbook tell the story at all? Because such guesswork suggests the possibilities of long-distance cultural contact even in the pre-modern world. Notice the careful words — it is believed, perhaps, may never be known. In an answer, keep them: never write that noodles certainly became spaghetti.

  1. Foods from America

Many of our common foods — potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies, sweet potatoes, and so on — were not known to our ancestors until about five centuries ago. They were introduced in Europe and Asia only after Christopher Columbus accidentally discovered the vast continent that would later become known as the Americas.

Point Your textbook's words
How Columbus found it accidentally discovered
What "America" means in this chapter North America, South America and the Caribbean

When they reached Europe and Asia after Columbus — about five centuries ago

Memory hook: "Five centuries, seven foods" — potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies, sweet potatoes.

(Beyond your textbook: historians usually trace soya to East Asia rather than to America. In the board exam, follow your textbook's list.)

  1. The potato: life and death

Your textbook says that sometimes the new crops could make the difference between life and death. The potato shows both sides:

Life Death
With the introduction of the humble potato, Europe's poor began to eat better and live longer. Ireland's poorest peasants became so dependent on potatoes that when disease destroyed the potato crop in the mid-1840s, hundreds of thousands died of starvation.

The danger was dependence: a family that lives on one crop has nothing to fall back on when that crop fails.

  1. Reading sources — NCERT Figs 3 and 4

NCERT Fig. 3 shows merchants from Venice and the Orient exchanging goods, from Marco Polo, Book of Marvels, fifteenth century. It shows merchants of Venice trading with merchants from the East (beyond your textbook: "the Orient" is an old word for the countries of Asia).

Question Answer for Fig. 3
What does it show? Merchants from Venice and the Orient exchanging goods.
What does it tell us? Merchants of Venice traded with merchants from the East: trade linked Europe and Asia.
What is its limit or point of view? It is a picture in a fifteenth-century book: one artist's view of the trade (our reading). It does not tell us which goods, how much, or how often.

NCERT Fig. 4 is a picture of the Irish Potato Famine from the Illustrated London News, 1849: hungry children digging for potatoes in a field that has already been harvested, hoping to find leftovers.

Question Answer for Fig. 4
What does it show? Hungry children searching a harvested field for leftover potatoes, in 1849.
What does it tell us? How desperate families were when the potato, their main food, failed. The caption adds: during the Great Irish Potato Famine (1845 to 1849), around 1,000,000 people died of starvation in Ireland, and double the number emigrated in search of work.
What is its limit or point of view? It was printed in a London magazine: one artist's picture of one scene (our reading: made for readers far away). It does not show the causes of the famine or the whole country.

Two numbers, two places. The main text says "hundreds of thousands died"; the caption of Fig. 4 says "around 1,000,000" died, with twice as many emigrating. Both are in your textbook. If you use a number in an answer, say which one you are using ("the caption of Fig. 4 says…").

Worked examples

Worked example 1

The noodle puzzle

Question. Your textbook says that the truth about the origins of noodles and spaghetti "may never be known". Why can it not be sure, and why does it still tell the story? (2 marks)

Asked: (i) the reason for the doubt; (ii) why the story is still useful.

Concept used: Food as long-distance cultural exchange (this topic, section 1).

  1. 1

    The doubt: there are two possible stories — noodles travelled west from China, or Arab traders took pasta to fifth-century Sicily — and similar foods were also known in India and Japan.

  2. 2

    Why tell it: even this guesswork suggests that long-distance cultural contact was possible in the pre-modern world.

  3. Check: two parts, two points. Test point 1 by asking "would a single clear origin leave any doubt?" — no; the doubt comes from more than one possible story and similar foods elsewhere.

  4. Answer Noodle-like foods are found in several places and there is more than one story of their spread; yet the guesswork shows long-distance contact in the pre-modern world.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Noodles may have gone west from China, or Arab traders may have taken pasta to Sicily; similar foods existed in India and Japan. (1)
    2. Even this guesswork suggests long-distance cultural contact in the pre-modern world. (1)
    Common trap: writing "noodles travelled from China and became spaghetti" as a fact. The textbook says "it is believed" and "perhaps".

Worked example 2

Which ingredients crossed the ocean?

Question. Meena's lunch box has rice, a wheat roti, potato curry with tomatoes, a little lemon pickle, a green chilli and some roasted groundnuts. Which of these does your textbook name among the foods not known to our ancestors until about five centuries ago, and where did they come from? (2 marks) (Meena is imagined; the foods named in the answer are from your textbook.)

Asked: the items on the textbook's list; their origin.

Concept used: Foods from America (this topic, section 2).

  1. 1

    Check each item against the textbook's list (potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies, sweet potatoes): potato, tomatoes, chilli and groundnuts are on it. Rice, wheat and lemon are not on the list — the textbook says nothing about them here.

  2. 2

    Origin: these foods came from America's original inhabitants, the American Indians, and reached Europe and Asia after Columbus accidentally discovered America.

  3. Check: every item you choose must be on the textbook's list. Choosing "rice" fails the Check at once — it is not on the list.

  4. Answer Potato, tomatoes, chilli and groundnuts — from the American Indians of America, after Columbus.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The potato, tomatoes, chilli and groundnuts in her lunch box are foods our ancestors did not know until about five centuries ago. (1)
    2. They came from America's original inhabitants, the American Indians, after Columbus accidentally discovered America. (1)
    Common trap: adding rice or wheat. The textbook's list is the test — and neither is on it.

Worked example 3

A blessing, then a disaster

Question. "For Europe's poor the potato was a blessing; for Ireland's poorest it became a disaster." Explain both parts of this statement. (3 marks)

Asked: why the potato helped; why it later caused disaster.

Concept used: The potato: life and death (this topic, section 3).

  1. 1

    Blessing: with the introduction of the potato, Europe's poor began to eat better and live longer.

  2. 2

    Dependence: Ireland's poorest peasants became so dependent on potatoes that they had little else to eat.

  3. 3

    Disaster: when disease destroyed the potato crop in the mid-1840s, hundreds of thousands died of starvation.

  4. Check: the statement has two parts; the answer gives the blessing (point 1) and the disaster with its cause (points 2–3). Without point 2, point 3 would not explain why a crop failure killed so many.

  5. Answer The potato fed Europe's poor better and helped them live longer, but Ireland's poorest depended on it so much that the crop's failure in the mid-1840s starved hundreds of thousands.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. With the introduction of the potato, Europe's poor began to eat better and live longer. (1)
    2. Ireland's poorest peasants became so dependent on potatoes that they had little else to fall back on. (1)
    3. When disease destroyed the potato crop in the mid-1840s, hundreds of thousands died of starvation. (1)
    Common trap: giving drought or war as the cause. The crop was destroyed by disease.

According to one possible story in your textbook, who may have taken pasta to Sicily in the fifth century?

Many of our common foods came from America's original inhabitants. What does your textbook call them?

The Caribbean is part of "America" in this chapter, but the textbook does not speak of "Caribbean traders".
"Many of our common foods came from America's original inhabitants — the American Indians."
The Spanish came later, as conquerors (Topic 4).
Dissenters were people who fled Europe for America (Topic 5).
Quick check · 3 of 3MCQ1 mark

Why did hundreds of thousands of Irish peasants die of starvation in the mid-1840s?

Dependence on one crop + disease destroying it = starvation.
The poorest were already so dependent on potatoes that their failure was a disaster.
Your textbook names disease, not war.
The opposite: they depended on potatoes.

Common mistakes!

  • "Noodles certainly travelled from China and became spaghetti."

    The textbook says it is believed, perhaps, and the truth may never be known.

    Keep the doubt words in your answer.

  • "Rice and wheat came from America."

    They are not on the textbook's list.

    Learn the seven: potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies, sweet potatoes.

  • "Columbus set out to find America."

    He accidentally discovered it.

    Write accidentally discovered.

  • "The Irish famine was caused by war (or drought)."

    Your textbook says disease destroyed the potato crop.

    Cause = disease + dependence.

  • Mixing the famine numbers.

    Text: hundreds of thousands died; Fig. 4 caption: around 1,000,000 died, double that number emigrated (1845–1849).

    Say which one you use.

Key points

  • Food offers many examples of long-distance cultural exchange: traders and travellers introduced new crops; even ready foods may share common origins.
  • Noodles and spaghetti: it is believed noodles went west from China; or perhaps Arab traders took pasta to fifth-century Sicily; similar foods were known in India and Japan — the truth may never be known, but the guesswork shows long-distance contact.
  • Potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies, sweet potatoes were not known to our ancestors until about five centuries ago; they reached Europe and Asia after Columbus accidentally discovered America, from America's original inhabitants — the American Indians.
  • New crops could make the difference between life and death: the potato helped Europe's poor eat better and live longer; Ireland's poorest became so dependent on it that when disease destroyed the crop in the mid-1840s, hundreds of thousands died of starvation.
