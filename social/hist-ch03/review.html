How this works

Each set comes back to you after a gap: about 1 day, 1 week and 1 month after you finish the chapter. Answer from memory first — the effort of remembering is what makes it stick. Then check. Any question you get wrong is a sign to reread that topic. All sets are on the board-exam part of the chapter (Topics 1–5).

Week 1

Set 2 — Week 1

5 questions, about 8 minutes

Focus: the reasons behind the facts.

W1.1 · 1 of 5●● Standard

Why does a ship carved on a tenth-century memorial stone matter to a historian of trade?

Answer: Ships appear regularly on memorial stones of the western coast from the ninth century, which shows the significance of oceanic trade for the people there. (Topic 1)

Did you get it?

W1.2 · 2 of 5●● Standard

Gold and silver flowed from Europe to Asia along the silk routes. What does your textbook say this flow was for?

Answer: It flowed "in return" for the goods that came west from Asia — silk, pottery, textiles and spices. (Topic 2)

Did you get it?

W1.3 · 3 of 5●● Standard

Why did the failure of one crop kill so many people in Ireland in the 1840s?

Answer: Ireland's poorest peasants had become so dependent on potatoes that when disease destroyed the crop, they had nothing else, and hundreds of thousands starved. (Topic 3)

Did you get it?

W1.4 · 4 of 5●● Standard

Smallpox killed people in parts of America that no European had yet reached. How?

Answer: Once introduced, smallpox spread deep into the continent, ahead even of any Europeans; the people had no immunity to it. (Topic 4)

Did you get it?

W1.5 · 5 of 5●● Standard

Why did religious dissenters in Europe have a special reason to leave for America?

Answer: Religious conflicts were common in Europe and dissenters were persecuted; so they, like thousands of others, fled to America. (Topic 5)

Month 1

Set 3 — Month 1

5 questions, about 12 minutes

Focus: linking topics — the kind of thinking that 2- and 3-mark answers need.

M1.1 · 1 of 5●●● Advanced

Precious metals moved between continents twice in this chapter. Name both movements and say what each paid for.

Answer: (1) On the silk routes, gold and silver flowed from Europe to Asia in return for Asia's goods; (2) from the sixteenth century, silver from mines in Peru and Mexico enhanced Europe's wealth and financed its trade with Asia. Both times the precious metal was tied to Europe's trade with Asia. (Topics 2 and 4)

Did you get it?

M1.2 · 2 of 5●●● Advanced

Germs appear twice in this chapter — in Topic 1 and in Topic 4. What is the difference between the two?

Answer: Topic 1: germs carried over vast distances by travellers and traders — a link traced to the seventh century and an unmistakable link by the thirteenth. Topic 4: a weapon against a people with no immunity — smallpox carried by the Spanish into America in the sixteenth century, which decimated communities and paved the way for conquest. (Topics 1 and 4)

Did you get it?

M1.3 · 3 of 5●●● Advanced

Columbus "accidentally discovered" America. Give one thing that went out of America and one thing that came into it as a result.

Answer: Out: foods such as potatoes, maize, tomatoes and chillies (to Europe and Asia) — or silver (to Europe). In: germs such as smallpox — or people fleeing Europe, or slaves captured in Africa. (Topics 3–5)

Did you get it?

M1.4 · 4 of 5●●● Advanced

"The Indian subcontinent was central to trade, yet Europe became the centre of world trade." How can both be true?

Answer: The subcontinent was central to the Indian Ocean flows for centuries before the sixteenth century, and China and India were among the richest countries until well into the eighteenth century. But Europeans redirected some flows towards Europe, China retreated, and the Americas rose — so the centre of world trade moved westwards to Europe. (Topics 4 and 5)

Did you get it?

M1.5 · 5 of 5●●● Advanced

Your textbook says Christian missionaries "almost certainly" used the silk routes and that the truth about noodles "may never be known". What do these careful phrases teach you about writing history answers?

Answer: Write what the evidence shows, and how sure we can be: keep the textbook's words of doubt ("almost certainly", "perhaps", "is said to have") instead of turning a likelihood into a fact. (Topics 2 and 3)

Did you get it?
