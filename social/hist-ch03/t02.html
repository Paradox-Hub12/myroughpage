Topic 2 of 6

Silk routes link the world

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why the silk routes are a good example of vibrant pre-modern trade and cultural links, and describe them — several routes over land and by sea, from before the Christian Era to almost the fifteenth century.
  • Say what travelled west along the silk routes (silk, pottery, textiles and spices) and what flowed east (gold and silver).
  • Explain how trade and cultural exchange went hand in hand on the silk routes, and read NCERT Fig. 2.

Picture a bale of shining Chinese silk loaded on to a camel, a boat, then another camel — and, many months later, sold in a market thousands of kilometres to the west. Now picture the same caravan also carrying bowls of fine pottery, rolls of cloth and sacks of spices — and, travelling with the traders, a monk carrying new ideas. That mix of goods and beliefs is the story of the silk routes.

The chain in one line: valuable goods (silk, pottery, textiles, spices) move west and precious metals move east → traders, missionaries and preachers use the same routes → trade and culture spread together, knitting distant regions into one network.

  1. What were the silk routes?

Your textbook calls the silk routes a good example of vibrant pre-modern trade and cultural links between distant parts of the world. Learn four facts about them:

Question Your textbook's answer
Why the name? It points to the importance of West-bound Chinese silk cargoes along the route.
One road or many? Several silk routes, over land and by sea, identified by historians.
Which regions did they join? They knitted together vast regions of Asia, and linked Asia with Europe and northern Africa.
How long did they last? They existed since before the Christian Era and thrived almost till the fifteenth century.

(Beyond your textbook: "the Christian Era" is the same as the Common Era, CE; "before the Christian Era" means BCE.)

  1. What moved, and which way?

The name tells only part of the story. Many goods travelled the same routes:

Moving west (from Asia towards Europe) Moving east (from Europe to Asia)
Silk from China Precious metals — gold and silver
Chinese pottery —
Textiles and spices from India and Southeast Asia —

Your textbook says the gold and silver flowed from Europe to Asia "in return" — that is (our reading of "in return"), as payment for the goods of Asia.

Memory hook: S-P-T-S go west, G-S go east — Silk, Pottery, Textiles, Spices west; Gold and Silver east.

  1. Trade and culture went hand in hand

Your textbook says "Trade and cultural exchange always went hand in hand." The same routes that carried goods carried faiths and ideas:

In time order What happened on the silk routes
First — "much before all this" Buddhism emerged from eastern India and spread in several directions through intersecting points on the silk routes.
Then Early Christian missionaries almost certainly travelled this route to Asia.
"A few centuries later" Early Muslim preachers travelled it too.

Two careful words to keep: Buddhism began in eastern India and spread outwards — it did not come to India along the routes; and the textbook says the missionaries "almost certainly" used the route — it is a strong likelihood, not a proven fact.

New word. Intersecting points — places where two or more routes cross (our definition); traders, goods and ideas could meet there.

  1. Reading a source — NCERT Fig. 2

NCERT Fig. 2 shows silk route trade as depicted in a Chinese cave painting, eighth century, Cave 217, Mogao Grottoes, Gansu, China.

Question Answer
What does it show? A painting on the wall of a cave in China, from the eighth century, showing trade on the silk route.
What does it tell us? (Our reading) The silk route trade was part of life in China in the eighth century — enough to be shown in a cave painting.
What is its limit or point of view? A painting shows how an artist pictured the trade. It does not tell us how much was traded, what prices were paid, or which exact route was used.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Two caravans, two directions

Question. Two caravans met at a crossing point on the silk routes: one was heading west towards Europe, the other east towards China. Using your textbook, say what kinds of goods each would have been carrying, and why one of them carried metal. (3 marks)

Asked: (i) the westbound goods; (ii) the eastbound goods; (iii) the reason for the metal.

Concept used: What moved, and which way (this topic, section 2).

  1. 1

    Westbound: silk and pottery from China, and textiles and spices from India and Southeast Asia.

  2. 2

    Eastbound: the precious metals gold and silver, from Europe to Asia.

  3. 3

    Why metal: the gold and silver went "in return" — as payment for Asia's goods (our reading of "in return").

  4. Check: the question has three parts; the answer has a westbound list, an eastbound list and a reason. Swap the two lists and the Check fails at once: the textbook says the metals flowed from Europe to Asia.

  5. Answer West: silk, pottery, textiles and spices. East: gold and silver, which flowed in return for Asia's goods.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. The westbound caravan carried Chinese silk and pottery, and textiles and spices from India and Southeast Asia. (1)
    2. The eastbound caravan carried precious metals — gold and silver — from Europe to Asia. (1)
    3. These precious metals flowed in return for the silk, pottery, textiles and spices that Asia sent west. (1)
    Common trap: writing "spices from China". Your textbook says the textiles and spices came from India and Southeast Asia.

Worked example 2

Why "silk"?

Question. Pottery, textiles and spices also moved along these routes. Why, then, are they called the "silk routes"? (2 marks)

Asked: the reason for the name, given that other goods travelled too.

Concept used: What were the silk routes? (this topic, section 1).

  1. 1

    The reason for the name: it points to the importance of West-bound Chinese silk cargoes along the routes.

  2. 2

    But the name is only part of the story: Chinese pottery, and textiles and spices from India and Southeast Asia, used the same routes, and gold and silver came back the other way.

  3. Check: point 1 gives the reason; point 2 shows you understood the "then" in the question — that more than silk travelled. An answer that just lists goods, without the reason, misses the first mark.

  4. Answer The name points to the importance of China's west-bound silk cargoes, even though many other goods used the routes.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The name points to the importance of the west-bound Chinese silk cargoes carried along these routes. (1)
    2. Yet Chinese pottery and Indian and Southeast Asian textiles and spices also travelled them, with gold and silver coming back. (1)
    Common trap: "because only silk was traded". The point of the question is that much more than silk was traded.

Worked example 3

Faiths on the move, in order

Question. Arrange in the order your textbook gives them, earliest first: I. Early Muslim preachers travel the silk routes. II. Buddhism spreads from eastern India through points on the silk routes. III. The silk routes are still thriving, near the end of their great age. IV. Early Christian missionaries almost certainly travel the route to Asia.

Asked: the order of the four events.

Concept used: Trade and culture went hand in hand (section 3) and the dates in section 1.

  1. 1

    Use the textbook's time words: Buddhism — "much before all this"; Christian missionaries — "early"; Muslim preachers — "a few centuries later"; the routes thrived "almost till the fifteenth century".

  2. 2

    Sort: II → IV → I → III.

  3. Check: the phrase "a few centuries later" ties I to after IV; "much before all this" puts II before both. III is the end of the routes' great age, so it must be last.

  4. Answer II, IV, I, III

    Common trap: putting the Christian missionaries first because they are named first in the paragraph. The textbook's words "much before all this" put Buddhism first.

Quick check · 3 of 3MCQ1 mark

For how long did the silk routes thrive, according to your textbook?

They existed since before the Christian Era and thrived almost till the fifteenth century.
The sixteenth century is when sea routes to Asia and America "shrank" the world (Topic 4).
The thirteenth century is the date for germs becoming an unmistakable link (Topic 1).
"The last 50 years or so" is the usual meaning of "globalisation" (Topic 1).

Common mistakes!

  • "The silk route was one road across the desert."

    Historians have identified several routes, over land and by sea.

    Write: several silk routes, over land and by sea.

  • "Only silk was traded."

    Pottery, textiles and spices used the same routes; gold and silver came back.

    Give two goods besides silk.

  • "Gold and silver went from Asia to Europe."

    They flowed from Europe to Asia, in return for Asia's goods.

    Remember S-P-T-S go west, G-S go east.

  • "Buddhism came to India along the silk routes."

    It emerged from eastern India and spread outwards, much before the missionaries and preachers.

    Write from eastern India, in several directions.

  • "The missionaries certainly used the route."

    Your textbook says "almost certainly".

    Keep the textbook's careful words.

  • Giving one example when the question says "explain with any two examples".

    Each example earns one mark; one example can earn only one.

    Write two numbered points, each with its own example.

Key points

  • The silk routes were a good example of vibrant pre-modern trade and cultural links between distant parts of the world; the name points to west-bound Chinese silk cargoes.
  • Several routes, over land and by sea, joined vast regions of Asia and linked Asia with Europe and northern Africa, from before the Christian Era till almost the fifteenth century.
  • West: silk and pottery from China; textiles and spices from India and Southeast Asia. East: gold and silver from Europe, in return.
  • Trade and cultural exchange always went hand in hand: Buddhism spread from eastern India through intersecting points on the routes (much before the others); early Christian missionaries almost certainly travelled to Asia; early Muslim preachers a few centuries later.
  • NCERT Fig. 2: silk route trade in an eighth-century Chinese cave painting, Cave 217, Mogao Grottoes, Gansu.
