The whole chapter on one page

The pre-modern world — "Globalisation" usually means the economic system of the last 50 years or so, but the global world has a long history of trade, migration, people in search of work and the movement of capital. Travellers, traders, priests and pilgrims travelled vast distances for knowledge, opportunity, spiritual fulfilment or to escape persecution, carrying goods, money, values, skills, ideas, inventions — and germs and diseases. 3000 BCE: coastal trade, Indus valley ↔ present-day West Asia. Cowries (cowdi, seashell currency) from the Maldives → China and East Africa for more than a millennium. Germs: traced to the seventh century, an unmistakable link by the thirteenth. Fig. 1: ship on a tenth-century memorial stone, Goa Museum; ships on such stones from the ninth century → oceanic trade mattered.

Silk routes — a good example of vibrant pre-modern trade and cultural links; the name = west-bound Chinese silk; several routes, over land and by sea; Asia ↔ Europe and northern Africa; before the Christian Era → almost the fifteenth century. West: silk, Chinese pottery, textiles and spices from India and Southeast Asia. East: gold and silver from Europe, in return. Trade and cultural exchange always went hand in hand: Buddhism from eastern India (much before) → early Christian missionaries (almost certainly) → early Muslim preachers (a few centuries later). Fig. 2: eighth-century cave painting, Cave 217, Mogao Grottoes, Gansu.

Food travels — traders and travellers introduced new crops; noodles believed to have gone west from China to become spaghetti, or perhaps Arab traders took pasta to fifth-century Sicily; similar foods in India and Japan → origins may never be known. Potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies, sweet potatoes — unknown to our ancestors until about five centuries ago; reached Europe and Asia after Columbus accidentally discovered America; from the American Indians. Potato: Europe's poor ate better and lived longer; Ireland's poorest depended on it → disease destroyed the crop in the mid-1840s → hundreds of thousands starved. Fig. 3: Venice and the Orient, Marco Polo, Book of Marvels. Fig. 4: Irish Potato Famine (1849; caption: 1845–1849, around 1,000,000 died, double emigrated).

Conquest, disease and trade — the world shrank in the sixteenth century: sea route to Asia; crossing of the western ocean to America. The Indian Ocean had bustled for centuries before; the Indian subcontinent was central; Europeans expanded or redirected some flows towards Europe. America, cut off for millions of years, now transformed trade: silver from present-day Peru and Mexico enhanced Europe's wealth and financed its trade with Asia; seventeenth-century legends → expeditions in search of El Dorado, the fabled city of gold. Portuguese and Spanish conquest under way by the mid-sixteenth century; germs such as smallpox, carried by the Spanish, were their most powerful weapon → no immunity → spread ahead of the Europeans → communities decimated → conquest. Guns could be captured; germs could not. Box 1 (May 1634): Winthorp (Massachusetts Bay) — smallpox "God's blessing" — one colonist's view.

Europe at the centre — until the nineteenth century: poverty, hunger, crowded cities, deadly diseases, religious conflict, dissenters persecuted → thousands fled Europe for America. By the eighteenth century: plantations worked by slaves captured in Africa grew cotton and sugar for European markets (Fig. 5: slaves for sale, New Orleans, 1851). China and India among the richest until well into the eighteenth century, pre-eminent in Asian trade; from the fifteenth century China is said to have restricted overseas contacts; China's reduced role + the Americas' rise → centre of world trade moves westwards → Europe the centre.

Key terms (as in your textbook — learn the words in bold)

  • Dissenter: one who refuses to accept established beliefs and practices (textbook "New words").
  • Cowries: seashells (Hindi cowdi) used as a form of currency.
  • Silk routes: several routes over land and by sea, named for west-bound Chinese silk cargoes.
  • America (in this chapter): North America, South America and the Caribbean.
  • Immunity (our definition): the body's power to resist a disease.
  • El Dorado: the fabled city of gold.
