For your project (not in the board exam): the world economy, 1815 to the 1970s

About 20 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Outline the four parts of the rest of the chapter — the nineteenth-century world economy, the inter-war economy, the post-war era and the end of Bretton Woods — with their key dates and terms.
  • Use the three flows (trade, labour, capital) to plan the interdisciplinary project.

Not in the board exam. The 2026-27 syllabus examines only Topics 1–5 of this chapter (your textbook's sections 1 to 1.3). The rest of the chapter — sections 2 to 4.4, from 1815 to the end of the Bretton Woods system — is for an interdisciplinary project that your school marks itself (5 marks of internal assessment), together with Geography Chapter 7 (Lifelines of National Economy) and Economics Chapter 4 (Globalisation and the Indian Economy). Read this page when you start the project. Do not use these facts in a board answer about the pre-modern world.

  1. The big idea — three flows

Your textbook says economists see three types of movement, or "flows", in international economic exchanges:

Flow What it means One example from your textbook
Trade mostly trade in goods (cloth, wheat) Food production expanded in Eastern Europe, Russia, America and Australia to meet Britain's demand
Labour migration of people in search of employment Nearly 50 million people emigrated from Europe to America and Australia in the nineteenth century
Capital money moved for short-term or long-term investment over long distances Capital flowed from financial centres such as London to build railways, ports and farms

The three were closely interwoven — but labour migration was often more restricted than goods or capital.

  1. The four parts at a glance

Part (textbook section) Years Key ideas and terms
The nineteenth century (§2) 1815–1914 Corn Laws abolished → food imports into Britain; a global agricultural economy by 1890; railways, steamships, the telegraph, refrigerated ships; the carving up of Africa (Berlin, 1885); rinderpest (cattle plague) in Africa in the 1890s; indentured labour from India (abolished 1921); Indian bankers and traders abroad; India's trade surplus and the "home charges"
The inter-war economy (§3) 1914 to the Second World War The First World War (1914–18), the first modern industrial war; the US turns from debtor to creditor; mass production (Henry Ford's assembly line, the T-Model); the Great Depression (about 1929 to the mid-1930s) and its effect on India (jute growers, gold exports)
Rebuilding the world economy (§4.1–4.3) from the Second World War The Second World War; two lessons from the inter-war years; the Bretton Woods conference (July 1944): the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank — the "Bretton Woods twins"; fixed exchange rates; multinational corporations (MNCs) (Box 4); decolonisation and the Group of 77 (G-77) demand for a new international economic order (NIEO)
The end of Bretton Woods (§4.4) from the 1960s The US dollar can no longer keep its value in relation to gold → floating exchange rates; debt crises in developing countries from the mid-1970s; MNCs move production to low-wage Asian countries; China, India and Brazil transform

  1. Key terms from these sections (your textbook's "New words" boxes)

  • Indentured labour — a bonded labourer under contract to work for an employer for a specific amount of time, to pay off his passage to a new country or home.
  • Tariff — tax imposed on a country's imports from the rest of the world, levied at the point of entry.
  • Exchange rates — they link national currencies for purposes of international trade; fixed (governments intervene to prevent movements) or floating (fluctuate with the demand and supply of currencies).

  1. How the project is organised

The syllabus suggests a 10-day plan (your teacher may change it): an introduction with the Second World War and its aftermath; the Great Depression; India and the Great Depression; rebuilding the world economy and the Bretton Woods institutions; the early post-war years and transport; decolonisation; the end of Bretton Woods and the beginning of globalisation; the impact of globalisation in India; and a final presentation. It is done in school, under your teacher's guidance.

Your textbook's own questions on these sections — the Activities, Discuss boxes, Write in brief Q3–Q5, Discuss Q6–Q9 and the Project — are answered on the NCERT solutions page, in the group "For your project — not in the board exam".

Common mistakes!

  • Writing about the Corn Laws, rinderpest or indentured labour in a board answer on the pre-modern world.

    These belong to the project part (1815 onwards), not to Topics 1–5.

    For the board exam, use only Topics 1–5.

  • Mixing the slaves of Topic 5 with the indentured labourers of the project part.

    Slaves were captured in Africa for America's plantations (by the eighteenth century); indentured labourers went under contract from India in the nineteenth century.

    Keep the two apart: captured — Africa; contract — India.

Key points

  • Not in the board exam: sections 2 to 4.4 of the chapter are an interdisciplinary project (5 marks, school-assessed) with Geography Chapter 7 and Economics Chapter 4.
  • Three flows: trade (goods), labour (migration for work), capital (investment over long distances).
  • Four parts: the nineteenth century (1815–1914); the inter-war economy (First World War, mass production, Great Depression); rebuilding after the Second World War (Bretton Woods, 1944: IMF and World Bank, fixed exchange rates); the end of Bretton Woods (floating rates, MNCs in Asia).
