Topic 4 of 6

Conquest, disease and trade: the world shrinks

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain what it means that the pre-modern world "shrank" in the sixteenth century, and the place of the Indian Ocean trade and the Indian subcontinent in it.
  • Explain how America's lands, crops and minerals began to transform trade — silver from Peru and Mexico, and the legends of El Dorado.
  • Explain why germs, not guns, were the most powerful weapon of the Spanish conquerors, and read Box 1.

Imagine a village in sixteenth-century America. No stranger has arrived yet — no ships, no soldiers. But people are falling ill with a disease nobody there has ever seen, and whole families are dying. The invaders are still far away, yet their most powerful weapon has already arrived. This topic explains how that could happen, and how the same century shrank the world.

The chain in one line: European sailors find a sea route to Asia and cross the ocean to America → the world "shrinks", and America's silver and crops pour into world trade → germs carried by the conquerors kill America's original inhabitants, who had no immunity → conquest.

  1. The world "shrank" in the sixteenth century

Your textbook says the pre-modern world shrank greatly in the sixteenth century after European sailors:

  1. found a sea route to Asia, and
  2. successfully crossed the western ocean to America.

"Shrank" does not mean the earth got smaller. It means that distant places became connected — goods, people, crops, silver and germs could now move between continents that had been far apart.

The Indian Ocean before the Europeans. For centuries before this, the Indian Ocean had known a bustling trade, with goods, people, knowledge and customs criss-crossing its waters. The Indian subcontinent was central to these flows and a crucial point in their networks. The Europeans did not start this trade: their entry helped expand or redirect some of these flows towards Europe.

  1. America joins the world

Before its "discovery", America had been cut off from regular contact with the rest of the world for millions of years. (The textbook puts 'discovery' in quotation marks: America was not empty — its original inhabitants lived there.) From the sixteenth century, America's vast lands and abundant crops and minerals began to transform trade and lives everywhere.

Silver. Precious metals, particularly silver, from mines in present-day Peru and Mexico:

  • enhanced Europe's wealth, and
  • financed its trade with Asia.

El Dorado. In seventeenth-century Europe, legends spread about South America's fabled wealth. Many expeditions set off in search of El Dorado, the fabled city of gold. "Fabled" means it existed in stories — the expeditions went in search of it.

  1. Conquest: germs, not guns

The Portuguese and Spanish conquest and colonisation of America was decisively under way by the mid-sixteenth century. Your textbook makes a striking point: European conquest was not just a result of superior firepower. The most powerful weapon of the Spanish conquerors was not a conventional military weapon at all. It was the germs, such as those of smallpox, that they carried on their person.

Step What happened
Cause Because of their long isolation, America's original inhabitants had no immunity against these diseases that came from Europe.
Event Smallpox in particular proved a deadly killer. Once introduced, it spread deep into the continent, ahead even of any Europeans reaching there.
Effect It killed and decimated whole communities, paving the way for conquest.

Why germs were worse than guns. Guns could be bought or captured and turned against the invaders. Diseases such as smallpox could not — and the conquerors were mostly immune to them.

Memory hook: "Guns can be captured; germs cannot."

New words.

  • Immunity — the body's power to resist a disease (our definition). America's people had none against Europe's diseases because they had been cut off for so long.
  • Decimated — killed in very large numbers (our definition).
  • Conventional weapon — an ordinary weapon of war, such as a gun or a sword (our definition).

  1. Reading a source — Box 1, "'Biological' warfare?"

Box 1 quotes John Winthorp, the first governor of the Massachusetts Bay colony in New England. In May 1634 he wrote that smallpox signalled God's blessing for the colonists: "… the natives … were neere (near) all dead of small Poxe (pox), so as the Lord hathe (had) cleared our title to what we possess". (The words in brackets are your textbook's modern spellings of his old ones.) The box is taken from Alfred Crosby's book Ecological Imperialism. (Beyond your textbook: the governor's name is usually spelt Winthrop; your textbook prints "Winthorp".)

Question Answer
What does it show? A colonist's own words, written in 1634, about the deaths of the original inhabitants from smallpox.
What does it tell us? Smallpox had killed nearly all "the natives" (our reading: those near the colony), and the colonists saw this as clearing the way for them to possess the land.
What is its limit or point of view? It is one colonist's view: he calls the disaster a "blessing" because it helped his side. We do not hear the original inhabitants' own voices. The box title asks "'Biological' warfare?" with a question mark — your textbook does not say the germs were spread on purpose; it says the conquerors carried them.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Cause or effect?

Question. Sort these four statements into the cause of the world's "shrinking" in the sixteenth century and its effects: (i) European sailors crossed the western ocean to America. (ii) Some Indian Ocean flows were redirected towards Europe. (iii) European sailors found a sea route to Asia. (iv) America's silver began to finance Europe's trade with Asia.

Asked: which statements are causes and which are effects.

Concept used: The world "shrank" (this topic, sections 1–2).

  1. 1

    Causes — the voyages: (i) and (iii). Your textbook says the world shrank after sailors found the sea route to Asia and crossed to America.

  2. 2

    Effects — what the new links did: (ii) the entry of the Europeans expanded or redirected some flows towards Europe; (iv) silver from Peru and Mexico financed Europe's trade with Asia.

  3. Check: ask of each statement, "Could this happen before the voyages?" Redirecting Indian Ocean flows and shipping American silver both needed the new sea routes, so they cannot be causes.

  4. Answer Causes: (i), (iii). Effects: (ii), (iv).

    Common trap: calling the Indian Ocean trade itself an effect. It had bustled for centuries before; only some of its flows were redirected.

Worked example 2

Why guns did not protect them

Question. America's original inhabitants could capture guns and turn them against the invaders, yet they could not defend themselves against conquest. Explain why, in three points. (3 marks)

Asked: why the inhabitants lost, even though guns could be captured.

Concept used: Conquest: germs, not guns (this topic, section 3). Why it applies: the question repeats the textbook's own contrast between guns and germs.

  1. 1

    No immunity: because of their long isolation, America's original inhabitants had no immunity against diseases that came from Europe.

  2. 2

    Smallpox spread ahead: the Spanish conquerors carried germs such as smallpox; once introduced, it spread deep into the continent, ahead even of any Europeans, killing and decimating whole communities.

  3. 3

    Germs could not be captured: guns could be bought or captured and turned against the invaders, but diseases could not, and the conquerors were mostly immune — so disease paved the way for conquest.

  4. Check: each point must be a reason, joined to the next by "so". Point 3 answers the "yet" in the question; an answer that only describes smallpox would leave out why captured guns did not help.

  5. Answer They had no immunity; smallpox spread ahead of the Europeans and decimated communities; and unlike guns, germs could not be captured, while the conquerors were mostly immune.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Long isolation meant America's original inhabitants had no immunity against diseases from Europe. (1)
    2. Smallpox germs carried by the Spanish spread deep into the continent, ahead of the Europeans, decimating whole communities. (1)
    3. Guns could be captured and turned against invaders, but not diseases, to which the conquerors were mostly immune. (1)
    Common trap: writing "the Spanish had better guns". The textbook says conquest was not just a result of superior firepower.

Worked example 3

Reading Box 1

Question. Read Box 1. (a) Why did the governor of the Massachusetts Bay colony call smallpox "God's blessing"? (b) Why do we need to be careful when using his words as evidence? (2 marks)

Asked: (a) his reason; (b) the limit of the source.

Concept used: Reading a source — Box 1 (this topic, section 4).

  1. 1

    (a) Smallpox had killed nearly all "the natives" (our reading: those near the colony), so he believed it had cleared the colonists' title to the land they possessed.

  2. 2

    (b) It is one colonist's view, written for his own side; the people who died are not heard.

  3. Check: (b) must be a limit of the source, not another fact about smallpox. "Smallpox was a deadly killer" is true but does not answer (b).

  4. Answer (a) The deaths cleared the land for the colonists. (b) It gives only the colonists' side.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    (a) Smallpox had killed nearly all the natives, so he believed God had cleared the colonists' title to the land. (1)
    (b) It is one colonist's view in his own favour; the original inhabitants' experience is not heard. (1)

    Common trap: writing that the colonists spread smallpox deliberately. The box title is a question — "'Biological' warfare?" — and the textbook says the germs were carried.

Common mistakes!

  • "The Spanish conquered America because they had better guns."

    Conquest was not just superior firepower; the most powerful weapon was germs.

    Write: no immunity → smallpox → communities decimated → conquest.

  • "The Spanish spread smallpox on purpose."

    Your textbook says the germs were carried on their person; Box 1's title is a question.

    Use the word carried.

  • "Smallpox came from America to Europe."

    The diseases came from Europe into America.

    Direction: Europe → America.

  • "Europeans started the Indian Ocean trade."

    It had bustled for centuries before; the Indian subcontinent was central.

    Europeans expanded or redirected some flows.

  • "El Dorado was a rich city the Spanish found."

    It was the fabled city of gold; expeditions went in search of it.

    Write fabled and legends.

  • "The silver came from Spain."

    It came from mines in present-day Peru and Mexico.

    Learn Peru and Mexico → Europe's wealth → trade with Asia.

Key points

  • The pre-modern world shrank greatly in the sixteenth century after European sailors found a sea route to Asia and crossed the western ocean to America.
  • For centuries before, the Indian Ocean had a bustling trade in goods, people, knowledge and customs; the Indian subcontinent was central to it; Europeans expanded or redirected some flows towards Europe.
  • America, cut off for millions of years, began from the sixteenth century to transform trade and lives everywhere: silver from present-day Peru and Mexico enhanced Europe's wealth and financed its trade with Asia; seventeenth-century legends sent expeditions in search of El Dorado, the fabled city of gold.
  • The Portuguese and Spanish conquest of America was decisively under way by the mid-sixteenth century; the most powerful weapon of the Spanish was germs such as smallpox, carried on their person.
  • Long isolation → no immunity → smallpox spread ahead of the Europeans and decimated whole communities, paving the way for conquest. Guns could be captured; germs could not.
  • Box 1 (1634): John Winthorp of the Massachusetts Bay colony called smallpox God's blessing for the colonists — one colonist's view (from Alfred Crosby, Ecological Imperialism).
