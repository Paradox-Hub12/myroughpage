Wrap-up

The pre-modern world on one page

About 25 minutes · 5 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Give the textbook's early examples of links with their dates.
  • Say what travelled west and east along the silk routes.
  • Name the foods that reached Europe and Asia from America.
  • Explain why germs, not guns, were the conquerors' most powerful weapon.
  • Explain how the centre of world trade moved westwards.

Board questions on this chapter come in two main shapes: a 1-mark item (an MCQ, an assertion–reason or a fill in the blank) and a 2-mark "explain with two examples" answer. Both need the same three things: the order of events, what travelled which way, and why it happened. This page puts the board part of the chapter (Topics 1–5) on four cards and works through four mixed questions. Topic 6 is for your project and is not used here.

Card 1 — The timeline, 3000 BCE to 1851

Remember the order by… Sequence
The early links 3000 BCE Indus–West Asia → seventh century germs traced → ninth century ships on stones → thirteenth century germs unmistakable
The silk routes' faiths Buddhism → Christian missionaries → Muslim preachers ("a few centuries later")
The sixteenth century sea routes → America joins → conquest by the mid-century → silver to Europe
After seventeenth century El Dorado → eighteenth century plantations → 1845–1849 Irish famine

Card 2 — What travelled, and which way?

What From To
Silk; pottery (Topic 2) China westwards, towards Europe
Textiles and spices (Topic 2) India and Southeast Asia westwards
Gold and silver (Topic 2) Europe Asia ("in return")
Buddhism (Topic 2) eastern India in several directions
Cowries (currency) (Topic 1) the Maldives China and East Africa
Potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies, sweet potatoes (Topic 3) America (the American Indians) Europe and Asia
Silver (Topic 4) mines in present-day Peru and Mexico Europe (financing its trade with Asia)
Germs such as smallpox (Topic 4) Europe (carried by the Spanish conquerors) America
People fleeing poverty, hunger, disease and persecution (Topic 5) Europe America
Slaves (Topic 5) captured in Africa America's plantations
Cotton and sugar (Topic 5) America's plantations European markets

Card 3 — Four cause → effect chains

Cause → Effect
Sea route to Asia + crossing to America (sixteenth century) → The world "shrinks"; some Indian Ocean flows redirected towards Europe
Silver from Peru and Mexico → Europe's wealth enhanced; its trade with Asia financed
Poverty, hunger, crowded cities, disease, persecution of dissenters (until the nineteenth century) → Thousands flee Europe for America
China's reduced role + the rising importance of the Americas → The centre of world trade moves westwards; Europe the centre

Card 4 — The 2-mark answer frame

Most board questions on this chapter say "Explain … with any two examples" or "Give two reasons". Use this frame:

Line What to write Example (silk routes and culture)
1. Example 1 + what it shows Buddhism emerged from eastern India and spread in several directions through points on the silk routes. (1)
2. Example 2 + what it shows Early Christian missionaries almost certainly, and later Muslim preachers, travelled the same routes. (1)

Rules: two separate points; each point names a fact from your textbook and links it to the statement; stay under 40 words.

Four mixed worked examples

Mixed example 1

Which came first?

Question. Arrange in order, earliest first: I. Thousands of hungry Irish peasants die when disease destroys the potato crop. II. Silver from Peru and Mexico begins to finance Europe's trade with Asia. III. Chinese silk cargoes travel west along routes that already existed before the Christian Era. IV. Expeditions set off in search of El Dorado.

Asked: the chronological order.

Concept used: The timeline (Card 1).

  1. 1

    Date each: III — before the Christian Era onwards; II — from the sixteenth century; IV — seventeenth century; I — mid-1840s.

  2. 2

    Sort: III → II → IV → I.

  3. Check: II and IV are the closest pair: the silver came "from the sixteenth century"; the El Dorado legends spread in "seventeenth-century Europe" — so II before IV ✔.

  4. Answer III, II, IV, I

    Common trap: putting the potato famine early because the potato is a "pre-modern" food. The famine is from the mid-1840s.

Mixed example 2

Assertion and reason

Question. Assertion (A): Diseases such as smallpox were more dangerous to America's original inhabitants than the invaders' guns. Reason (R): Guns could be bought or captured and turned against the invaders, but diseases could not. Choose: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A. (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A. (C) A is true but R is false. (D) A is false but R is true.

Asked: whether A and R are true, and whether R explains A.

Concept used: Germs, not guns (Topic 4; Card 3).

  1. 1

    A is true: the most powerful weapon of the Spanish conquerors was not a conventional weapon but germs such as smallpox.

  2. 2

    R is true: these are your textbook's words.

  3. 3

    Does R explain A? Yes — R gives the reason germs were more dangerous: a captured gun could be used against the invaders, a disease could not.

  4. Check: ask "Why were diseases more dangerous than guns?" — R answers exactly that, so (A), not (B).

  5. Answer (A)

    Common trap: choosing (B) because "no immunity" is the other reason. R does not need to be the only reason — it only has to explain A.

Mixed example 3

Two examples, two marks

Question. "People in the pre-modern world carried much more than goods when they travelled." Explain the statement with two examples. (2 marks)

Asked: two examples of non-goods that travelled, each linked to the statement.

Concept used: What people carried (Topic 1); trade and culture (Topic 2); germs (Topic 4).

  1. 1

    Faith and ideas: Buddhism spread from eastern India in several directions through points on the silk routes.

  2. 2

    Germs: disease-carrying germs spread over long distances; by the thirteenth century this was an unmistakable link.

  3. Check: neither example is a good for sale — the statement says "more than goods". Choosing "silk" or "spices" would fail the Check.

  4. Answer Faiths (Buddhism along the silk routes) and germs (an unmistakable link by the thirteenth century) travelled with people.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Faiths travelled: Buddhism emerged from eastern India and spread in several directions along the silk routes. (1)
    2. Germs travelled: the long-distance spread of disease became an unmistakable link by the thirteenth century. (1)
    Common trap: giving two goods. The question asks for things other than goods.

Mixed example 4

A two-way exchange

Question. "From the sixteenth century, America changed the world — and the world changed America." Give one point for each half of the statement and one example of a person or group that moved between them. (3 marks)

Asked: (i) how America changed the world; (ii) how the world changed America; (iii) a group that moved.

Concept used: Topics 3, 4 and 5 together.

  1. 1

    America → the world: silver from present-day Peru and Mexico enhanced Europe's wealth and financed its trade with Asia; foods such as potatoes and maize reached Europe and Asia.

  2. 2

    The world → America: germs such as smallpox, carried by the Spanish conquerors, decimated whole communities and paved the way for conquest.

  3. 3

    A group that moved: thousands fled Europe for America to escape poverty, hunger, disease and religious persecution (or: slaves captured in Africa were made to work America's plantations).

  4. Check: three parts, three points; point 1 moves out of America, point 2 into America. If both your points move the same way, one half of the statement is unanswered.

  5. Answer America's silver and foods changed the world; Europe's germs and conquest changed America; thousands of Europeans fled to America.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. America changed the world: its silver from Peru and Mexico enriched Europe and financed its trade with Asia. (1)
    2. The world changed America: smallpox carried by the Spanish decimated whole communities and paved the way for conquest. (1)
    3. Thousands of Europeans fled to America to escape poverty, hunger, disease and religious persecution. (1)
    Common trap: writing two points about the same direction. Check the arrows in Card 2.
