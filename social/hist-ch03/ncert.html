Discuss p. 56The world "shrank"3 marks

Explain what we mean when we say that the world 'shrank' in the 1500s.

Asked: the meaning of "shrank" — and what caused it in the 1500s (the sixteenth century).

Concept used: The world "shrank" (Topic 4, sections 1–2).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Not size but distance: "shrank" means that distant parts of the world became closely connected — the earth did not get smaller.

  2. 2

    The cause: in the sixteenth century, European sailors found a sea route to Asia and successfully crossed the western ocean to America.

  3. 3

    What it did to trade: the old, bustling Indian Ocean trade — with the Indian subcontinent at its centre — was expanded or partly redirected towards Europe.

  4. 4

    What it did to America: America, cut off for millions of years, joined the world: its lands, crops and minerals — silver from Peru and Mexico — began to transform trade and lives everywhere.

  5. Check: the answer gives the meaning (point 1) and what made it happen (points 2–4). An answer that only says "the world became small" repeats the question.

  6. Answer The world "shrank" because new sea routes to Asia and America connected distant continents; trade flows were redirected towards Europe and America's silver and crops changed trade everywhere.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. In the 1500s European sailors found a sea route to Asia and crossed the western ocean to America, linking distant continents. (1)
    2. The old Indian Ocean trade expanded, and some of its flows were redirected towards Europe. (1)
    3. America, cut off for millions of years, joined world trade; its silver and crops transformed trade and lives everywhere. (1)
    Common trap: writing that the world "shrank" because the silk routes opened. The silk routes were far older and thrived only till about the fifteenth century.
Write in brief Q1One exchange from Asia, one from the Americas2 marks

Give two examples of different types of global exchanges which took place before the seventeenth century, choosing one example from Asia and one from the Americas.

Asked: two exchanges of different types, one from Asia, one from the Americas, before the seventeenth century.

Concept used: Silk routes (Topic 2); foods from America (Topic 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Asia — trade and culture on the silk routes: Chinese silk and pottery, and textiles and spices from India and Southeast Asia, went west; gold and silver flowed from Europe to Asia; and Buddhism spread from eastern India along the same routes.

  2. 2

    The Americas — an exchange of food crops: potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies and sweet potatoes came from America's original inhabitants and reached Europe and Asia after Columbus — about five centuries ago, so before the seventeenth century.

  3. 3

    Why these are "different types": the first is an exchange of goods and faiths along trade routes; the second is an exchange of food crops between continents. (A second American example of a different type: the germs of smallpox that the Spanish conquerors carried into America.)

  4. Check: one example from each region, and each of a different kind. Two silk-route examples would fail the "one from the Americas" condition.

  5. Answer Asia: the silk routes carried goods (silk, pottery, spices) and faiths (Buddhism). The Americas: food crops such as potatoes, maize and chillies spread from America to Europe and Asia.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Asia: the silk routes carried Chinese silk and pottery and Indian spices westward, gold and silver eastward, and spread Buddhism. (1)
    2. The Americas: foods such as potatoes, maize, tomatoes and chillies reached Europe and Asia after Columbus. (1)
    Common trap: giving the Irish potato famine as the American example. The famine (mid-1840s) is after the seventeenth century, and it happened in Europe.
Write in brief Q2Disease and the colonisation of the Americas3 marks

Explain how the global transfer of disease in the pre-modern world helped in the colonisation of the Americas.

Asked: how diseases carried from Europe made the colonisation of America easier.

Concept used: Conquest: germs, not guns (Topic 4, section 3; Box 1).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    The germs travelled with the conquerors: the most powerful weapon of the Spanish conquerors was not a conventional military weapon but the germs, such as those of smallpox, that they carried on their person.

  2. 2

    No immunity: because of their long isolation, America's original inhabitants had no immunity against these diseases from Europe.

  3. 3

    Disease ran ahead: smallpox proved a deadly killer; it spread deep into the continent, ahead even of any Europeans, killing and decimating whole communities — paving the way for conquest.

  4. 4

    Why it was decisive: guns could be bought or captured and turned against the invaders, but diseases could not, and the conquerors were mostly immune.

  5. Check: the chain runs germs → no immunity → deaths → conquest. If an answer ends at "many people died" without "paving the way for conquest", the "helped in the colonisation" part is missing.

  6. Answer Germs such as smallpox, carried by the Spanish, killed America's people, who had no immunity, even before the Europeans arrived; decimated communities could not resist, and so conquest was easy.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. The Spanish conquerors carried germs such as smallpox; their most powerful weapon was not a conventional one. (1)
    2. Long isolated, America's original inhabitants had no immunity, so smallpox spread ahead of the Europeans as a deadly killer. (1)
    3. It decimated whole communities, paving the way for conquest; unlike guns, disease could not be captured and turned back. (1)
    Common trap: saying the colonisers spread disease deliberately. Your textbook says the germs were carried, and Box 1's title is a question: "'Biological' warfare?".

Activity p. 59Flow chart: Britain's food imports and migration3 marks

Prepare a flow chart to show how Britain's decision to import food led to increased migration to America and Australia.

Asked: a flow chart from Britain's decision to import food to more migration to America and Australia.

Concept used: A world economy takes shape (textbook section 2.1). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Start: population growth in Britain raised the demand for food grains; the Corn Laws restricting imports were abolished under pressure from industrialists and urban dwellers.

  2. 2

    In Britain: food could now be imported more cheaply; British agriculture could not compete; land was left uncultivated and thousands were thrown out of work — they flocked to the cities or migrated overseas.

  3. 3

    Abroad: as food prices fell and incomes rose, Britain imported more; in Eastern Europe, Russia, America and Australia lands were cleared and food production expanded.

  4. 4

    Labour shortage: these new farms needed railways, ports, homes — and labour; where labour was short, as in America and Australia, this led to more migration.

  5. Check: every box must lead to the next with "so". The flow ends at migration to America and Australia, as the question asks.

  6. Answer Corn Laws abolished → cheap food imports → British farming declines, people lose work and migrate → more food demanded from abroad → land cleared in America and Australia → labour short there → more migration.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Corn Laws abolished → food imported cheaply → British farmers could not compete; thousands lost work and migrated overseas. (1)
    2. Falling prices and rising incomes → more imports → lands cleared in Eastern Europe, Russia, America and Australia. (1)
    3. New farms, railways and ports needed labour; labour shortage in America and Australia → more migration. (1)
    Common trap: drawing the arrow from migration to the Corn Laws. The decision to import food comes first.
Activity p. 59An Irish worker in America3 marks

Imagine that you are an agricultural worker who has arrived in America from Ireland. Write a paragraph on why you chose to come and how you are earning your living.

Asked: a short first-person paragraph: why I came; how I earn my living.

Concept used: A world economy takes shape (textbook section 2.1); NCERT Figs 4 and 7. Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Why I came: at home the potato — the food of the poorest — failed; the caption of Fig. 4 says that during the famine of 1845–1849 about a million died and double the number emigrated in search of work. Fig. 7 shows Irish emigrants waiting to board a ship.

  2. 2

    Why America: America's lands were being cleared to grow food for Britain, and labour was in short supply, so there was work.

  3. 3

    How I earn: I am a wage worker on a large farm that only a generation ago was probably a forest; the grain goes by a railway built for that purpose and then by ship.

  4. Check: both parts — why and how — must be in the paragraph, and every fact must come from the textbook. Owning a farm at once would contradict "agricultural worker".

  5. Answer A guided paragraph (below): I left Ireland after the potato failed and many emigrated in search of work; in America I work for wages on a large new farm whose grain goes by rail and ship to Europe.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. At home the potato crop failed; hundreds of thousands starved, and many like me emigrated in search of work. (1)
    2. America was clearing land to grow food for Britain, and labour was short there. (1)
    3. I earn wages on a large farm that was forest a generation ago; our grain travels by railway and ship. (1)
    Common trap: writing that the worker came as a slave. Slavery belongs to Topic 5; this worker chose to migrate for wages.
Discuss p. 64Language and popular traditions3 marks

Discuss the importance of language and popular traditions in the creation of national identity.

Asked: how language and popular (folk) traditions helped people feel they belonged to one nation.

Concept used: Note: this box is printed in Chapter 3 beside the section on indentured labour, but it is the same question as Chapter 1's Discuss box on p. 15, and it is answered from Chapter 1 (Romanticism and the nation). In Chapter 1 it is board content. Here, in Chapter 3, it sits in the part that is not in the board exam.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Popular traditions: Romantics such as Herder said true German culture lay among the common people — in folk songs, folk poetry and folk dances; collecting them was part of nation-building.

  2. 2

    Reaching ordinary people: the vernacular language and local folklore also carried the nationalist message to large audiences who were mostly illiterate.

  3. 3

    The Grimm brothers collected folktales and wrote a German dictionary to oppose French domination and create a German national identity.

  4. 4

    Language as resistance — Poland: after Russian occupation, Polish was forced out of schools; after the 1831 rebellion the clergy used Polish for church gatherings and religious teaching; using Polish became a symbol of the struggle against Russian dominance.

  5. Check: the answer must show why language and traditions mattered (a shared culture; reaching ordinary people; resistance), not just list them.

  6. Answer Folk songs, tales and the people's language gave a nation a shared past, carried its message to ordinary people, and — as in Poland — became a weapon of resistance.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Herder held that true national culture lay in the people's folk songs, poetry and dances; collecting them helped build the nation. (1)
    2. Vernacular language and folklore carried the nationalist message to mostly illiterate people; the Grimms' folktales built a German identity. (1)
    3. In Poland, using Polish in church against Russian rule became a symbol of national struggle. (1)
    Common trap: answering from the indentured-labour page beside the box. The question is about national identity in nineteenth-century Europe (Chapter 1).
Discuss p. 73The jute growers' lament3 marks

Who profits from jute cultivation according to the jute growers' lament? Explain.

Asked: who gains from jute, according to the song, and why the growers do not.

Concept used: India and the Great Depression (textbook section 3.5). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    The growers' hope: "grow more jute, brothers, with the hope of greater cash" — peasants grew more, often on borrowed money.

  2. 2

    Their loss: "Costs and debts of jute will make your hopes get dashed" — in the Depression gunny exports collapsed and raw jute prices crashed more than 60 per cent; growers fell deeper into debt.

  3. 3

    Who profits: "traders, sitting at home, will pay only Rs 5 a maund" — the traders, who buy cheaply without doing the work.

  4. Check: the answer names the group that profits (traders) and explains why the growers lose — two sides of the lament.

  5. Answer The traders profit: sitting at home, they pay the growers only Rs 5 a maund, while the growers, who spent money and borrowed to grow more, are left with costs and debts.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Peasants grew more jute, often on loans, hoping for more cash. (1)
    2. In the Depression raw jute prices crashed over 60 per cent, so costs and debts dashed their hopes. (1)
    3. The traders profit: sitting at home, they pay only Rs 5 a maund. (1)
    Common trap: saying the factories or the government profit. The lament names the traders.
Discuss p. 75Two lessons of the inter-war years3 marks

Briefly summarise the two lessons learnt by economists and politicians from the inter-war economic experience?

Asked: the two lessons, in brief.

Concept used: Post-war settlement and the Bretton Woods institutions (textbook section 4.1). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Lesson 1: an industrial society based on mass production cannot last without mass consumption; that needs high and stable incomes, which need steady, full employment — and since markets alone cannot ensure it, governments must step in to reduce swings in prices, output and employment.

  2. 2

    Lesson 2: full employment can be achieved only if governments have the power to control flows of goods, capital and labour across borders.

  3. Check: two lessons, one about the economy at home, one about links with the outside world.

  4. Answer (1) Mass production needs mass consumption, stable incomes and full employment, so governments must intervene; (2) governments must be able to control flows of goods, capital and labour.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Mass production needs mass consumption, which needs high, stable incomes and full employment. (1)
    2. Markets alone cannot ensure full employment, so governments must intervene to steady prices, output and jobs. (1)
    3. Governments also need power to control flows of goods, capital and labour with the outside world. (1)
    Common trap: giving the aims of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) as the "lessons". The lessons came first; Bretton Woods was built on them.
Write in brief Q3 (a)Abolishing the Corn Laws3 marks

Write a note to explain the effects of the following: a) The British government's decision to abolish the Corn Laws.

Asked: the effects of abolishing the Corn Laws.

Concept used: A world economy takes shape (textbook section 2.1). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Cheaper food: food could be imported into Britain more cheaply than it could be produced there.

  2. 2

    Farming declined: British agriculture could not compete; vast areas were left uncultivated and thousands lost their work — they flocked to the cities or migrated overseas.

  3. 3

    More demand from abroad: food prices fell, consumption rose, and with higher incomes Britain imported more; lands were cleared in Eastern Europe, Russia, America and Australia to meet British demand.

  4. Check: each point is an effect that follows the decision, in order.

  5. Answer Cheap imports, decline of British farming and loss of jobs, migration, and a worldwide expansion of food production for Britain.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Food could be imported into Britain more cheaply than it could be grown there. (1)
    2. British agriculture could not compete; land lay uncultivated and thousands, out of work, moved to cities or migrated. (1)
    3. Cheaper food raised consumption; lands were cleared in Eastern Europe, Russia, America and Australia to feed Britain. (1)
    Common trap: writing the cause (high food prices, pressure from industrialists) instead of the effects asked.
Write in brief Q3 (b)Rinderpest in Africa3 marks

Write a note to explain the effects of the following: b) The coming of rinderpest to Africa.

Asked: the effects of rinderpest (cattle plague) in Africa.

Concept used: Rinderpest, or the cattle plague (textbook section 2.4). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Cattle destroyed: rinderpest arrived in the late 1880s, moved west "like forest fire", reached the Atlantic coast in 1892 and the Cape five years later, killing 90 per cent of the cattle.

  2. 2

    Livelihoods destroyed: Africans had lived by land and livestock; the loss of cattle destroyed African livelihoods.

  3. 3

    Forced into wage labour: planters, mine owners and colonial governments monopolised the scarce cattle that remained, to strengthen their power and force Africans into the labour market; this control helped colonisers conquer and subdue Africa.

  4. Check: the last point links the disease to colonial power, which is why the textbook tells the story.

  5. Answer Rinderpest killed 90 per cent of the cattle, destroyed livelihoods, and let Europeans control the remaining cattle to force Africans to work for wages and to subdue Africa.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Rinderpest spread across Africa in the 1890s and killed 90 per cent of the cattle. (1)
    2. With their cattle gone, Africans lost their livelihoods. (1)
    3. Planters, mine owners and colonial governments monopolised the remaining cattle, forcing Africans into wage labour and subduing Africa. (1)
    Common trap: confusing rinderpest with smallpox. Rinderpest was a cattle disease in nineteenth-century Africa; smallpox struck people in sixteenth-century America.
Write in brief Q3 (c)The deaths of working-age men in the war3 marks

Write a note to explain the effects of the following: c) The death of men of working-age in Europe because of the World War.

Asked: the effects of so many working-age men dying in the First World War.

Concept used: Wartime transformations (textbook section 3.1). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Scale: the war killed 9 million and injured 20 million; most of the killed and maimed were men of working age.

  2. 2

    Smaller workforce: these deaths and injuries reduced the able-bodied workforce in Europe.

  3. 3

    Lower family incomes: with fewer earning members in each family, household incomes declined after the war.

  4. 4

    Women's work: as men went to battle, women stepped in to do jobs that earlier only men were expected to do.

  5. Check: each point is an effect of the deaths, not a fact about the fighting.

  6. Answer Europe lost much of its able-bodied workforce, household incomes fell, and women took up work earlier done only by men.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Most of the 9 million killed were men of working age, so Europe's able-bodied workforce shrank. (1)
    2. With fewer earners in each family, household incomes declined after the war. (1)
    3. Women stepped into jobs that earlier only men were expected to do. (1)
    Common trap: describing the weapons of the war. The question asks for the effects of the deaths.
Write in brief Q3 (d)The Great Depression and India3 marks

Write a note to explain the effects of the following: d) The Great Depression on the Indian economy.

Asked: how the Depression affected India's economy.

Concept used: India and the Great Depression (textbook section 3.5). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Trade halved: India's exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934; wheat prices fell by 50 per cent.

  2. 2

    Peasants worst hit: agricultural prices fell sharply but the colonial government refused to reduce revenue demands; raw jute prices crashed over 60 per cent; peasants used up savings, mortgaged lands and sold jewellery — and India exported gold.

  3. 3

    Towns less hit: people with fixed incomes in towns were better off as everything cost less, and industrial investment grew as the government extended tariff protection to industries under nationalist pressure.

  4. Check: the answer shows both sides — rural misery and the less grim urban picture.

  5. Answer Trade halved and prices crashed; peasants fell into debt and India exported gold; but fixed-income town-dwellers gained and industry grew behind tariffs.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. India's exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934; wheat prices fell by half. (1)
    2. Peasants were worst hit: revenue was not reduced, debts grew, lands were mortgaged and gold was exported. (1)
    3. Urban fixed-income groups gained as prices fell, and industry grew with tariff protection. (1)
    Common trap: writing that everyone in India suffered equally. Your textbook says the Depression was less grim for urban India.
Write in brief Q3 (e)MNCs move production to Asia3 marks

Write a note to explain the effects of the following: e) The decision of MNCs to relocate production to Asian countries.

Asked: the effects of MNCs moving production to Asia.

Concept used: End of Bretton Woods and the beginning of "globalisation" (textbook section 4.4). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Why Asia: from the late 1970s MNCs (multinational corporations) shifted production to low-wage Asian countries; wages were relatively low in countries like China, which became attractive for investment.

  2. 2

    Trade and capital grew: the relocation of industry stimulated world trade and capital flows; many TVs, mobile phones and toys in our shops are made in China.

  3. 3

    A new economic map: the world's economic geography changed as India, China and Brazil underwent rapid economic transformation.

  4. Check: three effects — on where goods are made, on world trade and capital, and on which countries grew.

  5. Answer Production moved to low-wage Asian countries such as China, world trade and capital flows were stimulated, and countries like India, China and Brazil transformed rapidly.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Low-wage Asian countries such as China attracted MNC investment, and many goods came to be made there. (1)
    2. The relocation of industry stimulated world trade and capital flows. (1)
    3. The world's economic geography changed as India, China and Brazil transformed rapidly. (1)
    Common trap: saying MNCs moved because Asian workers were better trained. Your textbook gives low wages as the reason.
Write in brief Q4Technology and food2 marks

Give two examples from history to show the impact of technology on food availability.

Asked: two historical examples of technology affecting how much food people could get.

Concept used: Role of technology (textbook section 2.2). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Railways and ships: faster railways, lighter wagons and larger ships moved food more cheaply and quickly from faraway farms to final markets; railways linked farming regions to ports.

  2. 2

    Refrigerated ships: till the 1870s animals were shipped live from America to Europe — costly, and many died or became unfit to eat, so meat was a luxury. Refrigerated ships let animals be slaughtered in America, Australia or New Zealand and carried as frozen meat.

  3. 3

    The result: shipping costs and meat prices fell; the poor in Europe could add meat (and butter and eggs) to their diet of bread and potatoes.

  4. Check: two examples of technology and, for each, its effect on food availability.

  5. Answer Railways and larger ships carried food cheaply from distant farms; refrigerated ships made meat cheap enough for Europe's poor.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Faster railways, lighter wagons and larger ships moved food cheaply and quickly from distant farms to markets. (1)
    2. Refrigerated ships carried frozen meat to Europe, cutting meat prices so the poor could add meat to their diet. (1)
    Common trap: giving the arrival of the potato as an example. The potato is a new crop, not a technology.
Write in brief Q5The Bretton Woods Agreement3 marks

What is meant by the Bretton Woods Agreement?

Asked: what the agreement was, what it set up, and how it worked.

Concept used: Post-war settlement and the Bretton Woods institutions (textbook section 4.1). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Where and when: the framework of the post-war international economic system was agreed at the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference, July 1944, at Bretton Woods in New Hampshire, USA; its aim was to preserve economic stability and full employment in the industrial world.

  2. 2

    Institutions: it set up the International Monetary Fund (IMF), to deal with members' external surpluses and deficits, and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (World Bank), to finance post-war reconstruction — the "Bretton Woods twins".

  3. 3

    Fixed exchange rates: national currencies were pegged to the dollar at fixed rates, and the dollar was anchored to gold at 35 US dollars an ounce.

  4. Check: a "what is meant by" answer needs the meeting, the institutions and the system.

  5. Answer The 1944 agreement at Bretton Woods set up the IMF and the World Bank and a system of fixed exchange rates linked to the dollar and gold, to keep the industrial world stable with full employment.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. It was the framework agreed at Bretton Woods, USA, in July 1944, for economic stability and full employment. (1)
    2. It set up the IMF, for members' external surpluses and deficits, and the World Bank, for post-war reconstruction. (1)
    3. Currencies were fixed to the dollar, and the dollar to gold at 35 dollars per ounce. (1)
    Common trap: writing that Bretton Woods began globalisation. Globalisation, in your textbook, begins after the Bretton Woods system ends.
Discuss Q6A letter from the Caribbean5 marks

Imagine that you are an indentured Indian labourer in the Caribbean. Drawing from the details in this chapter, write a letter to your family describing your life and feelings.

Asked: a letter, in the voice of an indentured labourer, about life (work, conditions) and feelings, using only the chapter's details.

Concept used: Indentured labour migration from India (textbook section 2.4); Source A. Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    How I came: an agent, paid a small commission, recruited me with false information about the place, the voyage and the work; I was not told of the long sea journey.

  2. 2

    The contract: I must work five years on the plantation; the contract promises my return passage to India.

  3. 3

    Life here: living and working conditions are harsh, with few legal rights; as Source A (Ram Narain Tewary) says, tasks are too heavy to finish in a day, wages are cut for "unsatisfactory" work, and a man too hurt to work can be jailed.

  4. 4

    How we cope: some escape into the wilds and are punished if caught; others make new forms of expression — in Trinidad the Muharram procession has become the carnival Hosay, which workers of all races and religions join.

  5. 5

    Feelings: I miss home and fear I cannot save enough; many here stay on after their contracts, and I wonder whether I will.

  6. Check: every detail is from the chapter. A letter that says "I am a slave captured in Africa" mixes Topic 5 with indenture.

  7. Answer A guided letter (below) covering recruitment, the contract, harsh conditions, ways of coping and feelings.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Dear family, the agent gave me false information about the work and the place; he never told me of the long sea voyage. (1)
    2. My contract binds me to this plantation for five years, with my passage home promised at the end. (1)
    3. The tasks are too heavy to finish in a day; wages are cut, and a man who cannot work may be jailed. (1)
    4. Some escape into the wilds; we join in Hosay, the Muharram carnival that people of all races share. (1)
    5. I miss you all; many stay on after their contracts, and I do not know whether I will return. (1)
    Common trap: describing modern Caribbean life. Use only what your textbook says about the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.
Discuss Q7Three flows, with Indian examples5 marks

Explain the three types of movements or flows within international economic exchange. Find one example of each type of flow which involved India and Indians, and write a short account of it.

Asked: (i) the three flows explained; (ii) one Indian example of each, briefly told.

Concept used: The three flows (textbook section 2; Topic 6); sections 2.4–2.6. Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    The three flows: trade (mostly in goods such as cloth or wheat), labour (migration in search of work) and capital (money moved for short- or long-term investment over long distances).

  2. 2

    Trade — Indian cotton: fine Indian cottons were exported to Europe; British tariffs and competition cut their share of India's exports from about 30 per cent around 1800 to below 3 per cent by the 1870s, while raw cotton exports rose from 5 to 35 per cent (1812–1871).

  3. 3

    Labour — indentured workers: hundreds of thousands of Indians from eastern Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, central India and the dry districts of Tamil Nadu went under contract to the Caribbean, Mauritius and Fiji, and to Ceylon, Malaya and Assam.

  4. 4

    Capital — Indian bankers: the Shikaripuri shroffs and Nattukottai Chettiars financed export agriculture in Central and Southeast Asia, with their own funds or loans from European banks.

  5. Check: three flows defined, three Indian examples — one of each type, not two of the same.

  6. Answer Trade (Indian cotton exports and their decline), labour (indentured migration) and capital (Shikaripuri shroffs and Nattukottai Chettiars financing agriculture abroad).

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Economists see three flows: trade in goods, labour migration for work, and capital for investment over long distances. (1)
    2. Trade: Indian cotton textiles, once exported to Europe, fell from about 30 per cent of exports around 1800 to below 3 per cent by the 1870s. (1)
    3. Meanwhile raw cotton exports rose from 5 to 35 per cent between 1812 and 1871. (1)
    4. Labour: indentured workers from Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu went to the Caribbean, Mauritius and Fiji. (1)
    5. Capital: Shikaripuri shroffs and Nattukottai Chettiars financed export agriculture in Central and Southeast Asia. (1)
    Common trap: giving the silk routes as a "flow" example here. The question is about the nineteenth-century flows of section 2.
Discuss Q8Causes of the Great Depression5 marks

Explain the causes of the Great Depression.

Asked: the causes, in a cause → effect order.

Concept used: The Great Depression (textbook section 3.4). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    A fragile post-war economy — the textbook says the depression came from a combination of several factors.

  2. 2

    Agricultural overproduction: as prices fell, farmers produced more to keep their incomes up, worsening the glut and pushing prices lower; produce rotted for lack of buyers.

  3. 3

    Dependence on US loans: in the mid-1920s many countries financed investment with US loans; at the first sign of trouble US lenders panicked — overseas loans of over 1 billion US dollars in the first half of 1928 fell to a quarter of that a year later.

  4. 4

    Effects abroad: the withdrawal brought bank failures and the collapse of currencies such as the pound sterling in Europe, and deepened the slump in agricultural and raw-material prices in Latin America.

  5. 5

    US actions at home: the US doubled import duties, a severe blow to world trade; US banks cut lending and called back loans, so farms, households and businesses collapsed.

  6. Check: five points, each a cause or a step that spread the crisis, none a later effect such as the 1935 recovery.

  7. Answer Overproduction and falling farm prices, heavy dependence on US loans and their sudden withdrawal, and US protection and loan recalls together caused the Depression.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. The post-war world economy was fragile; several factors combined to cause the depression. (1)
    2. Agricultural overproduction and falling prices made farmers produce more, worsening the glut and pushing prices lower. (1)
    3. Many countries depended on US loans; US overseas loans fell from over 1 billion dollars in early 1928 to a quarter a year later. (1)
    4. This caused bank failures and currency collapses in Europe and deepened the slump in Latin America. (1)
    5. The US doubled import duties and its banks called back loans, collapsing farms, households and businesses. (1)
    Common trap: listing the effects (unemployment queues, closed banks) as causes.
Discuss Q9The G-775 marks

Explain what is referred to as the G-77 countries. In what ways can G-77 be seen as a reaction to the activities of the Bretton Woods twins?

Asked: (i) what the G-77 is; (ii) why it can be seen as a reaction to the IMF and the World Bank.

Concept used: Decolonisation and independence (textbook section 4.3). Not in the board exam — for your project.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    What it is: the Group of 77 — developing countries that organised themselves as a group because most of them did not benefit from the fast growth of the Western economies in the 1950s and 1960s.

  2. 2

    Twins not built for them: the IMF and the World Bank were designed for the financial needs of industrial countries, not to cope with poverty and lack of development in former colonies.

  3. 3

    Controlled by the West: decision-making in them is controlled by Western industrial powers, with an effective US veto; newly independent countries came under the guidance of agencies dominated by the former colonial powers.

  4. 4

    Resources still controlled: former colonial powers and large corporations still controlled vital resources such as minerals and land in many former colonies.

  5. 5

    Their demand — the NIEO: a new international economic order giving real control over natural resources, more development assistance, fairer prices for raw materials, and better access for their manufactured goods to developed markets.

  6. Check: part (i) is who; part (ii) is why it was a reaction — points 2–5 each name something about the twins or the order they ran.

  7. Answer The G-77 was a group of developing countries that, left out of post-war growth and of decision-making in the West-controlled IMF and World Bank, demanded a new international economic order.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. The G-77, or Group of 77, was formed by developing countries that had not benefited from the West's fast growth in the 1950s and 1960s. (1)
    2. The IMF and World Bank were designed for industrial countries' needs, not for the poverty of former colonies. (1)
    3. Western powers controlled their decisions, with an effective US veto, so ex-colonies were guided by former colonial powers. (1)
    4. Former colonial powers and big corporations still controlled vital minerals and land in many former colonies. (1)
    5. So it demanded a new international economic order (NIEO): control over resources, more aid, fairer raw-material prices and market access. (1)
    Common trap: describing the G-77 as part of the Bretton Woods system. It was a reaction against that system.
