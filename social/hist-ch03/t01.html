Topic 1 of 6

The pre-modern world: a long history of links

About 30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why your textbook says the global world has a long history, though "globalisation" usually means the last 50 years or so.
  • Describe who travelled in the pre-modern world, why they travelled, and what they carried with them.
  • Give the textbook's early examples with their dates — the Indus valley and West Asia, cowries from the Maldives, the spread of germs, and ships on memorial stones (NCERT Fig. 1).

In a museum in Goa there is a stone carved more than a thousand years ago. It was set up to remember someone, and on it the carver cut a ship, with people on board. Why put a ship on a memorial? Because for the people of that coast, the sea and the trade it carried were part of life. This chapter begins with a surprise: the world was linked up long before phones, aeroplanes or the internet.

The chain in one line: people travel for knowledge, work, faith or safety → they carry goods, money, ideas, skills — and germs → distant parts of the world become steadily more interlinked.

Quick reminder — reading centuries

Words in your textbook What they mean
3000 BCE 3,000 years before the Common Era — about 5,000 years before today
the seventh century roughly the years 600–699 CE (the "600s")
the thirteenth century roughly the years 1200–1299 CE (the "1200s")
a millennium (plural millennia) a thousand years

Tip: the century number is one more than the hundreds: the 600s are the seventh century, the 1500s the sixteenth.

  1. "Globalisation" is new — the global world is old

When people talk of globalisation today, they usually mean an economic system that has grown up over the last 50 years or so. Your textbook says the making of the global world is much older. It has a long history — of trade, of migration, of people in search of work, of the movement of capital (money for investment), and much more.

All through history, human societies have become steadily more interlinked. To understand the linked-up world we live in, we need to see the phases through which it was made. This chapter's board-exam part is the first phase: the pre-modern world, up to the sixteenth century and its effects.

New word. Pre-modern — the long period before the modern age; in this chapter, the world up to and around the sixteenth century (our definition; your textbook uses the word without defining it).

  1. Who travelled, why, and what they carried

From ancient times, people travelled vast distances. Your textbook gives three lists:

Who travelled Why they travelled What they carried with them
travellers, traders, priests and pilgrims for knowledge, opportunity and spiritual fulfilment, or to escape persecution goods, money, values, skills, ideas, inventions — and even germs and diseases

Read the table across, but do not pair each traveller with one reason: your textbook gives the reasons for all of them together. A trader could also be a pilgrim; a priest could also be escaping persecution.

The last item in the third column is the surprise: people carried germs as well as goods. Keep this in mind — it becomes very important in Topic 4.

  1. How far back? The textbook's early examples

  1. 3000 BCE — the Indus valley and West Asia. As early as 3000 BCE, an active coastal trade linked the Indus valley civilisations with present-day West Asia.
  2. Cowries — seashells used as money. For more than a millennium, cowries (the Hindi cowdi, or seashells, used as a form of currency) from the Maldives found their way to China and East Africa. (Beyond your textbook: the Maldives are a group of islands in the Indian Ocean, south-west of India.)
  3. Germs. The long-distance spread of disease-carrying germs may be traced as far back as the seventh century. By the thirteenth century it had become an unmistakable link between distant regions.

Notice the pattern: goods (trade), money (cowries) and diseases (germs) all moved over long distances — the same kinds of things the second table lists.

New word. Currency — anything a society uses as money (our definition). In the pre-modern world, cowrie shells were used as money in places far from where they were found.

  1. Reading a source — NCERT Fig. 1

NCERT Fig. 1 is a photograph of an image of a ship on a memorial stone in the Goa Museum, carved in the tenth century CE. The caption adds that from the ninth century, images of ships appear regularly on memorial stones found on the western coast.

Question Answer
What does it show? A ship carved on a stone that was set up to remember someone, from the tenth century CE.
What does it tell us? Ships appear regularly on such stones from the ninth century, so oceanic trade was significant in the lives of people on the western coast.
What is its limit or point of view? A carving does not tell us what the ship carried, where it sailed or who it belonged to. We learn how much the sea mattered, not the details of the trade.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

What a seashell tells us

Question. Cowries from the Maldives were found in China and in East Africa. What does this tell us about the pre-modern world? Give two points. (2 marks)

Asked: two things that the cowries' journey shows.

Concept used: Early examples of links (this topic, section 3). Why it applies: the cowries are one of your textbook's examples, and the question wants their meaning, not just the fact.

  1. 1

    They were money: cowries were seashells used as a form of currency; so money, not only goods, moved over long distances.

  2. 2

    Far-off places were linked: the shells travelled from the Maldives to two distant regions, China and East Africa, for more than a millennium — the world was interlinked long before modern times.

  3. Check: each point says what the cowries show. If a point only said "cowries are seashells", it would describe the shell and earn nothing for "what does this tell us".

  4. Answer Money moved across the world, and distant regions (China, East Africa) were linked to the Maldives for over a thousand years.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Cowries were seashells used as currency, so even money travelled over long distances in pre-modern times. (1)
    2. For over a millennium they reached both China and East Africa, showing that distant regions were already linked. (1)
    Common trap: writing that cowries went from China to the Maldives. They came from the Maldives.

Worked example 2

Reading a carved ship

Question. Look at NCERT Fig. 1, the ship on a memorial stone in the Goa Museum. (a) When was it carved? (b) What do such stones tell us, according to your textbook? (c) Give one thing the stone cannot tell us. (3 marks)

Asked: three separate parts — a date, a meaning, a limit.

Concept used: Reading a source (this topic, section 4).

  1. 1

    (a) Date: the stone is from the tenth century CE.

  2. 2

    (b) Meaning: from the ninth century, images of ships appear regularly on memorial stones of the western coast; this shows the significance of oceanic trade there.

  3. 3

    (c) Limit: a carving cannot tell us what the ship carried or where it sailed.

  4. Check: three parts, three answers. A Check you can do: cover the answer and ask "did I give a date, a meaning and a limit?" An answer with two meanings and no limit would miss part (c).

  5. Answer (a) Tenth century CE. (b) Oceanic trade mattered greatly on the western coast. (c) It does not tell us the cargo or the route.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    (a) The memorial stone was carved in the tenth century CE. (1)
    (b) From the ninth century, ships appear regularly on memorial stones of the western coast, showing that oceanic trade was significant. (1)
    (c) A carving cannot tell us what goods the ship carried or where it sailed. (1)

    Common trap: answering (b) with "people liked ships". The textbook's point is the significance of oceanic trade.

Worked example 3

Which came first?

Question. Arrange in order, earliest first: I. Images of ships begin to appear regularly on memorial stones of the western coast. II. The long-distance spread of germs can first be traced. III. Coastal trade links the Indus valley civilisations with West Asia. IV. The ship on the Goa Museum stone is carved.

Asked: the chronological order.

Concept used: The mini-timeline of this topic.

  1. 1

    Date each: III — 3000 BCE; II — seventh century; I — from the ninth century; IV — tenth century CE.

  2. 2

    Sort: 3000 BCE → seventh century → ninth century → tenth century.

  3. Check: BCE comes before every CE date, so III must be first. The spread of germs "may be traced as far back as the seventh century", before the ninth. Between I and IV: the textbook says ships appear regularly from the ninth century, and the Goa stone is tenth century, so I comes before IV.

  4. Answer III, II, I, IV

    Common trap: putting II last because germs became an "unmistakable link" only by the thirteenth century. The statement asks when the spread can first be traced — the seventh century.

By which century, says your textbook, had the long-distance spread of disease-carrying germs become "an unmistakable link"?

By the thirteenth century the spread of germs had become an unmistakable link.
The seventh century is how far back the spread can be traced — the beginning, not the "unmistakable" stage.
The tenth century is the date of the ship on the Goa memorial stone.
The sixteenth century is when the world "shrank" with new sea routes (Topic 4).

Common mistakes!

  • "Globalisation began only in the last 50 years."

    That is when the present economic system grew up; the making of the global world has a long history.

    Write: the global world has a long history of trade, migration and capital movement.

  • "Germs became a link in the seventh century."

    The spread can be traced back to the seventh century; it was an unmistakable link by the thirteenth.

    Learn the pair: traced — seventh; unmistakable — thirteenth.

  • "Cowries went from China to the Maldives."

    The shells came from the Maldives and reached China and East Africa.

    Think of the shells' home: the Maldives → out.

  • Matching each traveller with one reason ("pilgrims travelled for opportunity").

    Your textbook lists the travellers and the reasons together, not in pairs.

    Give the lists as the textbook does.

Key points

  • "Globalisation" usually means the economic system of the last 50 years or so, but the global world has a long history — of trade, migration, people in search of work and the movement of capital.
  • Travellers, traders, priests and pilgrims travelled vast distances for knowledge, opportunity, spiritual fulfilment or to escape persecution; they carried goods, money, values, skills, ideas, inventions — and germs and diseases.
  • 3000 BCE: coastal trade linked the Indus valley civilisations with present-day West Asia.
  • Cowries (cowdi), seashells used as currency, went from the Maldives to China and East Africa for more than a millennium.
  • The spread of disease-carrying germs can be traced to the seventh century and was an unmistakable link by the thirteenth.
  • NCERT Fig. 1: a ship on a tenth-century memorial stone (Goa Museum); ships on such stones from the ninth century show the significance of oceanic trade on the western coast.
