Geography · Chapter 2

Forest and Wildlife Resources

6 topicsAbout 8 hoursBoard exam
Why this chapter matters

The air you breathe, the water you drink and the soil that grows your food are kept healthy by living things — and forests sit at the centre of that web. India is one of the richest countries in the world for plants and animals, yet its tigers once came close to vanishing. This chapter explains why we must conserve forests and wildlife, what the government has done (laws, parks, Project Tiger), how India's forests are classified and managed, and how ordinary people and communities — villagers, farmers, movements, believers in sacred groves — have protected them.

You will learn the meaning of biodiversity, the reasons for conservation, the Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, Project Tiger (1973) and the six tiger reserves with their states, the three kinds of forest (reserved, protected, unclassed), and the work of communities: Sariska, the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri', the Chipko movement, the Beej Bachao Andolan and Navdanya, Joint Forest Management and sacred groves. It is one of the Geography chapters in the board exam (Geography carries 17 marks + 3 map marks). In the board papers of 2024–2026 it was asked 27 times — most often as a 1-mark question matching a tiger reserve with its state, then on communities and conservation, the kinds of forest and government measures.

What you'll learn

  • Explain biodiversity and the web of life, and give the textbook's reasons for conservation.
  • Describe the 1972 Act and the species projects, and put the conservation steps in order of year.
  • Explain why Project Tiger was needed and name the six tiger reserves with their states.
  • Classify forests as reserved, protected and unclassed, and say where each kind dominates.
  • Describe the community efforts in your textbook and explain Joint Forest Management.
  • Explain how traditional beliefs protect nature, and suggest measures to conserve forests and wildlife.
  • Put the chapter on four cards and answer mixed board-style questions.

Most of the marks in this chapter come from six skills, and every topic practises them:

  1. Match a reserve with its state — the six tiger reserves of your textbook.
  2. Classify — forests (reserved, protected, unclassed) and efforts (government or community).
  3. Explain why — the reasons for conserving forests and wildlife.
  4. Recall measures with their years — 1972, 1973, 1980 and 1986, 1988, 1991.
  5. Read a case — Project Tiger, sacred groves, Joint Forest Management; name the movement from a description.
  6. Write to the marks — 2 marks ≈ two points (up to 40 words), 3 marks ≈ three points (up to 60), 5 marks ≈ five points (up to 120), each on its own line; measures when measures are asked.

Not in the board exam

The textbook's three Activities — stories of harmony with nature (p. 13), sanctuaries and national parks on the map (p. 15) and a short essay on everyday practices (p. 17) — are classroom activities; they get short guided answers in the NCERT solutions and are not part of the board exam. Everything else in the chapter — including the biodiversity box, the Project Tiger box, the newspaper clippings, the sacred-groves box and Figs 2.1–2.2 — can be asked in the board exam. The syllabus has no map item for this chapter. Two of the syllabus learning outcomes for this chapter — the role of grazing and wood cutting, and how developmental works, grazing and wood cutting have affected forests — have no matching text in your current textbook, so this chapter teaches only what the textbook says about threats to forests and wildlife.
