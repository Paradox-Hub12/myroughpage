The whole chapter on one page

Biodiversity — we share the planet with millions of living beings, from micro-organisms and bacteria, lichens to banyan trees, elephants and blue whales. Humans are only a part of the web of life and depend on it: plants, animals and micro-organisms re-create the air, water and soil. Forests are the primary producers. Biodiversity is rich in wildlife and cultivated species, diverse in form and function, linked through interdependencies. India is one of the world's richest countries in biodiversity, but forests and wildlife are under great stress, mainly due to insensitivity to our environment.

Why conserve (E-L-G-A-F) — ecological diversity; life support systems: water, air, soil; genetic diversity for better growth of species and breeding; agriculture still depends on traditional crop varieties; fisheries depend on aquatic biodiversity.

Government steps — demand in the 1960s and 1970s → Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972: protecting habitats, an all-India list of protected species, ban on hunting, restricted trade — to protect the remaining populations of endangered species. National parks and wildlife sanctuaries; projects for the tiger, one-horned rhinoceros, Kashmir stag (hangul), three crocodiles (fresh water, saltwater, Gharial), Asiatic lion; recently the Indian elephant, black buck (chinkara), great Indian bustard (godawan), snow leopard. Focus moved to biodiversity: insects (notifications of 1980 and 1986: butterflies, moths, beetles, one dragonfly); plants in 1991 (six species).

Project Tiger (1973) — the tiger, a key species of the faunal web, had dwindled to 1,827 from an estimated 55,000 at the turn of the century. Threats: poaching for trade, shrinking habitat, depletion of prey base species, growing human population; trade in skins and bones (traditional medicines). India and Nepal — about two-thirds of surviving tigers. Tiger conservation also preserves large habitats ("biotypes of sizeable magnitude"). Reserves: Corbett National Park — Uttarakhand; Sunderbans National Park — West Bengal; Bandhavgarh National Park — Madhya Pradesh; Sariska Wildlife Sanctuary — Rajasthan; Manas Tiger Reserve — Assam; Periyar Tiger Reserve — Kerala. Fig. 2.2: Kaziranga National Park (rhino and deer).

Kinds of forest — mostly owned or managed by the government (Forest Department). Reserved: more than half; most valuable for conservation. Protected: almost one-third; protected from further depletion. Unclassed: other forests and wastelands of government, private individuals and communities. Permanent forest estates = reserved + protected (timber, forest produce, protection); Madhya Pradesh has the largest area (75 per cent of its forest). Reserved-heavy: Jammu and Kashmir, Andhra Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Maharashtra. Protected-heavy: Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Odisha, Rajasthan. Unclassed: all north-eastern states, parts of Gujarat (local communities).

Communities — Sariska villagers fought mining citing the Wildlife Protection Act; five Alwar villages: Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri', 1,200 hectares, own rules, no hunting; Chipko (Himalayas): resisted deforestation, community afforestation with indigenous species; Beej Bachao Andolan (Tehri) and Navdanya: diversified crops without synthetic chemicals; JFM (since 1988, Odisha first): village institutions protect degraded forest land of the forest department, get non-timber forest produce and a share in timber. Lesson: communities must be involved; still a long way to go to the centre-stage; accept only people centric, environment-friendly, economically rewarding activities.

Sacred groves — nature worship; forests of God and Goddesses, left untouched, interference banned. Mundas and Santhal (Chota Nagpur): mahua, kadamba. Tribals of Odisha and Bihar: tamarind, mango (weddings). Peepal and banyan sacred to many. Macaques and langurs fed at temples. Bishnoi villages (Rajasthan): blackbuck (chinkara), nilgai, peacocks — nobody harms them.

Terms, numbers and places sheet

Key terms (as in your textbook — learn the words in bold)

  • Biodiversity: immensely rich in wildlife and cultivated species, diverse in form and function but closely integrated in a system through multiple network of interdependencies.
  • Reserved forests: more than half of total forest land; most valuable for conservation of forest and wildlife resources.
  • Protected forests: almost one-third of total forest area, declared by the Forest Department; protected from any further depletion.
  • Unclassed forests: other forests and wastelands belonging to both government and private individuals and communities.
  • Permanent forest estates: reserved and protected forests, maintained for timber and other forest produce and for protective reasons.
  • Sacred groves: virgin forests in pristine form, "the forests of God and Goddesses", left untouched by local people.
  • Joint Forest Management: local (village) institutions protect mostly degraded forest land managed by the forest department and get intermediary benefits.

Numbers to know
