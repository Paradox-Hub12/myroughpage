Topic 5 of 6

Communities and conservation

About 45 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe what communities did at Sariska, in the Alwar district (Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'), in the Chipko movement and in the Beej Bachao Andolan and Navdanya.
  • Explain how Joint Forest Management works and why local communities must be involved in conservation.

Laws and reserves are one way to protect a forest. Another is far older: the people who live in and around the forest decide to protect it themselves — because their food, fuel and livelihood depend on it. In this topic you meet villagers who stopped a mine, villages that wrote their own forest rules, a movement that stopped the felling of trees, farmers who saved their seeds, and a scheme in which the government and villagers look after a forest together.

  1. Conservation is not new in India

  • Conservation strategies are not new in our country.
  • We often ignore that in India, forests are also home to some of the traditional communities.
  • In some areas, local communities are struggling to conserve these habitats along with government officials, recognising that only this will secure their own long-term livelihood.

Your textbook adds that attempts to revive the traditional conservation methods, or to develop new methods of ecological farming, are now widespread.

Note on the textbook's wording. Write 'Sonchuri' exactly as the textbook prints it, in quotation marks. The textbook also prints "citizen's groups"; read it as "citizens' groups".

Beyond your textbook

Your textbook places the Chipko movement only "in the Himalayas". It began in the hills of what is now Uttarakhand; a 2026 board question asked for this state.

Memory hook — "Chipko stops the axe; Beej Bachao saves the seed; JFM shares the forest".

  1. Joint Forest Management (JFM) — how it works

Step Your textbook
Since when in formal existence since 1988, when Odisha passed the first resolution for joint forest management
Who acts local (village) institutions are formed
What they do they undertake protection activities, mostly on degraded forest land managed by the forest department
What they get intermediary benefits like non-timber forest produce, and a share in the timber harvested by "successful protection"
Why it matters a good example of involving local communities in the management and restoration of degraded forests

Note on the textbook's wording. The textbook prints "non-timber forest produces"; read it as "non-timber forest produce" (forest products other than timber).

  1. The lesson — and its limit

Your textbook draws a clear lesson from India's experience of both environmental destruction and reconstruction:

  • Local communities everywhere have to be involved in some kind of natural resource management.
  • But there is still a long way to go before local communities are at the centre-stage in decision-making.
  • Accept only those economic or developmental activities that are people centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding.

Give both halves in an answer: communities must be involved, and they are not yet at the centre of decisions.

Worked example 1

Which effort is it?

Question. Name the effort described in each case: (a) farmers in Tehri show that many kinds of crops can be grown without synthetic chemicals, and still pay; (b) hill villagers stop the felling of trees and replant with local species; (c) a village committee guards a degraded forest of the forest department and gets a share of the timber; (d) villagers near a tiger reserve in Rajasthan oppose a mine by pointing to a wildlife law.

Asked: four efforts.

Concept used: The five community efforts (section 2) and JFM (section 3).

  1. 1

    (a) Tehri, diversified crops without synthetic chemicals → Beej Bachao Andolan.

  2. 2

    (b) Resisting deforestation; afforestation with indigenous species → Chipko movement.

  3. 3

    (c) Village institution, degraded forest land, share in timber → Joint Forest Management.

  4. 4

    (d) Rajasthan tiger reserve, mining opposed by citing the Act → the villagers of Sariska.

  5. Check: each description carries one clue found in only one row of the table: "Tehri", "felling … replant", "share of the timber", "mine … wildlife law".

  6. Answer (a) Beej Bachao Andolan; (b) Chipko movement; (c) Joint Forest Management; (d) Sariska villagers.

    Common trap: answering (a) with "Chipko" because both are hill movements. Beej Bachao Andolan is about seeds and crops, not trees.

Worked example 2

Starting JFM in a district

Question. A district officer wants to start Joint Forest Management in an area of degraded forest. Explain three things she must know about how JFM works, using your textbook. (3 marks)

Asked: three features of how JFM works.

Concept used: JFM (section 3).

  1. 1

    Who acts: JFM depends on forming local (village) institutions.

  2. 2

    What land: these institutions undertake protection activities, mostly on degraded forest land managed by the forest department.

  3. 3

    What they get: members are entitled to intermediary benefits such as non-timber forest produce and a share in the timber harvested by "successful protection".

  4. Check: the three points answer three different questions — who, where, what in return. "JFM began in 1988 in Odisha" is true but tells the officer nothing about how it works.

  5. Answer Village institutions protect degraded forest land of the forest department, and in return get non-timber forest produce and a share in the timber.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. JFM depends on forming local village institutions that take on the work of protection. (1)
    2. They protect mostly degraded forest land that is managed by the forest department. (1)
    3. In return, members get non-timber forest produce and a share in the timber harvested by "successful protection". (1)
    Common trap: writing "villagers become the owners of the forest". The land stays managed by the forest department; villagers get benefits.

Worked example 3

Communities at the centre?

Question. "Local communities must be at the centre of forest and wildlife conservation." Examine the statement with examples from your textbook. (5 marks)

Asked: an examination — why communities matter, examples, and how far the statement is true today.

Concept used: Sections 1–4.

  1. 1

    Why communities: forests are home to traditional communities; conserving habitats secures their own long-term livelihood.

  2. 2

    Example 1: in Sariska, Rajasthan, villagers fought against mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act.

  3. 3

    Example 2: five villages of Alwar declared 1,200 hectares as the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri', with their own rules banning hunting.

  4. 4

    Example 3: the Chipko movement resisted deforestation and showed that community afforestation with indigenous species can be enormously successful.

  5. 5

    How far true today: JFM (since 1988) involves communities in restoring degraded forests, but there is still a long way to go before communities are at the centre-stage in decision-making.

  6. Check: five different points — the reason, three different places or movements, and the textbook's limit. The last point keeps the textbook's balance: "must be involved" but "not yet at the centre".

  7. Answer Communities protect forests because their livelihood depends on them, as Sariska, the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri' and Chipko show; JFM involves them, but they are not yet at the centre of decisions.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Forests are home to traditional communities, and conserving habitats secures their own long-term livelihood. (1)
    2. In Sariska, Rajasthan, villagers fought against mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act. (1)
    3. Five villages of Alwar declared 1,200 hectares as the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri', with their own rules banning hunting. (1)
    4. The Chipko movement resisted deforestation and showed that community afforestation with indigenous species can succeed. (1)
    5. JFM involves communities in restoring degraded forests, but they are still far from centre-stage in decision-making. (1)
    Common trap: writing five lines about Chipko alone. "With examples" needs different examples, and "examine" needs the textbook's limit too.

Common mistakes!

  • Mixing the efforts: "Chipko grew crops without chemicals"; "Beej Bachao Andolan hugged trees"; "Sonchuri is in Tehri".

    Chipko — Himalayas, resisted deforestation; Beej Bachao Andolan — Tehri, crops without synthetic chemicals; Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri' — Alwar, Rajasthan.

    Learn each effort with its place and what the people did.

  • "JFM began in Rajasthan in 1972" or "JFM protects untouched forests" or "villagers own the forest".

    JFM: 1988, Odisha first; degraded forest land managed by the forest department; villagers get benefits, not ownership.

    Write who, what land, what benefit.

  • Calling a government step a community effort (e.g. "demarcating wildlife sanctuaries is people's participation").

    Laws, sanctuaries and Project Tiger are government measures; Sariska villagers, Sonchuri, Chipko, Beej Bachao Andolan are community efforts; JFM joins both.

    Ask: who started it and who does the work?

  • "Conservation is only the government's job" or "local people only harm forests".

    Your textbook: communities protect habitats themselves, and must be involved everywhere — yet they are not yet at the centre-stage of decisions.

    Give both halves of the textbook's lesson.

  • Writing one example for a 5-mark "how have communities conserved forests" answer.

    Five marks need five different points: Sariska, Sonchuri, Chipko, Beej Bachao Andolan or JFM, and the lesson.

    Count the marks and use a different effort in each point.

Key points

  • Forests are home to traditional communities; protecting habitats secures their long-term livelihood.
  • Sariska (Rajasthan): villagers fought mining, citing the Wildlife Protection Act.
  • Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri': five villages, Alwar; 1,200 hectares; own rules; no hunting; no outside encroachments.
  • Chipko (Himalayas): resisted deforestation; showed community afforestation with indigenous species can succeed.
  • Beej Bachao Andolan (Tehri) and Navdanya: diversified crops without synthetic chemicals — possible and economically viable.
  • JFM (since 1988; Odisha first): village institutions protect degraded forest land of the forest department; get non-timber forest produce and a share in timber.
  • Lesson: communities must be involved, but there is still a long way to go before they are at the centre-stage; accept only people centric, environment-friendly, economically rewarding activities.
