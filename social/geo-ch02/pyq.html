How this chapter is asked

The board papers of 2024–2026 asked this chapter 27 times: most often as a 1-mark question matching a tiger reserve or national park with its state (seven times), then on communities and conservation (Chipko, Joint Forest Management, sacred groves), the three kinds of forest, the Wildlife (Protection) Act and Project Tiger, and measures to conserve forests and wildlife. Four items were 4-mark source questions whose passages come word for word, or almost, from your textbook. The 2026-27 sample paper gives the chapter one 4-mark source question; the 2025-26 sample paper gives it two 1-mark items.

The sample-paper answers follow the official marking schemes. No marking scheme is published for the board papers, so their mark splits are our marking points, written to the CBSE word limits (2 marks — up to 40 words, 3 marks — up to 60, 5 marks — up to 120, case study — up to 100). CBSE Sample Question Paper 2026-27, Section B, Q184 marks

Question: Read the given text and answer the following questions:

Role of Communities in Forest Conservation
In many parts of India, especially in tribal and mountainous regions, local communities depend on forests for fuel, fodder, and livelihood. Over time, people realised that uncontrolled exploitation by contractors and commercial interests was destroying forests and threatening their survival. This led to community-led conservation efforts where people actively protected forests through traditional practices and movements. One such movement began in the Himalayan region, where villagers protected trees by hugging them, highlighting the importance of people's participation in conservation.
Source- Contemporary India – II,NCERT, Chapter 2

18.1 State any one objective of community-based forest conservation. (1)
18.2 How does people's participation lead to more effective forest resource management? Elaborate with the help of two examples. (2)
18.3 Name the forest conservation movement referred to in the given source. (1)

Asked: one objective, two ways people's participation helps (with two examples), and the name of the movement.

Concept used: Communities and conservation (Topic 5).

  1. 1

    18.1 The objective is to protect forests and use them sustainably by involving the local communities who depend on them — conserving habitats secures their own long-term livelihood.

  2. 2

    18.2 People who depend on the forest know it and protect it themselves. Two examples from your textbook: Joint Forest Management — village institutions protect degraded forests and share the benefits; the Beej Bachao Andolan (Tehri) and Navdanya — diversified crops without synthetic chemicals.

  3. 3

    18.3 A movement in the Himalayas that protected trees and resisted deforestation → the Chipko Movement.

  4. Check: 18.2 has 2 marks — so two points, each with its own example.

    Common trap: naming Joint Forest Management in 18.3. The passage describes a people's movement in the Himalayan region: that is Chipko.
18.1 To protect forest resources and use them sustainably by involving local communities. (1)
18.2 Local people know their forests and feel responsible for them, so they protect them; under Joint Forest Management, village institutions protect degraded forests and share the benefits (1); the Beej Bachao Andolan in Tehri and Navdanya showed diversified crops without synthetic chemicals are viable. (1)
18.3 The Chipko Movement. (1)

Note on the official text: the passage is credited to your textbook, but two of its details are not in the current edition — "contractors and commercial interests" and villagers "hugging" trees. Your textbook says the Chipko movement "in the Himalayas" resisted deforestation. The marking scheme's 18.2 points ("traditional knowledge", "sense of responsibility and ownership") are used above with the textbook's examples.

CBSE Sample Question Paper 2025-26, Section B, Q121 mark

Question: A total of 628 tigers died in India during the past five years due to natural causes and other reasons, including poaching, according to government data. Meanwhile, 349 people were killed in tiger attacks during this period, with Maharashtra alone recording 200 deaths. [source: https://www.ptinews.com/story/national/628-tigers-died-in-india-in-past-five-years-govt-data/1685133t/] Which of the following is the most significant indirect consequence of poaching on the tiger population?

Asked: an indirect effect of poaching on the tiger population.

Concept used: Threats to the tiger (Topic 3).

  1. 1

    Direct effect: poaching kills tigers.

  2. 2

    Indirect effect: when poaching also removes the animals that tigers hunt, the tiger's food supply dwindles — your textbook lists depletion of prey base species among the threats to the tiger.

  3. 3

    Rule out the rest: B is about people, not the tiger population; C is the opposite of what poaching does; D is about money, not tigers.

  4. Check: only A is an effect on the tigers themselves that comes through another link (their food).

    Common trap: choosing B because the data mention people killed in tiger attacks. The question asks about the tiger population.
Option (A): Reduction in the prey species, dwindling tiger's food supply
1 mark

Note on the official text: the marking scheme prints the key cut off: "A. Reduction in the prey species leading to the tiger's dwindling food supp". The figures in the question come from a news report, not your textbook. Your textbook lists "depletion of prey base species" as one of the threats to the tiger, alongside poaching; the sample paper links the two.

Board 2026, 32/3/1 Q17 · 32/3/2 Q17 · 32/3/3 Q174 marks

Question: Read the given source carefully and answer the questions that follow :

Joint Forest Management
In India the Joint Forest Management (JFM) programme furnishes a good example for involving local communities in the management and restoration of degraded forests. The programme has been in formal existence since 1988 when the state of Odisha passed the first resolution for joint forest management. JFM depends on the formation of local (village) institutions that undertake protection activities mostly on degraded forest land managed by the forest department. In return, the members of these communities are entitled to intermediary benefits like non-timber forest produces and share in the timber harvested by 'successful protection'.
The clear lesson from the dynamics of both environmental destruction and reconstruction in India is that local communities everywhere have to be involved in some kind of natural resource management. But there is still a long way to go before local communities are at the centre-stage in decision-making. Accept only those economic or developmental activities, that are people centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding.

(17.1) Why is conservation of forests necessary ? (1)
(17.2) Which type of forests are protected under the 'Joint Forest Management' programme ? (1)
(17.3) Explain the role of local communities in forest conservation. (2)

Asked: a reason for conservation, the kind of forest JFM protects, and two points on the role of local communities.

Concept used: Why conserve (Topic 1); JFM and the lesson (Topic 5).

  1. 1

    (17.1) Your textbook: conservation preserves ecological diversity and our life support systems — water, air and soil.

  2. 2

    (17.2) From the passage: degraded forests — degraded forest land managed by the forest department.

  3. 3

    (17.3) Village institutions protect and restore degraded forests and get benefits in return; and local communities everywhere have to be involved in natural resource management.

  4. Check: (17.2) is answered from the passage itself ("degraded forest land"); (17.3) has 2 marks, so two points.

    Common trap: answering (17.2) with "reserved forests". JFM works mostly on degraded forest land.
(17.1) It preserves ecological diversity and our life support systems — water, air and soil. (1)

(17.3) Village institutions protect and restore degraded forests and receive non-timber produce and a share of timber (1); local communities everywhere must be involved in managing natural resources, though they are not yet at the centre of decisions. (1)

Question: Read the given source and answer the questions that follow :

Sacred Groves – a wealth of diverse and rare species
Nature worship is an age old tribal belief based on the premise that all creations of nature have to be protected. Such beliefs have preserved several virgin forests in pristine form called Sacred Groves (the forests of God and Goddesses). These patches of forest or parts of large forests have been left untouched by the local people and any interference with them is banned.
Certain societies revere a particular tree which they have preserved from time immemorial. The Mundas and the Santhal of Chota Nagpur region worship mahua (Bassia latifolia) and kadamba (Anthocaphalus cadamba) trees, and the tribals of Odisha and Bihar worship the tamarind (Tamarindus indica) and mango (Mangifera indica) trees during weddings. To many of us, peepal and banyan trees are considered sacred.
Indian society comprises several cultures, each with its own set of traditional methods of conserving nature and its creations. Sacred qualities are often ascribed to springs, mountain peaks, plants and animals which are closely protected. You will find troops of macaques and langurs around many temples. They are fed daily and treated as a part of temple devotees. In and around Bishnoi villages in Rajasthan, herds of blackbuck (chinkara), nilgai and peacocks can be seen as an integral part of the community and nobody harms them.

35.1 How do sacred groves show the inter-connectivity of spirituality and ecology ? (1)
35.2 How do tribal practices promote conservation of forests ? (1)
35.3 Why is conservation of wildlife important for all of us ? Explain. (2)

Concept used: Sacred groves (Topic 6); why we conserve (Topic 1).

  1. 1

    35.1 A spiritual belief (nature worship: all creations of nature must be protected) keeps the groves untouched, so their ecology survives in pristine form.

  2. 2

    35.2 Tribes revere particular trees — the Mundas and Santhal worship mahua and kadamba; tribals of Odisha and Bihar worship tamarind and mango — and leave groves untouched, with any interference banned.

  3. 3

    35.3 From your textbook: conservation preserves ecological diversity and our life support systems — water, air and soil; it preserves genetic diversity for growth and breeding, on which agriculture and fisheries depend.

  4. Check: 35.3 has 2 marks: two different reasons, not one reason in two sentences.

    Common trap: answering 35.3 from the passage only ("nobody harms them"). The question asks why conservation matters — use the textbook's reasons.
