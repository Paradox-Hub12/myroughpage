Sacred groves and good practices

About 35 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain how sacred groves and traditional beliefs protect nature, with the examples in your textbook.
  • Suggest measures and good practices to conserve forests and wildlife, matching each to the problem it solves.

In many Indian villages there is a patch of forest that nobody cuts — not because a guard stands there, but because people believe it belongs to a god or goddess. Near some temples, monkeys are fed every day; around some villages, antelopes graze among the fields and nobody harms them. Belief, it turns out, can be one of the strongest ways to protect nature.

  1. Sacred groves (NCERT box "Sacred groves — a wealth of diverse and rare species")

  • Nature worship is an age old tribal belief based on the premise that all creations of nature have to be protected.
  • Such beliefs have preserved several virgin forests in pristine form called Sacred Groves — "the forests of God and Goddesses".
  • These patches of forest, or parts of large forests, have been left untouched by the local people, and any interference with them is banned.
  • That is why the box calls them a wealth of diverse and rare species.

  1. Who reveres what (your textbook)

People What they revere or protect
Mundas and the Santhal of the Chota Nagpur region mahua (Bassia latifolia) and kadamba trees
Tribals of Odisha and Bihar tamarind (Tamarindus indica) and mango (Mangifera indica) trees, worshipped during weddings
Many of us peepal and banyan trees, considered sacred
People around many temples troops of macaques and langurs, fed daily and treated as a part of temple devotees
Bishnoi villages in Rajasthan herds of blackbuck (chinkara), nilgai and peacocks — an integral part of the community; nobody harms them
  • Indian society comprises several cultures, each with its own set of traditional methods of conserving nature and its creations.
  • Sacred qualities are often ascribed to springs, mountain peaks, plants and animals, which are closely protected.

Note on the textbook's wording. The textbook prints the kadamba's scientific name as "Anthocaphalus cadamba"; many books spell it Anthocephalus. You need the common names (mahua, kadamba, tamarind, mango) in the exam.

Memory hook — "M-K for Munda, T-M for weddings": Mahua and Kadamba — Mundas and Santhal; Tamarind and Mango — tribals of Odisha and Bihar, at weddings.

  1. A tree that gives without asking (NCERT p. 17)

Your textbook quotes Gautama Buddha (487 B.C.): the tree is an organism of unlimited kindness and benevolence that makes no demand for its sustenance, gives generously the products of its life, and offers shade even to the axemen who destroy it. The quotation asks you to see how much a tree gives and how little it asks.

  1. Good practices — problem → measure

When a question says "suggest measures" or "good practices", write cures, not problems. Every measure below is from your textbook; the left column names the problem each measure answers.

Problem Measure or good practice (your textbook)
Poaching and trade in wildlife, skins and bones ban on hunting and restricting trade in wildlife (Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972)
Shrinking habitat legal protection of habitats; national parks, wildlife sanctuaries and tiger reserves (Project Tiger, 1973)
Many species gravely threatened projects for specific animals; protected lists now include insects (1980, 1986) and plants (1991)
Deforestation resisting it, as the Chipko movement did; community afforestation with indigenous species
Degraded forests Joint Forest Management: village institutions protect and restore them
Mining near a reserve villagers citing the Wildlife Protection Act, as at Sariska
Loss of crop varieties; synthetic chemicals diversified crop production without synthetic chemicals (Beej Bachao Andolan, Navdanya)
Forests cleared without care sacred groves left untouched; villages' own rules banning hunting (Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri')
Development that harms people or nature accept only activities that are people centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding

Worked examples

Worked example 1

The eco-club poster

Question. Your school's eco-club is making a poster on sacred groves. Write two points for it explaining how sacred groves help to conserve nature. (2 marks)

Asked: two ways in which sacred groves conserve nature.

Concept used: Sacred groves (section 1).

  1. 1

    The belief: nature worship rests on the belief that all creations of nature have to be protected; it has preserved virgin forests in pristine form.

  2. 2

    The rule: these patches are left untouched by local people and any interference is banned, so diverse and rare species survive in them.

  3. Check: point 1 gives the reason people protect the groves; point 2 gives what they do and the result. Two different points for two marks.

  4. Answer The belief that all of nature must be protected keeps the groves in pristine form; because nobody may touch them, diverse and rare species survive.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Nature worship holds that all creations of nature must be protected, so virgin forests survive in pristine form as sacred groves. (1)
    2. Local people leave these patches untouched and ban any interference, so diverse and rare species survive there. (1)
    Common trap: writing "the government guards sacred groves". Sacred groves are protected by people's beliefs, not by a law.

Worked example 2

A forest under pressure

Question. In a forest, wild animals are still being hunted for meat and skins, and trees are being cut without permission. Suggest three measures from your textbook to protect this forest and its wildlife. (3 marks)

Asked: three measures for two problems (hunting; cutting trees).

Concept used: Problem → measure (section 4).

  1. 1

    For the hunting: enforce the ban on hunting and the restrictions on trade in wildlife under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972.

  2. 2

    For the habitat: give the forest legal protection — for example as a wildlife sanctuary or national park.

  3. 3

    For the trees: involve local people — village institutions under Joint Forest Management, or community afforestation with indigenous species as the Chipko movement showed.

  4. Check: every point is a cure (enforce, protect, involve, replant), and the two problems in the question are both covered. "Hunting" or "cutting trees" written as a point would be the problem, not a measure.

  5. Answer Enforce the ban on hunting and trade; protect the habitat by law as a sanctuary or park; involve villagers through JFM and community afforestation.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Enforce the ban on hunting and the restrictions on trade in wildlife under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972. (1)
    2. Give the habitat legal protection by declaring the area a wildlife sanctuary or national park. (1)
    3. Involve local village institutions through Joint Forest Management, and replant with indigenous species. (1)
    Common trap: writing "poaching" and "deforestation" as two of the three points. Those are the problems.

Common mistakes!

  • "The tribals of Odisha and Bihar worship mahua and kadamba"; "Bishnois protect macaques".

    Mundas and Santhal (Chota Nagpur) — mahua, kadamba; Odisha and Bihar tribals — tamarind, mango at weddings; Bishnoi (Rajasthan) — blackbuck (chinkara), nilgai, peacocks; macaques and langurs — around temples.

    Learn each people with its tree or animal (V13).

  • "Sacred groves are protected by the government."

    They are protected by beliefs: left untouched, interference banned.

    Write "nature worship" and "left untouched" in a sacred-groves answer.

  • Writing "poaching", "mining" or "deforestation" when measures are asked.

    These are problems. A measure is a cure: ban on hunting, protected habitats, reserves, JFM, afforestation with indigenous species.

    Read the command word; use the right-hand column of V14.

Key points

  • Sacred groves = forests of God and Goddesses, kept in pristine form by nature worship; left untouched; interference banned — a wealth of diverse and rare species.
  • Mundas and Santhal (Chota Nagpur): mahua, kadamba. Tribals of Odisha and Bihar: tamarind, mango (weddings). Many of us: peepal, banyan.
  • Macaques and langurs are fed around temples; Bishnoi villages (Rajasthan): blackbuck (chinkara), nilgai, peacocks — nobody harms them.
  • Sacred qualities are given to springs, mountain peaks, plants and animals.
  • Measures: ban on hunting and trade; protected habitats; parks, sanctuaries, reserves; Chipko and community afforestation; JFM; citing the law (Sariska); crops without synthetic chemicals; people-centric, environment-friendly development.
