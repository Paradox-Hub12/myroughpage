About the question text

Each NCERT question is shown in full above its solution, with its textbook location. The solutions are our own.

How every solution is laid out: Asked → Concept used → Understand it (the reasoning, one step per line) → Check → ✅ Final answer → Write it in the exam (numbered points sized to the marks, with the mark for each point) → Common trap. In the board exam, answers must stay within the word limits: 2 marks — up to 40 words, 3 marks — up to 60 words, 5 marks — up to 120 words.

Marks shown in "Write it in the exam" are our estimate of how a board question of this kind is usually marked; NCERT does not give marks. Where a question asks for your own observation or opinion, the answer is marked "One possible answer".

Questions and activities in the chapter

Activity, p. 13Stories of people and nature3 marks

Find out stories prevalent in your region which are about the harmonious relationship between human beings and nature.

Asked: stories from your own region that show people living in harmony with nature.

Concept used: Traditional ways of conserving nature (Topic 6); the web of life (Topic 1).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Ask at home and in your neighbourhood: grandparents, teachers and village elders often know such stories — about a sacred tree, a pond nobody may pollute, a hill or grove nobody may cut.

  2. 2

    Look for the pattern your textbook describes: sacred qualities are given to springs, mountain peaks, plants and animals, which are then closely protected.

  3. 3

    Write each story in three lines: what the story says, what people do because of it, and what part of nature it protects.

  4. Check: a good story names one thing in nature and one thing people do (or do not do) to protect it.

  5. Answer One possible answer: a village grove where no tree may be cut because it belongs to the local deity; a temple pond whose fish are fed and never caught; the textbook's own examples — the Lepcha song to Narak, and the Bishnoi villages where blackbuck, nilgai and peacocks are never harmed.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. One possible answer: in many villages a grove belongs to the local deity, so no tree there may be cut. (1)
    2. Around temples, macaques and langurs are fed daily and treated as part of the devotees. (1)
    3. In Bishnoi villages of Rajasthan, blackbuck, nilgai and peacocks live among people and nobody harms them. (1)
    Common trap: writing a story about people using nature freely. The activity asks for harmony — people protecting what they use.
Activity, p. 15Wildlife sanctuaries and national parks on the map3 marks

Collect more information on the wildlife sanctuaries and national parks of India and cite their locations on the map of India.

Asked: a list of sanctuaries and national parks with their states, marked on a map of India.

Concept used: The tiger reserves and their states (Topic 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Start with the textbook's own list: Corbett National Park (Uttarakhand), Sunderbans National Park (West Bengal), Bandhavgarh National Park (Madhya Pradesh), Sariska Wildlife Sanctuary (Rajasthan), Manas Tiger Reserve (Assam), Periyar Tiger Reserve (Kerala).

  2. 2

    Add Kaziranga National Park (NCERT Fig. 2.2). Beyond your textbook: it is in Assam.

  3. 3

    Take an outline map of India, find each state, and mark the park inside it with a small symbol and its name. The tiger-reserve trainer in Topic 3 shows approximate locations.

  4. 4

    To add more, use your school atlas and list each park with its state.

  5. Check: every park on your map must sit inside its own state. Check each pair against the table in Topic 3.

  6. Answer A map with at least the six reserves of the textbook marked in Uttarakhand, West Bengal, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Assam and Kerala (and Kaziranga, in Assam — beyond your textbook), each labelled.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Corbett National Park — Uttarakhand; Sariska Wildlife Sanctuary — Rajasthan. (1)
    2. Bandhavgarh National Park — Madhya Pradesh; Sunderbans National Park — West Bengal. (1)
    3. Manas Tiger Reserve — Assam; Periyar Tiger Reserve — Kerala. (1)
    Common trap: placing Periyar in Tamil Nadu. It is in Kerala.
Box question, p. 16The gharial and the Yamuna birds3 marks

Can you find out the reasons for the above mentioned problems?

(The box above the question shows two newspaper headlines: "Gharial on the brink" — the gharial population is at its lowest since the 1970s — and "Bird deaths blamed on dirty Yamuna", with the line "Delhi Govt Report Points To Toxic Elements In Stagnant Water".)

Asked: reasons for the fall of the gharial and the death of birds near the Yamuna.

Concept used: Threats to wildlife (Topics 1 and 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Read the headlines first: the second one names its own reason — toxic elements in stagnant, dirty river water.

  2. 2

    Use the textbook's list of threats for the tiger, which can hurt other animals too: shrinking habitat, depletion of prey base species, growing human population.

  3. 3

    Use the textbook's big reason: forests and wildlife are under great stress, mainly due to insensitivity to our environment.

  4. Check: the question asks you to "find out", so this is your reasoning from the headlines and the textbook — present it as one possible answer, and keep each reason tied to a problem in the box.

  5. Answer One possible answer: polluted, stagnant river water with toxic elements; loss of river habitat and of the fish such animals feed on; and human insensitivity to the environment.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. One possible answer: a Delhi government report pointed to toxic elements in the dirty, stagnant Yamuna water behind the bird deaths. (1)
    2. Shrinking habitat and fewer prey animals, threats the textbook names for the tiger, also weaken river animals like the gharial. (1)
    3. Insensitivity to our environment, such as dumping waste into rivers, puts wildlife under great stress. (1)
    Common trap: writing only "pollution" three times. Give three different reasons.
Activity, p. 17Practices that protect the environment3 marks

Write a short essay on any practices which you may have observed and practised in your everyday lives that conserve and protect the environment around you.

Asked: a short essay on everyday practices that protect the environment.

Concept used: Good practices (Topic 6); traditional conservation (Topics 5 and 6).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    List what you and your family actually do: saving water, switching off lights, carrying a cloth bag, planting and watering a tree, feeding birds, not plucking flowers.

  2. 2

    Add practices you have seen in your community: a tree or grove nobody cuts, a pond kept clean, animals that are never harmed — like the textbook's sacred groves and Bishnoi villages.

  3. 3

    Write the essay in three parts: what the practice is, who does it, and how it helps nature.

  4. Check: each practice must protect something — water, soil, a tree, an animal. Name it.

  5. Answer One possible answer: an essay on planting and caring for trees of local kinds, saving water, using cloth bags instead of plastic, and protecting a village grove that nobody cuts.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. One possible answer: my family plants trees of local kinds and waters them until they grow strong. (1)
    2. We save water and carry cloth bags, so less waste reaches our soil and rivers. (1)
    3. Our village protects an old grove nobody may cut, like the sacred groves in the textbook. (1)
    Common trap: describing what the government should do. The activity asks about your own everyday life.

Exercise 1 — Multiple choice question

Exercise 1 (i)Which strategy does not involve the community?MCQ1 mark

Which of the following conservation strategies do not directly involve community participation?

Choose your answer first — the full solution opens after.

Joint forest management depends on local (village) institutions that protect degraded forest land.
The Beej Bachao Andolan is a farmers' and citizens' group in Tehri.
The Chipko movement is a people's movement in the Himalayas.
Demarcating wildlife sanctuaries is done by governments; communities do not directly take part.

Asked: the strategy in which communities do not directly take part.

Concept used: Government measures and community efforts (Topics 2 and 5).

  1. Understand it:

    • (a) ✘ Joint forest management depends on local (village) institutions that protect degraded forest land.
    • (b) ✘ The Beej Bachao Andolan is a farmers' and citizens' group in Tehri.
    • (c) ✘ The Chipko movement is a people's movement in the Himalayas.
    • (d) ✔ Demarcating wildlife sanctuaries is done by governments; communities do not directly take part.

  2. Check: three of the four are things people do themselves; only (d) is an act of government.

  3. Answer (d) Demarcation of Wildlife sanctuaries

    Write it in the exam (1 mark):

    (d)

    Demarcation of Wildlife sanctuaries (1)

    Common trap: choosing (a) because the forest department is involved in JFM. JFM still directly involves village communities.

Exercise 2 — Match the following

Exercise 2Kinds of forest1 mark

Match the following.
Reserved forests Other forests and wastelands belonging to both government and private individuals and communities.
Protected forests Forests are regarded as most valuable as far as the conservation of forest and wildlife resources.
Unclassed forests Forest lands are protected from any further depletion.

Asked: the right description for each kind of forest (the right-hand column is printed in a jumbled order).

Concept used: Three kinds of forest (Topic 4).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Reserved forests → "regarded as most valuable as far as the conservation of forest and wildlife resources" (the second description).

  2. 2

    Protected forests → "protected from any further depletion" (the third description).

  3. 3

    Unclassed forests → "other forests and wastelands belonging to both government and private individuals and communities" (the first description).

  4. Check: each key word belongs to one kind only — "most valuable" (reserved), "further depletion" (protected), "other forests and wastelands" (unclassed).

  5. Answer Reserved — most valuable for conservation; Protected — protected from further depletion; Unclassed — other forests and wastelands of government, private individuals and communities.

    Write it in the exam (1 mark):

    Reserved forests — most valuable for the conservation of forest and wildlife resources; Protected forests — protected from any further depletion; Unclassed forests — other forests and wastelands belonging to government, private individuals and communities. (1)

    Common trap: reading straight across the table. The right-hand column is jumbled on purpose; match by the key words.

Exercise 3 — Answer in about 30 words

Exercise 3 (i)Biodiversity and why it matters2 marks

What is biodiversity? Why is biodiversity important for human lives?

Asked: two parts — the meaning of biodiversity, and why it matters to people.

Concept used: Biodiversity box and the web of life (Topic 1).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Meaning: biodiversity is the rich variety of wildlife and cultivated species, diverse in form and function but closely integrated through a network of interdependencies.

  2. 2

    Importance: humans are only a part of this web and depend on it — plants, animals and micro-organisms re-create the air we breathe, the water we drink and the soil that produces our food.

  3. Check: one point answers "what", the other "why". An answer with two "what" points misses half the question.

  4. Answer Biodiversity is the variety of wild and cultivated species linked by interdependencies; it matters because our air, water and food depend on it.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Biodiversity is the rich variety of wild and cultivated species, diverse in form and function but linked through interdependencies. (1)
    2. It matters because plants, animals and micro-organisms re-create the air, water and soil on which human life depends. (1)
    Common trap: defining biodiversity as "wild animals only". Cultivated species are part of it.
Exercise 3 (ii)Human activities and the loss of flora and fauna3 marks

How have human activities affected the depletion of flora and fauna? Explain.

Asked: the human activities that have reduced plants and animals.

Concept used: The threats your textbook names (Topics 1, 3 and 5).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Hunting and trade: poaching for trade, the trade of tiger skins and the use of bones in traditional medicines left the tiger on the verge of extinction (55,000 → 1,827).

  2. 2

    Human pressure: a growing human population, shrinking habitat and the depletion of prey base species — three threats your textbook lists side by side.

  3. 3

    Mining and felling: villagers at Sariska had to fight mining; the Chipko movement had to resist deforestation.

  4. 4

    The root cause: forests and wildlife are under great stress, mainly due to insensitivity to our environment.

  5. Check: every point names a human activity and what it did. Your current textbook does not have a separate section on depletion, so this answer uses the threats it names in its boxes and case studies.

  6. Answer Poaching and trade in skins and bones, shrinking habitats with fewer prey, mining and deforestation — all driven by insensitivity to the environment — have depleted flora and fauna.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Poaching and trade in tiger skins and bones left the tiger on the verge of extinction, with only 1,827 by 1973. (1)
    2. Human pressure — a growing human population, shrinking habitat and the depletion of prey base species — threatens wild animals. (1)
    3. Mining near Sariska and deforestation, which Chipko resisted, have destroyed forests and wildlife habitats. (1)
    Common trap: writing measures (bans, reserves). The question asks how human activities caused the depletion.

Exercise 4 — Answer in about 120 words

Exercise 4 (i)How communities have conserved forests and wildlife5 marks

Describe how communities have conserved and protected forests and wildlife in India?

Asked: the ways in which communities have protected forests and wildlife, with examples.

Concept used: Communities and conservation (Topic 5); sacred groves (Topic 6).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Sariska, Rajasthan: villagers fought against mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act.

  2. 2

    Alwar district: five villages declared 1,200 hectares as the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri', with their own rules that do not allow hunting, protecting wildlife against outside encroachments.

  3. 3

    Chipko movement (Himalayas): resisted deforestation and showed that community afforestation with indigenous species can be enormously successful.

  4. 4

    Beej Bachao Andolan (Tehri) and Navdanya: showed that diversified crops without synthetic chemicals are possible and economically viable.

  5. 5

    JFM and sacred groves: under Joint Forest Management (since 1988, Odisha first) village institutions protect degraded forests; sacred groves are left untouched by belief.

  6. Check: five different communities or practices, each with a place or a name and what the people did. One example repeated five times would not describe "how communities" (plural) have conserved forests.

  7. Answer By fighting mining (Sariska), declaring their own sanctuary (Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'), resisting deforestation and replanting (Chipko), farming without synthetic chemicals (Beej Bachao Andolan, Navdanya), and protecting forests through JFM and sacred groves.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. In Sariska, Rajasthan, villagers fought against mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act. (1)
    2. Five villages of Alwar declared 1,200 hectares as the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri', banning hunting and outside encroachments. (1)
    3. The Chipko movement in the Himalayas resisted deforestation and showed community afforestation with indigenous species can succeed. (1)
    4. The Beej Bachao Andolan in Tehri and Navdanya showed diversified crop production without synthetic chemicals is viable. (1)
    5. Under Joint Forest Management, village institutions protect degraded forests; sacred groves are left untouched by belief. (1)

    Note on the textbook question. The question is printed with a question mark after "in India", though it begins with "Describe". Answer it as a description.

    Common trap: writing government measures (the 1972 Act, Project Tiger). The question asks about communities.
Exercise 4 (ii)Good practices for conserving forest and wildlife5 marks

Write a note on good practices towards conserving forest and wildlife.

Asked: a note on good practices — by the government, by communities and by each of us.

Concept used: Problem → measure (Topic 6); the Act and projects (Topic 2); communities (Topic 5).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Law: the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 protects habitats, publishes a list of protected species, bans hunting and restricts trade in wildlife.

  2. 2

    Protected places and projects: national parks, wildlife sanctuaries and Project Tiger; projects for the rhinoceros, hangul, crocodiles and Asiatic lion.

  3. 3

    Whole biodiversity: insects (1980, 1986) and plants (1991) added to the protected list.

  4. 4

    Communities: Chipko and community afforestation with indigenous species; JFM for degraded forests; Beej Bachao Andolan and Navdanya — crops without synthetic chemicals.

  5. 5

    Traditional beliefs and choices: sacred groves left untouched; Bishnoi villages where nobody harms blackbuck, nilgai and peacocks; accept only people centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding activities.

  6. Check: the five points move from law, to places, to all species, to communities, to beliefs and choices. Each is a practice (something people do), not a problem.

  7. Answer Good practices include the 1972 Act's protections, parks and Project Tiger, protecting insects and plants too, community efforts such as Chipko, JFM and chemical-free farming, and traditional protection such as sacred groves.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. The Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 protects habitats, bans hunting and restricts trade in wildlife. (1)
    2. National parks, wildlife sanctuaries and Project Tiger protect threatened animals and their habitats. (1)
    3. Protection now covers whole biodiversity: insects were added in 1980 and 1986, and plants in 1991. (1)
    4. Communities help: Chipko's afforestation with indigenous species, JFM on degraded forests, and farming without synthetic chemicals. (1)
    5. Sacred groves and Bishnoi villages protect nature by belief; accept only people-centric, environment-friendly development. (1)
    Common trap: listing threats (poaching, mining). A "good practice" is something that protects.
