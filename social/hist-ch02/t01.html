Topic 1 of 7

The war, satyagraha and the first satyagrahas

About 35 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why the growth of nationalism in colonies is linked to the anti-colonial movement.
  • Describe the economic and political situation the First World War created in India.
  • Explain the idea of satyagraha (Source A).
  • Describe Gandhiji's satyagrahas at Champaran (1917), Ahmedabad (1918) and Kheda (1918) — place, people and demand.

Imagine a village in 1918. The price of grain and cloth has doubled in five years. A new tax has arrived. Officials have come to take young men away to fight in a war far from home, and now the crops have failed and a fever is spreading from house to house. Nobody in the village has heard the word "nationalism" — but everybody is angry with the government. This is where the story of this chapter begins.

Quick reminder (History Chapter 1 and earlier classes)

Nationalism is the feeling of people that they belong to one nation and share a common identity. In Europe it grew with the making of nation-states. India in 1914 was a colony — it was ruled by Britain, through a viceroy and British officials, for Britain's benefit.

  1. Why nationalism in a colony is tied to the fight against colonial rule

In Europe, people discovered their nation while building nation-states. In India, as in many other colonies, people discovered their unity in the struggle against colonialism:

  • One shared bond: the feeling of being oppressed under colonial rule tied many different groups together.
  • Many different experiences: each class and group felt colonial rule differently, so their ideas of freedom were not the same.
  • One movement to join them: under Mahatma Gandhi, the Congress worked to unite these groups in a single movement — but, as you will see, the unity was never free of conflict.

So the growth of modern nationalism in India is closely connected to the anti-colonial movement.

  1. What the First World War did to India (1914–1918 and after)

The war changed India's economy and politics in four ways:

What happened What it meant for people
Defence expenditure rose hugely, paid for by war loans and higher taxes — customs duties went up and a new income tax began ordinary people paid more
Prices doubled between 1913 and 1918 extreme hardship for the common people
Villages had to supply soldiers — forced recruitment in rural areas widespread anger in the countryside
Harvests failed in many regions in 1918–19 and 1920–21, and an influenza epidemic spread acute food shortages; according to the census of 1921, 12 to 13 million people died of famines and the epidemic

People hoped their hardships would end when the war was over. They did not. That anger was ready to be turned into a movement — and a new leader offered a new way to struggle.

New word. Forced recruitment — when the colonial government made people join the army against their will.

  1. The idea of satyagraha

In January 1915 Mahatma Gandhi came back to India from South Africa. There he had fought a racist government using a new kind of mass protest, which he named satyagraha (NCERT Fig. 2 shows Indian workers marching through Volksrust in South Africa in 1913, during one of his satyagrahas).

What satyagraha means:

  1. Truth has power, and people must keep searching for the truth.
  2. If the cause is true and the struggle is against injustice, then physical force is not needed to fight the oppressor.
  3. Without seeking revenge or being aggressive, a satyagrahi wins through non-violence — by appealing to the oppressor's conscience — his sense of right and wrong.
  4. People, including the oppressors, are persuaded to see the truth, not forced to accept it through violence. In the end, truth is bound to win.

Gandhiji was convinced that non-violence, as a dharma, could bring all Indians together.

Reading Source A (Mahatma Gandhi on Satyagraha). In this source Gandhiji says that satyagraha is not "passive resistance", which people call a weapon of the weak. It "calls for intense activity" and can be used only by the strong. A satyagrahi causes no pain to the opponent and has no ill-will; satyagraha is "pure soul-force". He also argues that India cannot match Britain in weapons, but its millions have made non-violence their own. What the source tells us: for Gandhiji, non-violence was an active, courageous method of struggle, not a sign of weakness.

  1. The first three satyagrahas in India

After his return, Gandhiji organised satyagraha movements in different places. Learn them as place + year + people + demand:

Place and year State People and demand
Champaran, 1917 Bihar peasants — against the oppressive plantation system (the CBSE sample paper calls them indigo peasants)
Ahmedabad, 1918 Gujarat cotton mill workers — a satyagraha movement amongst the mill workers
Kheda (district), 1918 Gujarat peasants hit by crop failure and a plague epidemic — they could not pay the revenue and demanded that revenue collection be relaxed

Memory hook — C-A-K: Champaran (1917) → Ahmedabad (1918) → Kheda (1918), the order in which your textbook tells them. Only Champaran is outside Gujarat.

These successes emboldened Gandhiji to plan a nationwide satyagraha in 1919 (Topic 2).

Worked example 1

From war prices to anger

Question. "The First World War made the common people of India angry with British rule even before the war ended." Give three reasons from the economic effects of the war. (3 marks)

Asked: three economic effects of the war that caused anger.

Concept used: Effects of the First World War (this topic, section 2). Why it applies: the question asks for economic effects only, so forced recruitment (a political effect) is kept out unless it is linked to money.

  1. 1

    Higher taxes: to pay for huge defence spending, the government raised customs duties, introduced income tax and took war loans — people had to pay more.

  2. 2

    Rising prices: prices doubled between 1913 and 1918, causing extreme hardship.

  3. 3

    Food shortages: crops failed in 1918–19 and 1920–21, causing acute shortages of food; with the influenza epidemic, 12 to 13 million people died (census of 1921).

  4. Check: each point names an effect and says how it hurt ordinary people; there are three points for three marks. If a point only said "the war happened", it would earn nothing — it is not an effect.

  5. Answer (1) Higher taxes and war loans to pay for defence; (2) prices doubled between 1913 and 1918; (3) crop failures and food shortages, with millions dying of famine and epidemic.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Defence spending rose sharply and was paid for by war loans, higher customs duties and a new income tax. (1)
    2. Prices doubled between 1913 and 1918, causing extreme hardship for the common people. (1)
    3. Crops failed in 1918–19 and 1920–21, causing acute food shortages; with influenza, 12–13 million people died. (1)
    Common trap: writing "the war ended and things improved". The textbook says hardships did not end after the war — that is exactly why anger grew.

Worked example 2

Reading Source A

Question. In Source A, Gandhiji writes that India "cannot rival Britain or Europe in force of arms", while "the hundreds of millions in India can never carry arms". How does this line support his choice of non-violence as India's method of struggle? (2 marks)

Asked: how the line about arms supports non-violence.

Concept used: The idea of satyagraha (this topic, section 3). Why it applies: the line compares two kinds of strength.

  1. 1

    The comparison: in weapons, Britain was far stronger; an armed fight was one India could not win.

  2. 2

    The conclusion: India's strength lay in its numbers and its people's soul-force — millions who had made non-violence their own could resist without arms, through satyagraha.

  3. Check: the answer must use both halves of the line — Britain's arms and India's millions. An answer that says "Gandhiji wanted India to build an army" contradicts the line itself.

  4. Answer An armed struggle could not succeed against Britain's military power, but India's vast numbers, united in non-violence, could resist through satyagraha.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Gandhiji argued that India could never match Britain or Europe in weapons, so an armed struggle could not succeed. (1)
    2. India's strength lay in its millions, who had made non-violence their own; their united soul-force made satyagraha the right method. (1)
    Common trap: reading the line as "Indians are weak". Gandhiji's point is the opposite — non-violence is a strength of the many.

Worked example 3

Sorting the three satyagrahas

Question. Match each group with its satyagraha and give the year: (i) workers in textile mills; (ii) peasants who could not pay land revenue after crop failure and plague; (iii) peasants struggling against an oppressive plantation system.

Asked: the satyagraha and year for each group.

Concept used: The C-A-K table (this topic, section 4).

  1. 1

    (i) Mill workers → Ahmedabad, 1918 (cotton mill workers, Gujarat).

  2. 2

    (ii) Revenue after crop failure and plague → Kheda, 1918 (Gujarat).

  3. 3

    (iii) Plantation system → Champaran, 1917 (Bihar).

  4. Check: the only 1917 satyagraha is the only one outside Gujarat — Champaran; if your answer put the plantation struggle in Gujarat or in 1918, it does not fit the table.

  5. Answer (i) Ahmedabad, 1918; (ii) Kheda, 1918; (iii) Champaran, 1917.

    Common trap: linking "peasants" automatically with Champaran. Kheda was a peasant satyagraha too — about revenue.

Common mistakes!

  • Champaran called a Gujarat revenue struggle; Kheda given to mill workers.

    Champaran (Bihar, 1917) was against the plantation system; Kheda (Gujarat, 1918) was about revenue; Ahmedabad (1918) was the mill workers'.

    Learn C-A-K with place + people + demand.

  • "Satyagraha is passive resistance."

    Gandhiji says it calls for intense activity and is used only by the strong.

    Write: active, non-violent soul-force.

  • "The war's hardships ended in 1918."

    Crops failed again in 1920–21 and hardships did not end, which fed the anger.

    Link war effects → continuing hardship → anger.

  • Listing "the war" itself as a cause of anger.

    The war is the event; its effects (taxes, prices, recruitment, shortages) caused anger.

    Write each effect and how it hurt people.

Key points

  • In India, as in other colonies, nationalism grew through the struggle against colonialism; the shared sense of oppression united groups, but their ideas of freedom differed.
  • The First World War raised defence spending (war loans, customs duties, income tax), doubled prices (1913–1918), brought forced recruitment, and was followed by crop failures and an influenza epidemic (12–13 million deaths, census of 1921).
  • Gandhiji returned in January 1915 with satyagraha: the power of truth, non-violence, and an appeal to the oppressor's conscience — active, not passive (Source A).
  • First satyagrahas: Champaran 1917 (Bihar, plantation system) → Ahmedabad 1918 (cotton mill workers) → Kheda 1918 (Gujarat, relaxation of revenue).
