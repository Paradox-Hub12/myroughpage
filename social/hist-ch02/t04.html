Topic 4 of 7

Towards Civil Disobedience: the Salt March

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the developments of 1922–1930 — the Swaraj Party, the radicals, the Depression, the Simon Commission, Irwin's offer and the Lahore Congress.
  • Explain why salt was chosen and describe the Salt March.
  • Describe how the Civil Disobedience Movement spread, how it differed from Non-Cooperation, and how it ended; describe the HSRA.

Every family in India — rich or poor — needs salt every day. In 1930 the British government taxed it and did not allow anyone else to make it. So when Gandhiji bent down on a beach and made salt from sea water, he did something every Indian understood at once: he broke an unjust law in the simplest possible way. How did the national movement get from 1922 to that beach?

  1. After Non-Cooperation (1922–1928): a divided Congress

After the withdrawal in February 1922, the Congress was divided:

Group Leaders What they wanted
Swaraj Party (formed within the Congress) C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru a return to council politics — take seats in the provincial councils created under the Government of India Act of 1919, oppose British policies from inside, argue for reform, and show that the councils were not truly democratic
Younger, radical leaders Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose more radical mass agitation and full independence

  1. Two factors that reshaped politics in the late 1920s

Factor 1 — the Depression. A slump hit the whole world. Prices of farm produce started falling in 1926 and crashed after 1930. With less demand at home and abroad, peasants struggled to sell their crops and pay their revenue. By 1930 the countryside was in turmoil.

Factor 2 — the Simon Commission. Britain's new Tory government appointed a Statutory Commission, headed by Sir John Simon, to study how India's constitutional system was working and propose changes. The problem: not one member was Indian — every member was British.

  • When it arrived in 1928, it was greeted with "Go back Simon". Every party — the Congress and the Muslim League included — took part in the protests.
  • Box — Lala Lajpat Rai: he was assaulted by the British police during a peaceful demonstration against the Simon Commission and died of his injuries.
  • Irwin's offer, October 1929: the viceroy, Lord Irwin, made a vague offer of "dominion status" at some future date, with no date given, and promised a Round Table Conference to discuss a constitution. This did not satisfy the Congress leaders.

  1. Purna Swaraj — Lahore, December 1929

  • The radicals (Nehru, Bose) became more assertive; the liberals and moderates, who wanted a constitution within British dominion, lost influence.
  • At its Lahore session in December 1929, with Jawaharlal Nehru as president, the Congress made "Purna Swaraj" (full independence) its formal demand.
  • 26 January 1930 was to be celebrated as Independence Day, when people would take a pledge to fight for complete independence (Source C, the Independence Day Pledge, says that if any government deprives a people of their rights, the people may alter or abolish it, and that India must "sever the British connection").
  • But the celebrations attracted very little attention. Gandhiji had to link this abstract idea of freedom to concrete issues of everyday life.

  1. Why salt? — the Salt March

31 January 1930: Gandhiji wrote to Viceroy Irwin listing eleven demands. Some concerned everyone; others were the particular demands of different classes, from industrialists to peasants — so that every class could see its own interest in the campaign and join it.

The most stirring demand: abolish the salt tax. Why salt was a powerful symbol:

  1. Rich and poor alike used salt every day.
  2. It was among the most basic necessities of food.
  3. Taxing salt, and allowing only the government to produce it, showed — in Gandhiji's words — the most oppressive face of British rule.

The ultimatum: if the demands were not met by 11 March, the Congress would launch civil disobedience. Irwin was unwilling to negotiate.

The march (NCERT Fig. 7):

Fact Value
Volunteers with Gandhiji 78 trusted volunteers
From → to Gandhiji's ashram in Sabarmati → the Gujarati coastal town of Dandi
Distance over 240 miles
Time 24 days of walking — roughly 10 miles each day
On the way at every halt, thousands gathered; he told them what swaraj meant and asked them to defy the British peacefully
6 April 1930 at Dandi he broke the law in a public ceremony — boiling sea water to make salt

With this act, the Civil Disobedience Movement began.

  1. How Civil Disobedience differed from Non-Cooperation

Non-Cooperation (1921–22) Civil Disobedience (1930–34)
What people were asked to do refuse cooperation with the British refuse cooperation and break colonial laws
Typical actions give up titles; boycott schools, courts, councils, foreign goods make salt, break the salt law, refuse revenue and chaukidari taxes, break forest laws, plus boycott and picketing

How it spread: across the country, thousands made their own salt and held protests outside government salt factories; foreign cloth was boycotted; liquor shops picketed; peasants refused to pay revenue and chaukidari taxes; village officials resigned; forest people violated forest laws (entered Reserved Forests to collect wood and graze cattle).

Repression and violence:

  • The government began arresting Congress leaders one by one, which led to violent clashes.
  • April 1930: the arrest of Abdul Ghaffar Khan, a devoted follower of Gandhiji, brought angry crowds onto the streets of Peshawar; they faced armoured cars and police bullets, and many died.
  • A month later Gandhiji himself was arrested. In Sholapur, factory workers attacked police posts, municipal offices, lawcourts and railway stations — buildings that stood for British rule.
  • The government answered with brutal repression: peaceful satyagrahis attacked, women and children beaten, about 100,000 people arrested (NCERT Fig. 8).

  1. How it ended

When Event
5 March 1931 Gandhi–Irwin Pact: Gandhiji called off the movement and agreed to attend a Round Table Conference in London (the Congress had boycotted the first one); the government agreed to release political prisoners
December 1931 Gandhiji went to London for the (second) conference; negotiations broke down; he returned disappointed
Back in India repression had started again: Ghaffar Khan and Nehru were in jail, the Congress had been declared illegal, and meetings, demonstrations and boycotts were banned
1932 Gandhiji relaunched the Civil Disobedience Movement
By 1934 the movement lost its momentum

  1. Box 1 — "To the altar of this revolution we have brought our youth as incense"

Many nationalists believed the struggle could not be won through non-violence.

  • 1928: at a meeting in Ferozeshah Kotla ground, Delhi, revolutionaries founded the Hindustan Socialist Republican Army (HSRA). Leaders: Bhagat Singh, Jatin Das and Ajoy Ghosh.
  • It struck at symbols of British power: in April 1929 Bhagat Singh and Batukeswar Dutta exploded a bomb inside the Legislative Assembly; the same year there was an attempt to blow up the train in which Lord Irwin was travelling.
  • The colonial government tried and executed Bhagat Singh at the age of 23. At his trial he said he did not wish to glorify "the cult of the bomb and pistol" but wanted a revolution in society.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

"Go back Simon"

Question. Why was the Simon Commission greeted with the slogan "Go back Simon" by all Indian parties? (2 marks)

Asked: the reason for the protest.

Concept used: The Simon Commission (section 2). Why it applies: the protest was about who was on the commission.

  1. 1

    Its job: the Tory government set it up to study India's constitutional system and recommend changes — in other words, to shape India's future.

  2. 2

    The objection: not one of its members was Indian; every member was British. So Indians were to have no say in their own constitution.

  3. Check: the slogan was raised by all parties, including the Muslim League — so the reason must be one every party shared (no Indian member), not a Congress-only demand such as Purna Swaraj.

  4. Answer It was to suggest constitutional changes for India, yet it had no Indian member — Indians would have no voice in deciding their own future.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The Simon Commission was set up to review the working of India's constitutional system and suggest changes for the future. (1)
    2. It had not a single Indian member, so all parties, including the Congress and the Muslim League, opposed it. (1)
    Common trap: writing "because it was set up by a Tory government". The textbook's reason is no Indian member.

Worked example 2

A new kind of movement

Question. "Civil Disobedience asked more of the people than Non-Cooperation had." Explain with three examples. (3 marks)

Asked: how the Civil Disobedience Movement went further than the Non-Cooperation Movement, with examples.

Concept used: The comparison table (section 5).

  1. 1

    The difference: in 1921–22 people were asked only to refuse cooperation; in 1930 they were asked also to break colonial laws.

  2. 2

    Example — salt: thousands broke the salt law, made salt and demonstrated at government salt factories.

  3. 3

    Example — taxes and forests: peasants stopped paying revenue and chaukidari taxes, and forest dwellers went into Reserved Forests for wood and grazing — against the forest laws.

  4. Check: each example must be a law being broken, not just a boycott; "boycott of foreign cloth" alone would not show the difference, because both movements used it.

  5. Answer Non-Cooperation meant refusing to cooperate; Civil Disobedience also meant breaking laws — the salt law, revenue and chaukidari tax laws, and forest laws.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. In Non-Cooperation (1921–22) people only refused cooperation; in Civil Disobedience (1930) they also broke colonial laws. (1)
    2. Thousands broke the salt law, manufactured salt and demonstrated in front of government salt factories. (1)
    3. Peasants refused revenue and chaukidari taxes, and forest people violated forest laws by entering Reserved Forests for wood and grazing. (1)
    Common trap: giving only boycott examples — boycott was common to both movements.

Worked example 3

Order of 1928–1932

Question. Arrange in order, earliest first: I. Gandhi–Irwin Pact. II. Simon Commission arrives in India. III. Lahore Congress demands Purna Swaraj. IV. Gandhiji relaunches Civil Disobedience after the conference in London fails.

Asked: the chronological order.

Concept used: The mini-timeline (this topic).

  1. 1

    Date each: II — 1928; III — December 1929; I — 5 March 1931; IV — 1932.

  2. 2

    Sort: 1928 → 1929 → 1931 → 1932.

  3. Check: the Pact sent Gandhiji to the Round Table Conference, and the relaunch came after it failed — so I must come before IV ✔.

  4. Answer II, III, I, IV

    Common trap: putting the Pact (1931) before Lahore (1929) because the Pact "sounds earlier". The Pact ended the first phase of the movement that Lahore made possible.

Common mistakes!

  • "Civil Disobedience only asked people not to cooperate."

    It also asked people to break colonial laws.

    Write refuse cooperation + break laws.

  • "The Swaraj Party was formed by Nehru and Bose."

    Its founders, C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru, wanted council politics; Nehru and Bose were radicals.

    Swaraj Party = Das + Motilal Nehru, councils.

  • Mixing December 1929 and 26 January 1930.

    Lahore (December 1929) demanded Purna Swaraj; 26 January 1930 was celebrated as Independence Day.

    Demand first, celebration next month.

  • Mixing the 1931 Pact and the 1932 Poona Pact.

    Gandhi–Irwin Pact: 5 March 1931; Poona Pact: September 1932 (Topic 5).

    Irwin 1931, Poona 1932.

  • "The Simon Commission was opposed because it was set up by a Tory government."

    The textbook's reason is that it had not a single Indian member.

    Write: no Indian member — all British.

Key points

  • After 1922: Swaraj Party (C. R. Das, Motilal Nehru) for council politics vs radicals (Nehru, Bose) for mass agitation and full independence.
  • Depression: farm prices fell from 1926, collapsed after 1930; countryside in turmoil.
  • Simon Commission (arrived 1928): no Indian member → "Go back Simon"; Lala Lajpat Rai died of police assault. October 1929: Irwin's vague dominion status offer.
  • Lahore, December 1929 (Nehru): Purna Swaraj; 26 January 1930 Independence Day.
  • Salt: used by all, essential, taxed and monopolised. 11 demands (31 January 1930); deadline 11 March; 78 volunteers, over 240 miles, 24 days, Sabarmati → Dandi, salt law broken 6 April 1930 → Civil Disobedience (break laws, not only refuse cooperation).
  • Gandhi–Irwin Pact, 5 March 1931 → Round Table Conference (December 1931) fails → relaunch 1932 → lost momentum by 1934. HSRA (1928): Bhagat Singh, Jatin Das, Ajoy Ghosh.
