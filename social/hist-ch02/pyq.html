How this chapter is asked

The 2-mark History map question comes from this chapter in every board paper (all 54 sets of 2024–2026). The most asked places are Dandi, where the salt law was broken, and Madras, the session of 1927; Calcutta, Champaran, Amritsar, Nagpur, Kheda and Ahmedabad also appear. In the 2026 papers this chapter gave each set 7 or 8 of the 20 History marks: a 1-mark item, the map, and either a 4-mark source-based question (12 of the 15 sets) or a 5-mark long answer (the three 32/2 sets). Across 2024–2026, its 1-mark items are mostly arrange-in-order questions, with picture items on Bharat Mata and match the person and the event items. The written answers keep returning to a few themes: Non-Cooperation in the towns and the countryside, who joined the Civil Disobedience Movement and why, the Salt March, and the symbols, folklore and history that built a sense of belonging.

About the marks

The sample-paper answers follow the official marking schemes. No marking scheme is published for the board papers, so their mark splits are our marking points, written to the CBSE word limits (2 marks — up to 40 words, 3 marks — up to 60, 5 marks — up to 120, case study — up to 100). Where an official question is printed with a slip, gives a clue that fits two places, or has no option that matches the textbook exactly, it is quoted exactly and a note explains the textbook answer.

CBSE Sample Question Paper 2026-27, Section A, Q7A5 marks

Question: Evaluate the economic consequences of the Non-Cooperation Movement for colonial rule in India.

Asked: the economic effects of Non-Cooperation, judged for what they meant to British rule.

Concept used: The movement in the towns (Topic 3, section 1). Why it applies: the textbook says the effects "on the economic front were more dramatic".

  1. 1

    Boycott and picketing: foreign goods were boycotted, liquor shops picketed and foreign cloth burnt in bonfires.

  2. 2

    Imports fell: foreign cloth imports halved between 1921 and 1922, from Rs 102 crore to Rs 57 crore — a direct blow to British trade.

  3. 3

    Traders joined: in many places merchants and traders refused to trade in foreign goods or finance foreign trade.

  4. 4

    Indian cloth: as people discarded imported clothes, they wore only Indian ones.

  5. 5

    Indian production rose: production of Indian textile mills and handlooms went up.

  6. 6

    "Evaluate": a strong answer also notes the limit — khadi was often costlier than mill cloth, so the poor could not keep up the boycott for long.

  7. Check: every point is economic (trade, imports, production); a point about students leaving schools is about institutions, not the economy, and would not earn a mark here.

    Common trap: writing about Chauri Chaura or the withdrawal. The question asks for economic consequences.
  1. On the economic front the effects were dramatic: foreign goods were boycotted, liquor shops picketed and foreign cloth burnt in huge bonfires. (1)
  1. The import of foreign cloth halved between 1921 and 1922, its value dropping from Rs 102 crore to Rs 57 crore. (1)
  1. In many places merchants and traders refused to trade in foreign goods or finance foreign trade. (1)
  1. As the boycott spread, people discarded imported clothes and wore only Indian ones, shrinking the market for British cloth. (1)
  1. Production of Indian textile mills and handlooms went up, though costly khadi limited how long the poor could keep up the boycott. (1) — 5 marks (one mark per point, as the scheme lists five points)

Note on the marking scheme: its point 5 adds that "making and wearing of Khadi became very popular". Your textbook does not say this; it says khadi "was often more expensive than mass-produced mill cloth and poor people could not afford to buy it". Point 5 above follows the textbook.

CBSE Sample Question Paper 2026-27, Section A, Q7B5 marks

Question: Analyse how Indian merchants and industrialists associated themselves with the Civil Disobedience Movement, highlighting the forms of support they extended and the reasons for their participation.

Asked: (i) why business groups joined; (ii) how they supported the movement; (iii) for "analyse", how their support changed.

Concept used: The business classes (Topic 5, section 1).

  1. 1

    Background: during the First World War, Indian merchants and industrialists made huge profits and became powerful; keen to expand, they reacted against colonial policies that restricted business.

  2. 2

    Their demands: protection against imports of foreign goods, and a rupee–sterling exchange ratio that would discourage imports.

  3. 3

    Organisation: the Indian Industrial and Commercial Congress (1920) and FICCI (1927); led by Purshottamdas Thakurdas and G. D. Birla, they attacked colonial control over the economy.

  4. 4

    Support: they gave financial assistance and refused to buy or sell imported goods; they saw swaraj as a time without colonial restrictions on business.

  5. 5

    Change: after the Round Table Conference failed, they were no longer uniformly enthusiastic — they feared militant activities, long disruption of business and the growing influence of socialism among young Congress members.

  6. Check: the answer covers reasons (points 1–2), forms of support (points 3–4) and the change (point 5); missing any of the three asked parts loses marks.

    Common trap: writing about industrial workers here. The question is about merchants and industrialists, the employers.
  1. During the First World War, Indian merchants and industrialists made huge profits; keen to expand, they opposed colonial policies restricting business. (1)
  1. They wanted protection against foreign imports and a rupee–sterling exchange ratio that would discourage imports. (1)
  1. They formed the Indian Industrial and Commercial Congress (1920) and FICCI (1927); led by Purshottamdas Thakurdas and G. D. Birla, they attacked colonial economic control. (1)
  1. They gave financial assistance and refused to buy or sell imported goods, seeing swaraj as freedom for trade and industry from colonial restrictions. (1)
  1. After the Round Table Conference failed, they cooled, fearing militancy, prolonged disruption of business and socialism among young Congress members. (1) — 5 marks (one mark per point, as the scheme lists five points)

CBSE Sample Question Paper 2025-26, Section A, Q6B3 marks

Question: Salt March 'became an effective tool of resistance against colonialism.' Justify the statement with suitable arguments.

Asked: three arguments for why the Salt March worked.

Concept used: The Salt March (Topic 4).

  1. 1

    Shared symbol: salt was consumed by rich and poor alike — it could unite the nation.

  2. 2

    Clear grievance: of the eleven demands sent to Irwin, abolishing the salt tax was the most stirring; the tax and the monopoly showed the most oppressive face of British rule.

  3. 3

    Mass action: Irwin would not negotiate, so the march began; thousands joined, broke the salt law, made salt and demonstrated at salt factories.

  4. 4

    Wider defiance: people also refused taxes and revenue, picketed liquor shops, boycotted foreign cloth and violated forest laws.

  5. Check: each argument shows why it was effective (it united people, exposed injustice, or spread defiance); a bare narrative of the march does not justify the statement.

    Common trap: describing only the route and the distance. The question asks for arguments.
  1. Salt was consumed by rich and poor alike, so it became a powerful symbol that could unite the nation. (1)
  1. Of Gandhiji's eleven demands, abolishing the salt tax was the most stirring; it showed British rule's most oppressive face. (1)
  1. When Irwin refused to negotiate, thousands broke the salt law, refused taxes, boycotted foreign cloth and picketed liquor shops. (1) — 3 marks (the scheme accepts any three of its five points)

Note on the marking scheme: it says Gandhiji found in salt "a powerful bond" (textbook: "a powerful symbol") and that "Most" of the eleven demands were of general interest (textbook: "Some of these were of general interest; others were specific demands of different classes"). The answer above follows the textbook.

Question: Read the given source carefully and answer the questions that follow :

‘To the altar of this revolution we have brought our youth as incense’ — Many nationalists thought that the struggle against the British could not be won through non-violence. In the year 1928, the Hindustan Socialist Republican Army (HSRA) was founded at a meeting in Ferozeshah Kotla ground in Delhi. Amongst its leaders were Bhagat Singh, Jatin Das and Ajoy Ghosh. In a series of dramatic actions in different parts of India, the HSRA targeted some of the symbols of British power. In April 1929, Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutta threw a bomb in the Legislative Assembly. In the same year there was an attempt to blow up the train that Lord Irwin was travelling in. Bhagat Singh was 23 when he was tried and executed by the colonial government. During his trial, Bhagat Singh stated that he did not wish to glorify ‘the cult of the bomb and pistol’ but wanted a revolution in society – ‘Revolution is the inalienable right of mankind. Freedom is the imprescriptible birthright of all. The labourer is the real sustainer of society. To the altar of this revolution we have brought our youth as incense, for no sacrifice is too great for so magnificent a cause. We are content. We await the advent of revolution. Inquilab Zindabad!’

8.1 Why is the formation of ‘Hindustan Socialist Republican Army (HSRA)’ considered as a turning point in the freedom struggle of India ? (1)
8.2 How did Bhagat Singh’s trial speeches transform him from a revolutionary to a philosopher of freedom ? (1)
8.3 In what way did Bhagat Singh redefine the meaning of ‘revolution’ for his generation ? Explain. (2)

Asked: why the HSRA marked a change; what Bhagat Singh's trial words showed; what "revolution" meant to him.

Concept used: Box 1 (Topic 4). The passage is your textbook's Box 1, so every answer comes from it.

  1. 1

    8.1: the HSRA came from nationalists who felt the struggle could not be won through non-violence. It chose a different path: dramatic actions against symbols of British power.

  2. 2

    8.2: at his trial he said he did not wish to glorify "the cult of the bomb and pistol". He spoke of ideas instead: freedom as the birthright of all.

  3. 3

    8.3: revolution meant a revolution in society, an inalienable right of mankind, in which the labourer is the real sustainer of society. It was worth any sacrifice by the young.

  4. Check: 8.3 carries 2 marks, so give two parts of his meaning (society, not bombs; the labourer and freedom for all).

    Common trap: describing the bomb in the Assembly as the "meaning" of revolution. He said the opposite: he wanted a change in society, not the cult of the bomb.
8.1 Many nationalists felt non-violence could not win freedom; the HSRA (1928) chose dramatic actions against symbols of British power. (1)
8.2 At his trial he rejected glorifying "the cult of the bomb and pistol" and spoke of freedom as everyone's birthright. (1)
8.3 Revolution meant changing society, an inalienable right of mankind (1); the labourer sustains society, and youth must sacrifice for this cause. (1)

Question: “The revival of Indian folklore strengthened nationalism in India.” Support the statement with appropriate arguments.

Asked: five arguments linking the folklore revival to nationalism.

Concept used: The sense of collective belonging, folklore (Topic 6).

  1. 1

    What they did: in late-nineteenth-century India, nationalists recorded folk tales sung by bards and toured villages to gather folk songs and legends.

  2. 2

    Why: they believed these tales gave a true picture of traditional culture, corrupted and damaged by outside forces.

  3. 3

    The link to nationalism: preserving folk tradition helped people discover their national identity and restore pride in their past.

  4. 4

    Bengal: Rabindranath Tagore collected ballads, nursery rhymes and myths and led the folk revival.
