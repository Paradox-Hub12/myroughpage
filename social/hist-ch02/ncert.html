About the question text

Each NCERT question is shown in full above its solution, with its textbook location. The solutions are our own.

How every solution is laid out: Asked → Concept used → Understand it (the reasoning, one step per line) → Check → ✅ Final answer → Write it in the exam (numbered points sized to the marks, with the mark for each point) → Common trap. In the board exam, answers must stay within the word limits: 2 marks — up to 40 words, 3 marks — up to 60 words, 5 marks — up to 120 words.

Marks shown in "Write it in the exam" are our estimate of how a board question of this kind is usually marked; NCERT does not give marks.

Activity p. 31Satyagraha as active resistance3 marks

Read the text carefully. What did Mahatma Gandhi mean when he said satyagraha is active resistance?

Asked: the meaning of "active resistance" in Source A.

Concept used: The idea of satyagraha (Topic 1, section 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    What he rejected: "passive resistance", which people called the weapon of the weak — as if it meant doing nothing.

  2. 2

    What he meant: satyagraha "calls for intense activity" and can be used only by the strong. The satyagrahi acts — protests, disobeys unjust laws, accepts suffering — but without violence and without ill-will.

  3. 3

    Its force: it is "soul-force", based on truth; it works by appealing to the conscience of the oppressor until he sees the truth.

  4. Check: every point describes something the satyagrahi does; an answer like "satyagraha means not reacting at all" describes passive resistance, which Gandhiji rejects in the very first lines.

  5. Answer He meant that satyagraha is not a weapon of the weak: it is a strong, active, non-violent struggle, using truth and soul-force to move the oppressor's conscience.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Gandhiji said satyagraha is not "passive resistance", the so-called weapon of the weak; it can be used only by the strong. (1)
    2. It calls for intense activity — people resist injustice firmly but without violence, revenge or ill-will towards the opponent. (1)
    3. It is soul-force based on truth, which wins by appealing to the conscience of the oppressor rather than by physical force. (1)
    Common trap: writing that satyagraha uses force "only when needed". Source A says it is not physical force at all.
Activity p. 34A poster for students, 19213 marks

The year is 1921. You are a student in a government-controlled school. Design a poster urging school students to answer Gandhiji's call to join the Non-Cooperation Movement.

Asked: a poster with a clear message for students.

Concept used: The idea of non-cooperation (Topic 2, section 5) and the movement in the towns (Topic 3, section 1).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    A headline that says the main idea in a few words.

  2. 2

    The reason, from Hind Swaraj: British rule survives only because Indians cooperate.

  3. 3

    The action students can take: leave government-controlled schools; boycott foreign cloth; picket liquor shops — non-violently.

  4. 4

    Who else is doing it, to show they are not alone: teachers and headmasters resigning, lawyers giving up practice.

  5. Check: the poster must ask students to leave government schools (that is what non-cooperation meant for them) and must stay non-violent; a poster urging them to attack a school would go against Gandhiji's call.

  6. Answer A sample poster:

    STUDENTS OF INDIA — YOUR NATION CALLS!

    British rule stands only because we cooperate. Withdraw that cooperation and swaraj will come within a year.
    Leave government schools and colleges — thousands of students, teachers and headmasters have already done so.
    Boycott foreign cloth. Wear Indian cloth. Stand peacefully with those who picket liquor shops.
    No violence — only truth and courage.
    Join the Non-Cooperation Movement. Mahatma Gandhi has called — answer him!

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Headline and call: "Students of India — leave government schools and join Non-Cooperation!" — because British rule survives only on Indian cooperation. (1)
    2. Actions: walk out of government-controlled schools and colleges, boycott foreign cloth and wear Indian cloth, and support peaceful picketing. (1)
    3. Spirit: thousands have already joined; stay non-violent, as Gandhiji asks, and swaraj will come within a year. (1)
    Common trap: making the poster only about the boycott of foreign cloth. For students, the key step was to leave government schools.
Activity p. 35A peasant in Uttar Pradesh, 19203 marks

If you were a peasant in Uttar Pradesh in 1920, how would you have responded to Gandhiji's call for Swaraj? Give reasons for your response.

Asked: a response, with reasons, as an Awadh (UP) peasant of 1920.

Concept used: The peasant movement in Awadh (Topic 3, section 2).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    My situation: talukdars and landlords demand very high rents and cesses; I must do begar on their farms; I can be evicted at any time.

  2. 2

    My response: I would join — through the Oudh Kisan Sabha, set up in October 1920 with Baba Ramchandra and Jawaharlal Nehru.

  3. 3

    My reasons: swaraj, to me, means reduction of revenue, an end to begar, and freedom from oppressive landlords; the Sabha's social boycott (nai–dhobi bandhs) gives me a peaceful way to fight.

  4. 4

    A thought to add: I would try to keep the struggle non-violent, as Gandhiji asked, though some peasants went further.

  5. Check: the reasons must be an Awadh peasant's reasons (rent, begar, eviction); "to get the right to leave the tea garden" belongs to Assam plantation workers and would not fit.

  6. Answer I would join the movement through the Oudh Kisan Sabha, because swaraj promised relief from high rents, begar and eviction, and I would use non-violent social boycott of oppressive landlords.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. As an Awadh peasant, I suffer very high rents and cesses, forced begar and eviction at will by talukdars and landlords. (1)
    2. So I would answer Gandhiji's call and join the Oudh Kisan Sabha of Baba Ramchandra and Jawaharlal Nehru. (1)
    3. Swaraj, for me, means lower revenue and no begar; I would socially boycott oppressive landlords, without violence. (1)
    Common trap: writing a general answer about "freedom from the British" only. The question asks for a peasant's reasons — name the peasant's own grievances.

Discuss p. 43Why groups joined the Civil Disobedience Movement5 marks

Why did various classes and groups of Indians participate in the Civil Disobedience Movement?

Asked: the reasons each group had for joining.

Concept used: How participants saw the movement (Topic 5, section 1).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Rich peasants (Patidars, Jats): hit by falling prices, could not pay revenue; wanted revenue reduced.

  2. 2

    Poor peasants: could not pay rent; wanted unpaid rent remitted.

  3. 3

    Business classes: wanted protection against foreign imports and a favourable rupee–sterling ratio; saw swaraj as an end to colonial restrictions on business.

  4. 4

    Industrial workers (mainly the Nagpur region): used boycott in their own fight against low wages and poor working conditions.

  5. 5

    Women: moved by Gandhiji's call, saw service to the nation as a sacred duty.

  6. Check: five groups, each with its own reason; if two of your points give the same reason ("to free India"), the answer has not shown why various groups joined.

  7. Answer Each group joined for its own reason: lower revenue (rich peasants), rent relief (poor peasants), freedom for business (industrialists), better wages and conditions (some workers), and service to the nation (women).

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Rich peasants such as the Patidars and Jats, hit by falling prices, could not pay revenue; for them swaraj meant a fight against high revenue. (1)
    2. Poor peasants, mostly small tenants, could not pay rent as cash incomes dwindled, and wanted their unpaid rent remitted. (1)
    3. Merchants and industrialists wanted protection against imports and a rupee–sterling ratio discouraging imports; swaraj meant no colonial restrictions on business. (1)
    4. Some industrial workers, mainly in the Nagpur region, adopted the boycott of foreign goods in their own struggle against low wages and poor conditions. (1)
    5. Women, moved by Gandhiji's call, marched, made salt and picketed shops, seeing service to the nation as a sacred duty. (1)
    Common trap: describing what the groups did without saying why. The question asks for their reasons.
Discuss p. 45Iqbal's idea of communalism3 marks

Read the Source D carefully. Do you agree with Iqbal's idea of communalism? Can you define communalism in a different way?

Asked: Iqbal's idea; your reasoned view; another definition.

Concept used: Source D and the Muslim question (Topic 5, section 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Iqbal's idea: each community should be free to develop on its own lines; this is "communalism in its higher aspect", not ill-will towards others; separate electorates are a safeguard for a minority. He says he respects other communities.

  2. 2

    The other view (held, for example, by those who saw "nation" as one people): emphasising communities and separate electorates can divide people and weaken a common national identity — Gandhiji argued in the same spirit that separate electorates for dalits would slow their integration.

  3. 3

    Your view: you may agree or disagree, but give a reason from the source or the chapter.

  4. 4

    A different definition: communalism can also be understood as putting the interest of one's own religious community above that of the nation and against other communities, leading to distrust and conflict — the kind of communal clashes the chapter describes from the mid-1920s.

  5. Check: a good answer states Iqbal's view fairly before judging it, and supports its own opinion with a reason; an answer that only says "I agree" or "I disagree" has no reason and would not earn the marks.

  6. Answer Iqbal saw communalism as a community's right to develop freely and defended separate electorates as a safeguard. Another view sees communalism as placing one religious community's interest against others, which divides the nation. Your answer should give your view with a reason.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Iqbal (1930) said each community should develop freely on its own lines without ill-will, and separate electorates safeguarded Muslims as a minority. (1)
    2. Others held that separate electorates divide people; communalism can also mean placing one religious community's interests against others'. (1)
    3. One possible view: minorities' fears deserve safeguards, but these should not deepen distrust between communities. (1)

    You may agree or disagree with Iqbal; the mark is for a clear view with a reason from the source or the chapter.

    Common trap: turning the answer into an attack on a community. Explain ideas and give reasons, as the textbook does.
Activity p. 48Will these images appeal to all?3 marks

Look at Figs. 12 and 14. Do you think these images will appeal to all castes and communities? Explain your views briefly.

(The book's "Fig. 14" is the Bharat Mata print labelled Fig. 14a.)

Asked: whether the two images of Bharat Mata could appeal to everyone, with reasons.

Concept used: Bharat Mata and the limits of symbols (Topic 6, sections 1 and 5).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    What they show: Fig. 12 — a calm, ascetic, spiritual mother giving learning, food and clothing; Fig. 14a — a mother with a trishul beside a lion and an elephant, symbols of power.

  2. 2

    Their appeal: for many people, devotion to the mother figure became a way to show nationalism; the images gave the nation a face.

  3. 3

    Their limit: both draw on Hindu iconography (a goddess-like figure, the mala, the trishul). The textbook says that when images were drawn from Hindu iconography, people of other communities felt left out.

  4. 4

    View: so they would not appeal equally to all communities.

  5. Check: the answer must point to a detail in the images (mala, trishul) and link it to the textbook's statement about Hindu iconography; a view with no reason would not earn the marks.

  6. Answer Not to all: they gave many Indians a powerful image of the nation, but because they drew on Hindu iconography, people of other communities could feel left out.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Fig. 12 shows a calm, ascetic mother giving learning, food and clothing; Fig. 14a shows her with a trishul, lion and elephant. (1)
    2. For many Indians, devotion to this mother figure became an expression of nationalism. (1)
    3. But both draw on Hindu iconography, so other communities could feel left out; they would not appeal to all. (1)
    Common trap: describing the pictures without answering the question "will they appeal to all?".

Write in brief Q1 (a)Nationalism and the anti-colonial movement3 marks

Explain: a) Why growth of nationalism in the colonies is linked to an anti-colonial movement.

Asked: why nationalism in colonies grew through the anti-colonial struggle.

Concept used: Topic 1, section 1.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    A common enemy: people in a colony shared the experience of being ruled and oppressed by a foreign power.

  2. 2

    Unity discovered in struggle: while fighting colonialism, they discovered their unity — the sense of oppression became a shared bond tying many groups together.

  3. 3

    Different groups, one movement: each class felt colonialism differently, but in India the Congress under Gandhiji brought them together in one movement against colonial rule.

  4. Check: the answer must say why the two are linked (shared oppression → unity in struggle); merely saying "colonies had nationalism" restates the question.

  5. Answer Because people in colonies discovered their unity in the struggle against colonial rule: the shared sense of oppression bound different groups together into one national movement.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. In colonies like India, people discovered their unity in the process of their struggle against colonialism. (1)
    2. The shared sense of being oppressed under colonial rule tied many different groups together. (1)
    3. Though each group felt colonialism differently, the Congress under Gandhiji tried to forge them into one movement. (1)
    Common trap: writing about nation-states in Europe. The question is about colonies.
Write in brief Q1 (b)The First World War and the national movement3 marks

Explain: b) How the First World War helped in the growth of the National Movement in India.

Asked: how the war's effects fed the national movement.

Concept used: Topic 1, section 2.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Economic burden: defence spending rose; it was paid for by war loans and higher taxes — customs duties raised, income tax introduced; prices doubled between 1913 and 1918.

  2. 2

    Forced recruitment: villages were made to supply soldiers, causing widespread anger.

  3. 3

    Famine and epidemic: crops failed in 1918–19 and 1920–21; with influenza, 12–13 million died (census of 1921).

  4. 4

    The result: hardships did not end after the war, so anger was ready for a new movement — which Gandhiji gave through satyagraha.

  5. Check: each point must end in anger or hardship (the link to the movement); listing war events without that link does not answer "how it helped".

  6. Answer The war brought higher taxes and prices, forced recruitment, and famine and epidemic; the resulting hardship and anger, which did not end after the war, fed the national movement.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Defence spending, financed by war loans, higher customs duties and a new income tax, rose sharply, and prices doubled between 1913 and 1918. (1)
    2. Forced recruitment of soldiers from villages caused widespread anger. (1)
    3. Crop failures and an influenza epidemic killed 12–13 million; as hardships continued after the war, people were ready for a new movement. (1)
    Common trap: giving only one kind of effect (taxes). Show the economic and the political side.
Write in brief Q1 (c)Outrage at the Rowlatt Act3 marks

Explain: c) Why Indians were outraged by the Rowlatt Act.

Asked: the reasons for the outrage.

Concept used: Topic 2, section 1.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    How it was passed: hurriedly, through the Imperial Legislative Council, despite the united opposition of the Indian members.

  2. 2

    Repressive powers: it gave the government enormous powers to repress political activities.

  3. 3

    No trial: it allowed political prisoners to be detained without trial for two years.

  4. Check: all three reasons are about the Act itself — not about Jallianwala Bagh, which came after the protest against the Act began.

  5. Answer It was forced through against the Indian members' united opposition, gave the government huge repressive powers, and allowed detention without trial for two years.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. The Act was hurriedly passed through the Imperial Legislative Council despite the united opposition of all its Indian members. (1)
    2. It gave the government enormous powers to repress political activities, so protests and meetings could be crushed easily. (1)
    3. It allowed the detention of political prisoners without trial for two years, so Indians saw it as an unjust law denying basic rights. (1)
    Common trap: writing about the Jallianwala Bagh massacre here. It was a result of the protests, not a reason for the outrage at the Act.
Write in brief Q1 (d)Withdrawal of the Non-Cooperation Movement3 marks

Explain: d) Why Gandhiji decided to withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement.

Asked: Gandhiji's reasons for withdrawal in February 1922.

Concept used: Topic 3, section 6.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Chauri Chaura: in Gorakhpur, a peaceful demonstration in a bazaar turned into a violent clash with the police.

  2. 2

    Violence elsewhere: he felt the movement was turning violent in many places.

  3. 3

    Not ready yet: satyagrahis needed to be properly trained before they would be ready for mass struggles.

    1. At Chauri Chaura in Gorakhpur, a peaceful demonstration in a bazaar turned into a violent clash with the police. (1)
    2. Gandhiji felt the movement was turning violent in many places, against his principle of non-violence. (1)
    3. He believed satyagrahis needed proper training before mass struggles, so he withdrew it in February 1922. (1)

Write in brief Q2The idea of satyagraha3 marks

What is meant by the idea of satyagraha?

Asked: the meaning of satyagraha.

Concept used: Topic 1, section 3.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Truth: satyagraha emphasises the power of truth and the need to search for truth.

  2. 2

    No force needed: if the cause is true and the struggle is against injustice, physical force is not necessary to fight the oppressor.

  3. 3

    Non-violence and conscience: without vengeance or aggression, the satyagrahi wins by appealing to the oppressor's conscience; people are persuaded, not forced, to see the truth.

  4. 4

    Unity: Gandhiji believed this dharma of non-violence could unite all Indians.

  5. Check: the four points move from truth to method to result; leaving out non-violence would leave out the heart of the idea.

  6. Answer Satyagraha is a method of mass struggle based on truth and non-violence: if the cause is just, the satyagrahi wins without force by appealing to the oppressor's conscience.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Satyagraha emphasised the power of truth and the need to search for truth. (1)
    2. If the cause was true and the struggle against injustice, physical force was not needed; a satyagrahi could win through non-violence, without vengeance or aggression. (1)
    3. It worked by appealing to the oppressor's conscience, persuading rather than forcing; Gandhiji believed this dharma of non-violence could unite all Indians. (1)
    Common trap: writing "satyagraha means passive resistance". Gandhiji called it active soul-force.
Write in brief Q3 (a)Newspaper report: Jallianwala Bagh5 marks

Write a newspaper report on: a) The Jallianwala Bagh massacre

Asked: a newspaper report — headline, place and date, and the facts in order.

Concept used: Topic 2, sections 2–3.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    A report answers who, what, where, when, why, and what next, in the past tense, with a headline and a dateline.

  2. 2

    Background: Rowlatt Act; hartal on 6 April; arrests in Amritsar; firing on 10 April; martial law; General Dyer.

  3. 3

    The event (13 April): crowd in the enclosed ground — protesters and Baisakhi visitors, many unaware of martial law; exits blocked; firing; hundreds killed.

  4. 4

    Dyer's stated aim: to "produce a moral effect" — terror and awe.

  5. 5

    What followed: protests in north Indian towns; brutal repression (crawling orders, flogging, bombing near Gujranwala).

  6. Check: the dates in the report must run in order (6 → 10 → 13 April) and the place must be Amritsar; a report dated 1930 or placed in Delhi would be wrong.

  7. Answer A sample report is given below.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    HUNDREDS KILLED IN AMRITSAR FIRING

    — Amritsar, April 1919

    1. On 13 April a large crowd gathered in the enclosed ground of Jallianwala Bagh, some to protest against repressive measures, others for the Baisakhi fair. (1)
    2. Many villagers did not know that martial law had been imposed after police fired on a procession on 10 April. (1)
    3. General Dyer blocked the exit points and opened fire on the crowd, killing hundreds. (1)
    4. He later declared that his aim was to "produce a moral effect" — terror and awe among satyagrahis. (1)
    5. Protests spread to north Indian towns; the government replied with brutal repression — crawling orders, flogging and the bombing of villages. (1)
    Common trap: writing an essay with no headline or date. A report needs a headline, a dateline and facts in order.
Write in brief Q3 (b)Newspaper report: the Simon Commission5 marks

Write a newspaper report on: b) The Simon Commission

Asked: a newspaper report on the Simon Commission's arrival and the protests.

Concept used: Topic 4, section 2.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Who set it up and why: the new Tory government in Britain — a Statutory Commission under Sir John Simon, set up in response to the nationalist movement, to look into the working of the constitutional system and suggest changes.

  2. 2

    The objection: not a single Indian member.

  3. 3

    The arrival (1928): "Go back Simon"; all parties including the Congress and the Muslim League joined.

  4. 4

    A tragedy: Lala Lajpat Rai was assaulted by the police during a peaceful demonstration and died of his injuries.

  5. 5

    What next: to win them over, Lord Irwin in October 1929 offered a vague dominion status and a Round Table Conference — which did not satisfy the Congress.

  6. Check: the year of arrival is 1928 and the reason for protest is no Indian member; a report saying the commission "included Indian leaders" contradicts the main fact.

  7. Answer A sample report is given below.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    "GO BACK SIMON!"

    — India, 1928

    1. The Statutory Commission under Sir John Simon, set up by Britain's new Tory government, has arrived to review India's constitutional system. (1)
    2. Indians are angry because it does not have a single Indian member; all its members are British. (1)
    3. It has been greeted everywhere with "Go back Simon"; all parties, including the Congress and the Muslim League, have joined the demonstrations. (1)
    4. Lala Lajpat Rai, assaulted by the British police during a peaceful demonstration against the commission, has died of his injuries. (1)
    5. Set up in response to the nationalist movement, the commission has instead united all parties against it. (1)
    Common trap: saying the commission was boycotted "because it wanted independence for India". It was boycotted because Indians had no place on it.
Write in brief Q4Bharat Mata and Germania3 marks

Compare the images of Bharat Mata in this chapter with the image of Germania in Chapter 1.

Asked: likenesses and differences between Bharat Mata and Germania.

Concept used: Topic 6, section 1, and the quick reminder on Germania.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Alike: both are female figures — the nation shown as a woman, giving an abstract idea a form people can identify with.

  2. 2

    Germania: the allegory of the German nation; she wears a crown of oak leaves, because the oak stands for heroism. Philip Veit's Germania (History Chapter 1, NCERT Fig. 17, read with Box 3 on p. 24) adds more symbols: her sword shows she is ready to fight; the olive branch wound round it shows she is also willing to make peace; the breastplate with an eagle stands for the strength of the German empire; and the broken chains show a people being freed.

  3. 3

    Bharat Mata (Fig. 12): an ascetic mother — calm, composed, divine, spiritual — giving learning, food and clothing.

  4. 4

    Bharat Mata (Fig. 14a): with a trishul, a lion and an elephant — symbols of power and authority, closer in spirit to Germania's heroism.

  5. 5

    A difference in use: devotion to Bharat Mata became a test of nationalism, and her Hindu iconography left some communities out.

  6. Check: the answer has at least one likeness and one difference; a comparison that only describes one figure is not a comparison.

  7. Answer Both personify the nation as a woman; Germania stands for heroism (oak crown), while Bharat Mata appears either as a calm, spiritual giver (Fig. 12) or as a powerful figure (Fig. 14a), drawn from Hindu iconography.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Bharat Mata and Germania are both female allegories that give the nation a visible form. (1)
    2. Germania wears a crown of oak leaves, as oak stands for heroism; Fig. 14a's Bharat Mata also shows power — trishul, lion, elephant. (1)
    3. Abanindranath Tagore's Bharat Mata (Fig. 12) differs: a calm, ascetic mother dispensing learning, food and clothing. (1)
    Common trap: writing that Germania is "a goddess like Bharat Mata". Germania is an allegory of the German nation; your textbook links Bharat Mata to Hindu iconography.

Discuss Q1Groups in the Non-Cooperation Movement5 marks

List all the different social groups which joined the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1921. Then choose any three and write about their hopes and struggles to show why they joined the movement.

Asked: (i) a list of all the groups; (ii) three groups' hopes and struggles.

Concept used: Topic 3 (all sections) and the Khilafat link (Topic 2).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    The list: the urban middle class (students, teachers, headmasters, lawyers); merchants and traders; Muslims of the Khilafat movement; peasants (Awadh); tribal peasants (Gudem Hills); plantation workers (Assam).

  2. 2

    Choose three, each with hope + struggle: Awadh peasants, Gudem tribals and Assam plantation workers have the clearest "own meaning" of swaraj.

  3. Check: the list part must be complete (all six groups) before the three are discussed; answering only three loses the "list all" marks.

  4. Answer Groups: middle class in towns, merchants and traders, Muslims (Khilafat), Awadh peasants, Gudem tribals, Assam plantation workers. Three discussed below.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Groups: middle-class students, teachers and lawyers; merchants and traders; Muslims of the Khilafat movement; Awadh peasants; Gudem Hills tribals; Assam plantation workers. (1)
    2. Awadh peasants, led by Baba Ramchandra, struggled against talukdars' high rents and begar; they hoped for lower revenue, no begar and boycott of oppressive landlords. (1)
    3. Gudem Hills tribals resented the closing of forests to grazing and fuelwood and forced begar for roads; they hoped to regain traditional rights. (1)
    4. Led by Alluri Sitaram Raju, they fought a guerrilla war, believing only force could win swaraj. (1)
    5. Assam plantation workers, confined by the Inland Emigration Act of 1859, hoped to move freely and go home; thousands left, believing Gandhi Raj was coming. (1)
    Common trap: describing the groups' actions without their hopes — the question asks why they joined.
Discuss Q2The Salt March as a symbol of resistance5 marks

Discuss the Salt March to make clear why it was an effective symbol of resistance against colonialism.

Asked: why the Salt March worked as a symbol of resistance.

Concept used: Topic 4, sections 4–5.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    A symbol everyone shared: salt was used by rich and poor alike and was an essential food.

  2. 2

    A clear injustice: the salt tax and the government monopoly showed the most oppressive face of British rule.

  3. 3

    A wide appeal: the eleven demands included something for every class; abolishing the salt tax was the most stirring.

  4. 4

    A visible march: 78 volunteers, over 240 miles, 24 days; thousands came to hear Gandhiji explain swaraj.

  5. 5

    A simple act of defiance anyone could copy: on 6 April 1930 he made salt at Dandi — thousands broke the salt law; the Civil Disobedience Movement began.

  6. Check: each point explains why it worked (shared, unjust, visible, copyable); a point like "the march was long" without saying why that mattered does not answer "effective".

  7. Answer Salt united rich and poor, its tax showed British oppression clearly, the long march drew crowds, and making salt was an easy act of defiance that millions could copy — so it began the Civil Disobedience Movement.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Salt was consumed by rich and poor alike and was one of the most essential items of food, so it could unite the nation. (1)
    2. The salt tax and the government monopoly over production revealed, Gandhiji said, the most oppressive face of British rule. (1)
    3. Of the eleven demands sent to Irwin, abolishing the salt tax was the most stirring; when Irwin would not negotiate, the march began. (1)
    4. Walking over 240 miles in 24 days with 78 volunteers, Gandhiji drew thousands at every stop and urged them to defy the British peacefully. (1)
    5. On 6 April 1930 he made salt at Dandi; thousands across India then broke the salt law, starting the Civil Disobedience Movement. (1)
    Common trap: narrating the march without saying why salt was a good symbol — that is what "effective" asks.
Discuss Q3A woman in the Civil Disobedience Movement5 marks

Imagine you are a woman participating in the Civil Disobedience Movement. Explain what the experience meant to your life.

Asked: a first-person account of what joining meant.

Concept used: Women in the Civil Disobedience Movement (Topic 5, section 1).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Who you could be: a woman from a high-caste family in a town, or from a rich peasant household in a village.

  2. 2

    What you did: listened to Gandhiji during the salt march; joined protest marches; made salt; picketed foreign cloth and liquor shops; perhaps went to jail.

  3. 3

    What it meant: for the first time, moving out of the home into a public arena (NCERT Fig. 9); service to the nation as a sacred duty.

  4. 4

    Its limits: the Congress gave women little real authority; Gandhiji still saw home and hearth as women's duty — so the change in your position was limited.

  5. Check: the account must include both the new experience and its limits; leaving out the limits misses half of what the textbook says.

  6. Answer A sample first-person answer is given below.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. I come from a rich peasant household; when Gandhiji's salt march passed near our village, I went to hear him — my first time in a public crowd. (1)
    2. Moved by his call, I joined protest marches and helped to manufacture salt in defiance of the law. (1)
    3. With other women I picketed shops selling foreign cloth and liquor; some of my friends went to jail. (1)
    4. I began to see service to the nation as a sacred duty of women, and gained confidence outside my home. (1)
    5. Yet little changed in how women were seen: home and hearth were still our duty, and the Congress was reluctant to give women any position of authority. (1)

Discuss Q4Separate electorates3 marks

Why did political leaders differ sharply over the question of separate electorates?

Asked: the reasons for the sharp disagreement.

Concept used: Topic 5, sections 2–3.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    For separate electorates: dalit leaders like Ambedkar believed political empowerment would end their social disabilities; Muslim leaders like Iqbal saw them as a safeguard for a minority's political interests.

  2. 2

    Against: Gandhiji felt separate electorates for dalits would slow their integration into society, and fasted when they were conceded; many nationalists felt they divided the nation.

  3. 3

    The outcome: the Poona Pact (1932) gave dalits reserved seats elected by the general electorate; Congress–League talks on representation failed in 1928 — Jinnah would give up separate electorates only if Muslims got reserved seats in the Central Assembly and representation in proportion to population in Bengal and Punjab, and M. R. Jayakar opposed compromise.

  4. Check: a sharp difference needs two sides — an answer with only the arguments for, or only against, cannot explain a disagreement.

  5. Answer Some leaders saw separate electorates as a safeguard for minorities and dalits, others as a danger to national unity — so the issue divided them sharply.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Ambedkar sought separate electorates so that political empowerment would end dalits' social disabilities; Iqbal saw them as a safeguard for Muslims. (1)
    2. Gandhiji believed they would slow dalits' integration into society, and fasted unto death when they were conceded. (1)
    3. Jinnah would drop them only for assured reserved seats, but the Hindu Mahasabha's M. R. Jayakar blocked compromise in 1928. (1)
    Common trap: writing only about dalits. The question also covers Muslim representation.
