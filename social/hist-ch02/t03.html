Topic 3 of 7

Differing strands within the Non-Cooperation Movement

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the movement in the towns, its economic effects and why it slowed down.
  • Describe the peasant movement in Awadh, the tribal movement in the Gudem Hills and the plantation workers of Assam, and what swaraj meant to each.
  • Explain why Gandhiji withdrew the Non-Cooperation Movement in February 1922.

In 1921 one word was on everybody's lips: swaraj. A law student in a city heard it and walked out of college. A tenant farmer in Awadh heard it and refused to work for his landlord for free. A tea-garden worker in Assam heard it and started walking home. They all answered the same call — but each heard something different in it.

In January 1921 the Non-Cooperation–Khilafat Movement got under way. People from many walks of life joined, and each group had its own hopes. They all answered the call for swaraj — but each group heard its own meaning in that word.

  1. The movement in the towns

Who: it began with the middle class in the cities.

  • Students in their thousands walked out of schools and colleges run by the government; teachers and headmasters gave up their jobs; lawyers stopped going to court.
  • Most provinces boycotted the council elections. Madras was the exception: there the Justice Party, which spoke for non-Brahmans, chose to contest, because a seat in the council gave it a share of power that Brahmans had usually kept to themselves.

Economic effects — "more dramatic":

  • People refused foreign goods, stood in protest outside liquor shops, and piled foreign cloth into great bonfires (NCERT Fig. 4 shows the boycott of foreign cloth in July 1922 — foreign cloth stood for the West's economic and cultural domination).
  • Between 1921 and 1922 imports of foreign cloth fell to about half — in value, from Rs 102 crore to Rs 57 crore.
  • In many places merchants and traders would not deal in foreign goods or lend money for foreign trade.
  • As more people switched to Indian cloth, Indian mills and handloom weavers produced more.

New word. Picket — protesters standing at the door of a shop, factory or office so that people cannot go in.

Why it slowed down in the towns:

  1. Khadi often cost more than cloth made in mills, and the poor could not afford it — so they could not keep up the boycott of mill cloth for long.
  2. Alternative Indian institutions (schools, courts) were needed in place of the British ones, but they were slow to come up.
  3. So students and teachers trickled back to government schools and lawyers went back to government courts.

  1. Rebellion in the countryside — peasants of Awadh

  • Leader: Baba Ramchandra — a sanyasi who had once worked in Fiji as an indentured labourer.
  • Against: talukdars and landlords who charged huge rents plus many extra cesses. Peasants had to work on the landlords' fields for nothing (begar), and since tenants could be thrown off the land at any time, they could never gain any right to it.
  • Demands: lower revenue, an end to begar, and a social boycott of cruel landlords. Village panchayats called nai–dhobi bandhs, so that such landlords could not get the services of a barber or a washerman.
  • Congress link: from June 1920 Jawaharlal Nehru toured Awadh's villages to learn about the peasants' problems. In October the Oudh Kisan Sabha was formed, led by Nehru, Baba Ramchandra and others, and in a month it had over 300 branches.
  • What went beyond the Congress: in 1921 some peasants attacked the homes of talukdars and merchants, looted markets and seized stores of grain. Local leaders claimed that Gandhiji had told peasants not to pay taxes and that land would be shared out among the poor — Gandhiji's name was being used to justify anything.

Reading Source B (Nehru near Rae Bareli, 6 January 1921). After police fired at peasants, Nehru, angry, spoke to them. He admits that for a moment "non-violence was almost forgotten", but the calm of the kisans and the thought of Gandhiji made him speak to them about non-violence, and they dispersed peacefully. What it tells us: non-violence was hard to keep even for leaders, and the peasants' discipline impressed him.

Box — Bardoli, 1928. In Bardoli, a taluka in Gujarat, peasants led by Vallabhbhai Patel resisted a rise in land revenue. The Bardoli Satyagraha succeeded, and news of it won the peasants sympathy across India.

New word. Begar — unpaid work that villagers were made to do.

  1. Tribal peasants — the Gudem Hills

  • Where: the Gudem Hills of Andhra Pradesh, early 1920s — a militant guerrilla movement, not a form of struggle the Congress could approve.
  • Why they rose: the government had shut off large stretches of forest, so the hill people could no longer graze cattle there or gather fuelwood and fruit. This hurt their living and took away rights they had always had. When they were also forced into unpaid road-building work (begar), they rebelled.
  • Leader: Alluri Sitaram Raju, who claimed special powers (foretelling events, healing, surviving bullets); his followers believed he was an incarnation of God. Raju spoke highly of Gandhiji, said Non-Cooperation inspired him, and urged people to take up khadi and stop drinking — yet he insisted that only force, not non-violence, could free India.
  • Actions: attacks on police stations, attempts to kill British officials, guerrilla warfare for swaraj.
  • End: the British caught Raju and executed him in 1924; in time he became a hero of folk memory.

  1. Swaraj in the plantations — Assam

  • For tea-garden workers in Assam, freedom meant being free to come and go from the gardens where they were shut in, and to stay in touch with the villages they came from.
  • Under the Inland Emigration Act of 1859, they could not leave the tea gardens without permission, which was rarely given.
  • Hearing of Non-Cooperation, thousands defied the authorities and headed home, believing "Gandhi Raj" was coming and they would get land in their villages.
  • They never got home: a strike on the railways and steamers left them stuck on the way, and the police caught them and beat them badly.

  1. Many meanings, one movement

These groups interpreted swaraj in their own ways — often as a time when all suffering would be over. Yet when they chanted Gandhiji's name or raised the slogan "Swatantra Bharat", they were relating emotionally to an all-India agitation that went beyond their own locality.

  1. Withdrawal — February 1922

In a bazaar at Chauri Chaura (Gorakhpur), a demonstration that began peacefully ended in a violent clash with the police (NCERT Fig. 5). In February 1922 Gandhiji called off the Non-Cooperation Movement: in his view it was becoming violent in many places, and satyagrahis needed proper training before any new mass struggle.

Worked example 1

Why the towns grew quiet

Question. The Non-Cooperation Movement in the cities began with great energy but gradually slowed down. Explain three reasons. (3 marks)

Asked: three reasons for the slowdown in the cities.

Concept used: The movement in the towns (section 1). Why it applies: the textbook gives two problems and their result.

  1. 1

    Khadi cost more: it was often more expensive than mill cloth, so poor people could not afford it and could not keep up the boycott for long.

  2. 2

    No alternatives: for the boycott of British institutions to work, Indian schools and courts were needed in their place; these were slow to come up.

  3. 3

    People returned: so students and teachers went back to government schools and lawyers went back to government courts.

  4. Check: each reason explains why people stopped boycotting; an answer about "the Chauri Chaura violence" fails here — that was the reason for the withdrawal of the whole movement, not for the slowdown in the cities.

  5. Answer Khadi was too costly for the poor; Indian alternatives to British institutions were slow to come up; so students, teachers and lawyers drifted back.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Khadi was often costlier than mill cloth, so the poor could not afford it or boycott mill cloth for long. (1)
    2. Boycotting British institutions needed Indian alternatives, such as schools and courts, but these were slow to come up. (1)
    3. So students and teachers trickled back to government schools, and lawyers returned to government courts. (1)
    Common trap: giving the effects of the boycott (imports halved, Indian mills produced more) when the question asks why it slowed down.

Worked example 2

Awadh and the Gudem Hills compared

Question. Compare the peasant movement in Awadh with the tribal movement in the Gudem Hills: their leaders, grievances and methods. Plan a 5-mark answer.

Asked: leaders, grievances and methods of the two movements, compared.

Concept used: Rebellion in the countryside (sections 2–3).

  1. 1

    Leaders: Awadh — Baba Ramchandra, a sanyasi; later linked to the Congress through Nehru and the Oudh Kisan Sabha. Gudem — Alluri Sitaram Raju, seen by the rebels as having special powers.

  2. 2

    Grievances: Awadh — high rents and cesses, begar, no security of tenure under talukdars and landlords. Gudem — forests closed to grazing and fuelwood, and begar for road building.

  3. 3

    Methods (Awadh): social boycott of landlords, nai–dhobi bandhs; later attacks on talukdars' houses and bazaars.

  4. 4

    Methods (Gudem): guerrilla warfare — attacks on police stations and officials; Raju believed India could be freed only by force.

  5. 5

    Link to the national movement: both used Gandhiji's name and saw themselves as part of an all-India struggle, but both went beyond what the Congress approved.

  6. Check: every point has an Awadh half and a Gudem half (or one sentence about both). A 5-mark answer that only describes Awadh would get at most half the marks.

  7. Answer Different leaders and grievances (rent and begar vs forest rights and begar), different methods (social boycott vs armed guerrilla struggle), but both identified with Gandhiji's swaraj.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Leaders: Baba Ramchandra, a sanyasi, led the Awadh peasants and headed the Oudh Kisan Sabha with Nehru; Alluri Sitaram Raju led the Gudem rebels. (1)
    2. Awadh grievances: talukdars and landlords demanded very high rents and cesses, forced begar, and evicted tenants at will. (1)
    3. Gudem grievances: forests were closed to grazing and fuelwood collection, and hill people were forced to do begar for road building. (1)
    4. Methods: Awadh peasants used social boycott and nai–dhobi bandhs, later attacking talukdars' houses; the Gudem rebels waged guerrilla war, believing only force could free India. (1)
    5. Both invoked Gandhiji's name and saw themselves as part of the struggle for swaraj, but acted in ways the Congress did not approve. (1)
    Common trap: calling Raju a Congress leader or a follower of non-violence. He admired Gandhiji but rejected non-violence.

Worked example 3

Cause or effect?

Question. Tick the correct order: (A) "Gandhiji withdrew the Non-Cooperation Movement → violence broke out at Chauri Chaura" or (B) "Violence at Chauri Chaura → Gandhiji withdrew the Non-Cooperation Movement". Explain in one line.

Asked: which is cause and which is effect.

Concept used: Withdrawal in February 1922 (section 6).

  1. 1

    The clash at Chauri Chaura came first: a demonstration that began peacefully ended in violence against the police.

  2. 2

    Hearing of it, and feeling the movement was turning violent in many places, Gandhiji called a halt in February 1922.

  3. Check: NCERT's Fig. 5 caption says "Hearing of the incident, Mahatma Gandhi called a halt" — the hearing comes before the halt, so (A) cannot be right.

  4. Answer (B) — the violence at Chauri Chaura was a cause; the withdrawal was the effect.

    Common trap: reading "Chauri Chaura; Gandhiji withdraws" in the dates box as two unrelated events.

Common mistakes!

  • "Swaraj meant the same to every group."

    Each group read it its own way: rents and begar (Awadh), forest rights (Gudem), freedom to go home (Assam).

    One group per point, each with its own meaning.

  • Calling the Gudem rebels non-violent or Congress-approved.

    Raju said India could be freed only by force; the Congress could not approve guerrilla war.

    "Inspired by Gandhiji, but used force."

  • "Chauri Chaura happened because Gandhiji withdrew the movement."

    The clash came first; the withdrawal followed.

    Violence → withdrawal (cause → effect).

  • Writing the boycott's effects when asked why it slowed down.

    Imports halving is an effect, not a reason for slowing.

    Slowed down: khadi costly; alternatives slow; people returned.

  • One long sentence (or a list of names) for a 3- or 5-mark answer.

    Examiners give a mark for each separate, explained point.

    3 marks = 3 numbered points; 5 marks = 5 points, each with a reason or example.

Key points

  • January 1921: Non-Cooperation–Khilafat Movement begins; swaraj meant different things to different groups.
  • Towns: students, teachers and lawyers left British institutions; elections boycotted (except Madras — Justice Party). Foreign cloth imports halved (Rs 102 crore → Rs 57 crore); Indian mills and handlooms produced more. Slowed because khadi was costly and Indian alternatives were slow to come up.
  • Awadh: Baba Ramchandra; against talukdars, high rents, begar; Oudh Kisan Sabha (Nehru, October 1920, 300+ branches); later violence the Congress disliked.
  • Gudem Hills (Andhra Pradesh): forests closed, begar; Alluri Sitaram Raju; guerrilla war; executed 1924.
  • Assam: Inland Emigration Act 1859; workers left for home, believing "Gandhi Raj" was coming; caught and beaten.
  • February 1922: after Chauri Chaura (Gorakhpur), Gandhiji withdrew the movement — it was turning violent and satyagrahis needed training.
