Topic 2 of 7

The Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala Bagh and the call for Non-Cooperation

About 35 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why Indians were outraged by the Rowlatt Act and describe the Rowlatt satyagraha and its limits.
  • Describe the Jallianwala Bagh massacre (13 April 1919) and its aftermath.
  • Explain the Khilafat issue and why Gandhiji took it up.
  • Explain why Gandhiji proposed non-cooperation, its stages, and its acceptance at Calcutta (September 1920) and Nagpur (December 1920).

Suppose a law said that the police could pick you up for a political speech and keep you in jail for two years without any trial — no judge, no lawyer, no chance to prove you were innocent. In 1919 such a law was passed in India, even though all the Indian members of the council opposed it. What would you do? Gandhiji's answer led, within a week, to the darkest day of the national movement.

  1. The Rowlatt Act (1919) and why Indians were outraged

These first successes gave Gandhiji confidence. In 1919 he called for a satyagraha across the whole country against a new law, the Rowlatt Act.

Why the Act caused outrage:

  1. How it was made: it was rushed through the Imperial Legislative Council, even though all the Indian members opposed it.
  2. What power it gave: the government could crush political activity almost as it pleased.
  3. What it allowed: political prisoners could be kept in jail without any trial for up to two years.

Gandhiji's answer was civil disobedience without violence — people would peacefully refuse to obey such an unjust law. It was to begin with a hartal (a day when shops and work stop) on 6 April.

  1. The Rowlatt satyagraha and the crackdown

  • The response: rallies in many cities; workers in railway workshops went on strike; shops closed down.
  • The fear: the size of the response worried the British, who feared that railway and telegraph lines — their communications — might be cut. So they struck at the leaders: local leaders in Amritsar were arrested, and Gandhiji was not allowed to enter Delhi.
  • 10 April: in Amritsar, police opened fire on a procession that was peaceful. Angry crowds then attacked banks, post offices and railway stations. The city was put under martial law (rule by the army), with General Dyer in charge.

  1. Jallianwala Bagh — 13 April 1919

On 13 April, a large crowd had gathered inside Jallianwala Bagh, a ground in Amritsar closed in on all sides.

  • Who was there: some came to protest against the new repressive measures; others had come for the annual Baisakhi fair. Many villagers from outside the city did not know that martial law had been imposed.
  • What Dyer did: he marched in with his soldiers, closed the ways out and ordered them to fire on the crowd. Hundreds were killed.
  • Why, in his own words: to "produce a moral effect" — to create terror and awe in the minds of satyagrahis.

The aftermath:

  • When people heard what had happened, protests broke out in many towns of north India — strikes, fights with the police, attacks on government buildings.
  • The government hit back with brutal repression, aimed at humiliating people and filling them with fear. Satyagrahis had to crawl along streets, touch their noses to the ground and salute every sahib (NCERT Fig. 3 shows Dyer's "crawling orders" being carried out in Amritsar); people were whipped (flogged); villages near Gujranwala in Punjab (now in Pakistan) were bombed.
  • Seeing violence spread, Gandhiji called off the movement.

The limit of the Rowlatt satyagraha: it had spread far, but it had stayed mainly in cities and towns. Gandhiji now wanted a movement with a much wider base — and he believed no such movement was possible unless Hindus and Muslims came closer together.

  1. The Khilafat issue

  • Ottoman Turkey was on the losing side in the First World War. Word spread that the winners would force a harsh peace treaty on its emperor — the Khalifa, regarded as the spiritual head of the Islamic world.
  • In March 1919, a Khilafat Committee was set up in Bombay to protect the Khalifa's worldly (temporal) powers.
  • Younger Muslim leaders — among them the brothers Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali — talked with Gandhiji about joint mass action.
  • Why Gandhiji took it up: it was a chance to bring Muslims into one united national movement, alongside Hindus.

  1. Why non-cooperation? — the idea and its stages

Gandhiji had set out the idea years earlier, in his book Hind Swaraj (1909). His argument ran like this:

  • the British could rule India only because Indians worked with them — in offices, courts, the army, schools and trade;
  • so if Indians stopped cooperating, British rule would fall within a year, and swaraj (self-rule) would follow.

The movement was to unfold in stages:

  1. Give back the titles the government had given to Indians.
  2. Boycott British-run services and institutions — the civil services, army, police, courts, legislative councils and schools — and foreign goods.
  3. If the government answered with repression, move on to full civil disobedience.

Through the summer of 1920, Gandhiji and Shaukat Ali toured widely to win support.

New word. Boycott — to protest by refusing to have anything to do with someone or something: not buying, using, joining or dealing with it.

  1. Two Congress sessions of 1920 — "Calcutta convinced, Nagpur adopted"

Session When What happened
Calcutta September 1920 Gandhiji won over the other leaders: a non-cooperation movement would be started for Khilafat and for swaraj together
(in between) September–December 1920 An intense tussle in the Congress: many did not want to boycott the council elections of November 1920, and feared popular violence
Nagpur December 1920 The two sides reached a compromise, and the Congress adopted the Non-Cooperation programme

Worked example 1

Outrage at the Rowlatt Act

Question. Why did even moderate Indians, who had never broken a law, feel outraged by the Rowlatt Act? Give two reasons. (2 marks)

Asked: two reasons for the outrage.

Concept used: The Rowlatt Act (this topic, section 1). Why it applies: both reasons are about how the Act was passed and what it allowed.

  1. 1

    How it was passed: it was rushed through the Imperial Legislative Council although all the Indian members opposed it — so Indian opinion was ignored.

  2. 2

    What it allowed: sweeping powers to crush political activity, and jail without trial for up to two years — so anyone could lose their freedom without a hearing.

  3. Check: each reason is about the Act itself. "Because of Jallianwala Bagh" fails the check — the massacre came after the protest against the Act began, so it cannot be a reason for the outrage at the Act.

  4. Answer It was forced through despite all the Indian members' opposition, and it allowed political prisoners to be jailed for two years without trial.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The Act was hurriedly passed through the Imperial Legislative Council despite the united opposition of its Indian members. (1)
    2. It gave the government huge powers to repress political activity and allowed detention of political prisoners without trial for two years. (1)
    Common trap: writing "it raised taxes". The Rowlatt Act was about repression and detention, not taxes.

Worked example 2

Why was the Rowlatt satyagraha called off?

Question. Explain how the Rowlatt satyagraha ended and what lesson Gandhiji drew from it. (3 marks)

Asked: how it ended; the lesson.

Concept used: The April 1919 chain and its limits (sections 2–3).

  1. 1

    Repression: after the hartal, leaders were arrested in Amritsar, the police fired on a procession on 10 April, martial law followed, and on 13 April General Dyer fired on the crowd at Jallianwala Bagh, killing hundreds.

  2. 2

    Violence spread: strikes, clashes and attacks on government buildings followed in north Indian towns; the government replied brutally. Seeing violence spread, Gandhiji called off the movement.

  3. 3

    The lesson: the movement had been limited mostly to cities and towns, so Gandhiji wanted a more broad-based movement, which needed Hindus and Muslims to come closer — this led him to the Khilafat issue.

  4. Check: the order of the three points follows the dates (April 1919 → withdrawal → plan for a wider movement); swapping them would put the lesson before the event.

  5. Answer Repression and the Jallianwala Bagh massacre led to spreading violence, so Gandhiji withdrew the movement; since it had stayed mostly urban, he looked for a broader movement uniting Hindus and Muslims.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Leaders were arrested in Amritsar, police fired on a procession, martial law followed, and Dyer killed hundreds at Jallianwala Bagh on 13 April 1919. (1)
    2. Strikes and clashes spread in north Indian towns and met brutal repression; seeing violence spread, Gandhiji called off the movement. (1)
    3. As it was mostly urban, he wanted a broad-based movement uniting Hindus and Muslims. (1)
    Common trap: saying the movement ended because it "succeeded". It was withdrawn because of violence.

Worked example 3

Put 1919–1920 in order

Question. Arrange in order, earliest first: I. Non-Cooperation programme adopted at Nagpur. II. Khilafat Committee formed in Bombay. III. Jallianwala Bagh massacre. IV. Gandhiji convinces leaders at the Calcutta session.

Asked: the chronological order.

Concept used: The mini-timeline (this topic).

  1. 1

    Date each event: II — March 1919; III — 13 April 1919; IV — September 1920; I — December 1920.

  2. 2

    Sort: March 1919 → April 1919 → September 1920 → December 1920.

  3. Check: the session that adopted the programme must come after the session that convinced the leaders — I after IV ✔.

  4. Answer II, III, IV, I

    Common trap: putting Nagpur before Calcutta because "N" sounds earlier or because both are "1920". Calcutta was in September, Nagpur in December.

Common mistakes!

  • "Calcutta adopted the programme; Nagpur convinced the leaders."

    Calcutta (September 1920) came first and convinced; Nagpur (December 1920) adopted.

    Calcutta convinced, Nagpur adopted.

  • Mixing 6, 10 and 13 April 1919.

    6 April hartal → 10 April Amritsar firing → 13 April Jallianwala Bagh.

    Learn the three dates as one chain.

  • Placing Jallianwala Bagh in Delhi or Lahore.

    It is in Amritsar, Punjab; Gandhiji was barred from entering Delhi.

    Amritsar — Punjab.

  • "The Rowlatt Act was opposed because it raised taxes."

    It allowed detention without trial and repression.

    Write without trial, two years.

  • "The Khilafat movement was against the Khalifa."

    It was to defend the Khalifa's temporal powers.

    Khilafat = defend the Khalifa.

Key points

  • Rowlatt Act (1919): passed despite the Indian members' united opposition; repression of political activity; detention without trial for two years. Hartal on 6 April 1919.
  • 10 April: firing on a procession in Amritsar; martial law; General Dyer. 13 April 1919: Jallianwala Bagh — exits blocked, hundreds killed, to "produce a moral effect". Brutal repression followed; Gandhiji called off the movement.
  • The Rowlatt satyagraha was mostly urban, so Gandhiji wanted a broad-based movement uniting Hindus and Muslims → the Khilafat issue (Committee: Bombay, March 1919; Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali).
  • Hind Swaraj (1909): British rule survives on Indian cooperation; refuse it and swaraj will come. Stages: surrender titles → boycott → full civil disobedience.
  • Calcutta, September 1920: leaders convinced. Nagpur, December 1920: programme adopted.
