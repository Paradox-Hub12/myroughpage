Topic 6 of 7

The sense of collective belonging

About 30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain how images (Bharat Mata), songs, folklore, flags and the reinterpretation of history created a sense of nationalism.
  • Explain the limits of these efforts (Source E).
  • Describe the Quit India Movement (1942) from the textbook box.

Why does your heart beat faster when the national anthem plays before a match, or when the flag goes up on 15 August? You have never met most of the people in your country, yet you feel you belong with them. A hundred years ago, people in India were learning to feel exactly this — through pictures, songs, stories, flags and history.

Quick reminder (History Chapter 1)

In nineteenth-century Europe, artists showed nations as female figures (allegories) — for example Germania, the allegory of the German nation, who wears a crown of oak leaves because the oak stands for heroism. A figure like this gives an abstract idea, the nation, a form people can see and identify with.

Nationalism grows when people come to feel that they belong to one nation — when they find something that ties them together. In India this sense of collective belonging came partly from united struggles (Topics 1–5) and partly from cultural processes: history and fiction, folklore and songs, popular prints and symbols.

  1. An image of the nation — Bharat Mata

  • In the twentieth century, people began to picture India as Bharat Mata — Mother India.
  • Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay was the first to create this image. In the 1870s he composed "Vande Mataram", a hymn to the motherland; he later put it into his novel Anandamath, and it was sung everywhere during the Swadeshi movement in Bengal.
  • Inspired by the Swadeshi movement, Abanindranath Tagore made his well-known painting of Bharat Mata (NCERT Fig. 12, 1905): an ascetic figure — calm, composed, divine and spiritual; she dispenses learning, food and clothing, and the mala in one hand shows her ascetic quality. Abanindranath, like Ravi Varma before him, tried to develop a truly Indian style of painting.
  • Later the image took many forms in popular prints and paintings. NCERT Fig. 14a shows a contrast: Bharat Mata with a trishul, beside a lion and an elephant — symbols of power and authority.
  • NCERT Fig. 13 (a popular print) shows Jawaharlal Nehru holding a picture of Bharat Mata and a map of India against his chest; many prints showed leaders offering their heads to Bharat Mata — the idea of sacrifice for the mother was powerful.
  • Being devoted to this mother became a sign that a person was a true nationalist.

  1. Folklore

  • From the late nineteenth century, nationalists wrote down the folk tales that bards sang and travelled from village to village collecting folk songs and legends. To them, these stories showed traditional culture as it really was, before outside forces damaged it — and saving them would help people find their national identity and feel proud of their past.
  • In Bengal, Rabindranath Tagore gathered ballads, nursery rhymes and myths, and led the revival of folk culture.
  • In Madras, Natesa Sastri brought out a huge collection of Tamil folk tales in four volumes, The Folklore of Southern India; he believed folklore was national literature — "the most trustworthy manifestation of people's real thoughts and characteristics".

  1. Icons and symbols — flags

Swaraj flag (designed by Gandhiji) by 1921 red, green and white tricolour a spinning wheel in the centre — the Gandhian ideal of self-help

Marchers who carried the flag and held it high were openly defying the British. NCERT Fig. 11 (an early-twentieth-century print of Bal Gangadhar Tilak) shows another use of symbols: Tilak is framed by the sacred places of different faiths — temple, church, masjid — as symbols of unity.

  1. Reinterpretation of history

  • British writers described Indians as backward and primitive — unable to rule themselves.
  • To answer this, by the late nineteenth century Indians started studying the past to show India's great achievements — in art and architecture, science and mathematics, religion and culture, law and philosophy, crafts and trade.
  • These histories told of a glorious past and a decline once India was colonised; they asked readers to be proud of what India had achieved and to fight to change the hard conditions of life under British rule.

  1. The limits — who felt left out?

When the past that was praised was a Hindu past, and the images came from Hindu religious art (iconography), people of other communities felt they had no place in them.

Reading Source E (Tarinicharan Chattopadhyay, Bharatbarsher Itihas, 1858). The author contrasts the courage and truthfulness of the "Arya vamsa" in earlier times with the present, when "a few soldiers from a tiny island far away" rule India. What it shows: pride in a glorious past was used to stir people against colonial rule. Its limit: it describes the glorious past as a Hindu one — exactly the kind of history that made other communities feel left out.

  1. The Quit India Movement, 1942 (textbook box)

  • Causes: discontent spread after the Cripps Mission failed and as World War II hit India. Gandhiji called for the complete withdrawal of the British from India.
  • 14 July 1942, Wardha: the Congress Working Committee passed the "Quit India" resolution, asking for power to be handed over to Indians at once.
  • 8 August 1942, Bombay: the All India Congress Committee approved the resolution and called for a non-violent mass struggle across the country; Gandhiji gave his "Do or Die" speech.
  • What happened: across much of India the government machinery almost stopped working; there were hartals, demonstrations, and processions singing national songs and shouting slogans. It was a true mass movement of students, workers and peasants, with leaders such as Jayprakash Narayan, Aruna Asaf Ali and Ram Manohar Lohia, and women such as Matangini Hazra (Bengal), Kanaklata Barua (Assam) and Rama Devi (Odisha).
  • The British used great force, but needed over a year to crush the movement.
  • NCERT Fig. 14b is a photograph of women marching in procession in Bombay in the Quit India Movement (this photograph appears in the CBSE sample paper 2026-27).

  1. Conclusion — "a nation with many voices"

Anger against colonial rule brought many groups into a common struggle, and the Congress under Gandhiji tried to channel their grievances into organised movements. But the groups had different aspirations, so freedom meant different things to them. The Congress kept trying to resolve differences so that one group's demands did not alienate another — which is why unity kept breaking down: times of strong unity were followed by times of division and quarrels between groups. What emerged was a nation with many voices wanting freedom from colonial rule.

Worked example 1

Two images of Bharat Mata

Question. Look at the descriptions of NCERT Fig. 12 (Abanindranath Tagore's Bharat Mata) and Fig. 14a (a later Bharat Mata). How do the two images present the mother figure differently? (3 marks)

Asked: the difference between the two images.

Concept used: Bharat Mata (section 1).

  1. 1

    Fig. 12: an ascetic figure — calm, composed, divine and spiritual; she gives learning, food and clothing; the mala stresses her ascetic quality.

  2. 2

    Fig. 14a: she holds a trishul and stands beside a lion and an elephant — symbols of power and authority.

  3. 3

    The meaning: one shows the nation as a spiritual, giving mother; the other as a powerful, protective figure — the image changed as it circulated in popular prints.

  4. Check: each point must name a detail you can point to in the figure (mala, food and clothing; trishul, lion, elephant). An answer that only says "both are goddesses" names no detail and would not earn the marks.

  5. Answer Fig. 12 shows a calm, spiritual mother who gives learning, food and clothing; Fig. 14a shows a powerful figure with a trishul, a lion and an elephant — spiritual giver vs symbol of power.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Abanindranath Tagore's Bharat Mata (1905) is an ascetic figure — calm, composed, divine and spiritual, holding a mala. (1)
    2. She dispenses learning, food and clothing — a nurturing mother, not a warrior. (1)
    3. Fig. 14a is a contrast: she holds a trishul and stands beside a lion and an elephant, symbols of power and authority. (1)
    Common trap: writing that Rabindranath Tagore painted Bharat Mata. The painter was Abanindranath Tagore; Rabindranath collected folk ballads.

Worked example 2

Two tricolours

Question. Compare the flag designed during the Swadeshi movement with the Swaraj flag of 1921. (2 marks)

Asked: the differences between the two flags.

Concept used: Flags (section 3).

  1. 1

    Swadeshi flag: red, green and yellow; eight lotuses standing for British India's eight provinces, and a crescent moon standing for Hindus and Muslims.

  2. 2

    Swaraj flag (Gandhiji, by 1921): red, green and white; a spinning wheel in the centre for the Gandhian ideal of self-help.

  3. Check: the colours differ in only one colour (yellow → white) — if your answer gives both flags the same three colours, look again.

  4. Answer Swadeshi flag: red–green–yellow, 8 lotuses and a crescent moon. Swaraj flag: red–green–white, a spinning wheel for self-help.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The Swadeshi movement flag in Bengal was red, green and yellow, with eight lotuses for eight provinces and a crescent moon for Hindus and Muslims. (1)
    2. Gandhiji's Swaraj flag (by 1921): red, green and white, with a spinning wheel for self-help. (1)
    Common trap: giving the Swaraj flag a chakra or saffron. That is the later national flag — not in this chapter.

Worked example 3

Who felt left out?

Question. Why did some efforts to build a sense of collective belonging fail to appeal to all communities? (2 marks)

Asked: the reason for the limited appeal.

Concept used: The limits (section 5).

  1. 1

    Whose past: the past being glorified was often Hindu, and the images (like Bharat Mata) were drawn from Hindu iconography.

  2. 2

    The result: other communities did not see themselves in these symbols, so the symbols could not unite everyone.

  3. Check: the answer must explain why the appeal was limited; "because people were not educated" gives a reason the textbook does not.

  4. Answer The past being praised and many of the images were Hindu, so other communities felt excluded.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Nationalist histories often glorified a Hindu past, and images such as Bharat Mata drew on Hindu iconography. (1)
    2. As a result, people of other communities felt left out, which limited the unifying power of these symbols. (1)
    Common trap: writing that "no one liked the symbols". The textbook says they were powerful — but not for all communities.

Common mistakes!

  • "Rabindranath Tagore painted Bharat Mata."

    Abanindranath Tagore painted it; Rabindranath collected folk ballads.

    Painter = Abanindranath; folklore = Rabindranath.

  • "Bankim Chandra painted Bharat Mata."

    He first created the image and wrote "Vande Mataram" — he did not paint it.

    Bankim = image + hymn.

  • Swapping the two flags' colours and symbols.

    Swadeshi: red–green–yellow, lotuses, crescent; Swaraj flag: red–green–white, spinning wheel.

    Yellow → lotuses; white → spinning wheel.

  • "All symbols united everyone."

    Hindu images and a Hindu past made other communities feel left out.

    Always add the limit.

Key points

  • Collective belonging came from united struggles and cultural processes.
  • Bharat Mata: image first created by Bankim Chandra ("Vande Mataram", 1870s, Anandamath, sung in the Swadeshi movement); painted by Abanindranath Tagore (1905) as calm, spiritual, ascetic; later shown with trishul, lion and elephant (power).
  • Folklore: Rabindranath Tagore (Bengal); Natesa Sastri, The Folklore of Southern India (Madras).
  • Flags: Swadeshi — red, green, yellow, 8 lotuses, crescent moon; Swaraj flag (by 1921) — red, green, white, spinning wheel (self-help); carrying it = defiance.
  • History: pride in a glorious past; decline under colonial rule. Limit: a Hindu past and Hindu images left others out (Source E).
  • Quit India (1942): Cripps Mission failed; Wardha 14 July, Bombay 8 August, "Do or Die"; a mass movement; took the British over a year to suppress.
