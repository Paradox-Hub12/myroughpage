History · Chapter 2

Nationalism in India

7 topicsAbout 9 hoursBoard exam
Why this chapter matters

In April 1919 a crowd gathered in an enclosed ground in Amritsar; the exits were blocked and soldiers opened fire, killing hundreds. Eleven years later Mahatma Gandhi walked over 240 miles to the sea to make salt from sea water — and thousands of people across India broke the law with him. Between these two moments, students left their schools, peasants refused to pay revenue, workers left the tea gardens, and people who had never thought of themselves as one nation began to see India as a mother they belonged to. This chapter tells how that happened, and why the unity was never easy.

You will follow the national movement from the First World War to the Non-Cooperation Movement (1921–22) and the Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–34), see what swaraj meant to peasants, tribals, workers, business people, women and dalits, and learn how images, songs, folklore, flags and history built a sense of belonging. You will also learn the eight map places of this chapter. It is one of the four History chapters in the board exam (History carries 18 marks + 2 map marks). In the CBSE sample papers it supplies a 1-mark picture question, a 5-mark long answer and the 2-mark History map question (2026-27), and a 1-mark picture question, a 3-mark answer and the map question (2025-26). In the CBSE board papers of 2024, 2025 and 2026, the History map question came from this chapter in every set, and in 2026 the chapter gave each set 7 or 8 of the 20 History marks.

What you'll learn

  • Explain how the First World War angered Indians, what satyagraha means, and what happened at Champaran, Ahmedabad and Kheda.
  • Explain the Rowlatt satyagraha, the Jallianwala Bagh massacre, the Khilafat issue and how Non-Cooperation was adopted in 1920.
  • Describe how towns, peasants, tribals and plantation workers joined — each with its own idea of swaraj — and why the movement was withdrawn.
  • Explain the road from 1922 to 1930, the Salt March, and how Civil Disobedience spread and ended.
  • Compare what each group wanted, and explain the dalit and Muslim questions.
  • Explain how Bharat Mata, songs, folklore, flags and history built nationalism, and where they fell short; describe the Quit India Movement.
  • Locate and label the eight places of this chapter on an outline map of India and link each to its date and event.
  • Put the whole story in order, compare the two movements, and answer mixed board-style questions.

Most of the marks in this chapter come from five skills, and every topic practises them:

  1. Keep the order of events — every topic ends with a mini-timeline; the wrap-up joins them into one.
  2. Link cause → event → effect — why something started, what happened, what it led to.
  3. Compare groups — who joined, what swaraj meant to them, why some drifted away.
  4. Read a source or picture — what it shows, what it tells you, what it leaves out.
  5. Write to the marks — 2 marks ≈ two points, 3 marks ≈ three points, 5 marks ≈ five explained points, each on its own line.

The spaced-review sets come back to you after 1 day, 1 week and 1 month. Don't skip them — remembering something after a gap is what makes it stick.

Not in the board exam

The textbook's Project (the anti-colonial movement in Indo-China) and the part of the Activity on page 36 that asks about Indo-China refer to a chapter that is not in your 2026-27 book. They get a short guided answer in the NCERT solutions and are not part of the board exam. Everything else in the chapter — including the boxes on Bardoli, Lala Lajpat Rai, the HSRA, "Some important dates" and the Quit India Movement — can be asked in the board exam.
