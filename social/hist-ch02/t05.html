Topic 5 of 7

Who joined the Civil Disobedience Movement — and who held back

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why rich peasants, poor peasants, business classes, industrial workers and women joined the Civil Disobedience Movement, and what each wanted.
  • Explain the dalit question — Gandhiji's view, Ambedkar's demand and the Poona Pact (1932).
  • Explain why many Muslim groups were lukewarm and why leaders differed over separate electorates (Source D).

A rich farmer, a poor tenant, a mill owner, a mill worker, a woman who had never left her home alone, a dalit schoolteacher and a Muslim lawyer all lived in India in 1930. Would all seven march behind the same flag? Some did, some did for a while, and some held back — each for a reason. Learning those reasons is how you answer half the questions on this chapter.

  1. How the participants saw the movement

Group Why they joined — and what swaraj meant to them What happened later
Rich peasants — e.g. Patidars of Gujarat, Jats of Uttar Pradesh as producers of commercial crops, they were hard hit by the trade depression and falling prices; with cash income gone they could not pay revenue, and the government refused to reduce it. Swaraj meant: a struggle against high revenues bitterly disappointed that the movement ended in 1931 with no change in revenue rates; many stayed out when it restarted in 1932
Poorer peasants — mostly small tenants on rented land could not pay their rent as incomes fell; wanted unpaid rent to the landlord remitted. Swaraj meant: relief from rent turned to radical movements, many led by Socialists and Communists; the Congress, afraid of annoying rich peasants and landlords, would not support "no rent" campaigns in most places → their relationship stayed uncertain
Business classes — merchants and industrialists had grown rich and powerful through large wartime profits and now resented colonial policies that held back business; wanted protection against foreign imports and a rupee–sterling exchange ratio that would discourage imports. Swaraj meant: a time without colonial restrictions on business, when trade and industry would flourish formed the Indian Industrial and Commercial Congress (1920) and FICCI (1927); led by Purshottamdas Thakurdas and G. D. Birla; gave financial help and refused to buy or sell imported goods. After the Round Table Conference failed, no longer uniformly keen: feared militant activity, long disruption of business, and socialism among young Congress members
Industrial workers mostly stayed away — the Nagpur region was the exception; some used the boycott of foreign goods as part of their own fight against low wages and poor working conditions — railway strikes (1930), dockworkers (1932), Chotanagpur tin mine workers in Gandhi caps (1930). Swaraj meant: better wages and working conditions the Congress was reluctant to include workers' demands: it feared alienating industrialists and dividing the anti-imperial forces
Women large-scale participation — listened to Gandhiji on the salt march, marched in protests, made salt, and picketed shops selling foreign cloth or liquor; many were jailed (NCERT Fig. 9). Urban women from high-caste families; rural women from rich peasant households. Swaraj meant: service to the nation as a sacred duty their public role did not radically change how women's position was seen: Gandhiji thought women's duty was home and hearth; the Congress was reluctant to give women positions of authority and wanted only their symbolic presence

New word. Remit a rent — cancel or let off what is owed.

  1. The limits of Civil Disobedience — the dalits

  • From around the 1930s the "untouchables" began to call themselves dalit (oppressed). The Congress had long neglected them, worried about upsetting the sanatanis (conservative high-caste Hindus).
  • Gandhiji's way: he said that without ending untouchability, swaraj would not come even in a hundred years. He called the "untouchables" harijan (children of God), organised satyagraha for their entry into temples and access to public wells, tanks, roads and schools, cleaned toilets himself to dignify the work of the bhangi (sweepers), and asked upper castes to give up "the sin of untouchability".
  • Many dalit leaders wanted a political solution: seats kept aside for them in educational institutions, and a separate electorate in which dalit voters alone would elect dalit members of the legislative councils. Political empowerment, they believed, would solve their social disabilities. So dalit participation in the Civil Disobedience Movement was limited, especially in Maharashtra and the Nagpur region, where their organisation was strong.
  • Dr B. R. Ambedkar, who brought dalits together in the Depressed Classes Association (1930), came into conflict with Gandhiji at the second Round Table Conference over his demand for separate electorates for dalits.
  • The British accepted Ambedkar's demand; in response Gandhiji began a fast unto death — he believed separate electorates would slow down their integration into society.
  • Poona Pact, September 1932: Ambedkar accepted Gandhiji's position. The Depressed Classes (later the Scheduled Castes) got seats reserved for them in the provincial and central legislative councils, but all voters — the general electorate — would elect those members. The dalit movement remained apprehensive of the Congress-led movement.

Separate electorate vs reserved seats — the difference in one line each:

  • Separate electorate: only voters of that community elect its representatives.
  • Reserved seats (Poona Pact): some seats can be contested only by dalit candidates, but all voters of the area elect them.

  1. The limits of Civil Disobedience — Muslim groups

  • Once the Non-Cooperation–Khilafat Movement faded, many Muslims felt cut off from the Congress.
  • From the mid-1920s the Congress was seen more and more alongside openly Hindu religious nationalist groups like the Hindu Mahasabha. As relations worsened, religious processions with militant fervour led to communal clashes and riots; every riot deepened the distance.
  • 1927: the Congress and the Muslim League tried to renegotiate an alliance, and unity seemed possible. The key difference was over representation in future assemblies.
  • Muhammad Ali Jinnah offered to drop the demand for separate electorates provided Muslims were promised reserved seats in the Central Assembly and representation in proportion to population in the Muslim-majority provinces (Bengal and Punjab).
  • 1928, All Parties Conference: the hope ended when M. R. Jayakar of the Hindu Mahasabha firmly resisted any compromise.
  • So, when the Civil Disobedience Movement began, there was suspicion and distrust; many Muslim leaders worried that a Hindu majority would swamp the culture and identity of minorities.

Reading Source D (Sir Muhammad Iqbal, 1930, as president of the Muslim League). Iqbal defended separate electorates as a safeguard for Muslims' political interests. He said that a community's wish to develop on its own lines was not narrow communalism, that he respected other communities, and that Indian society is made of communal groups, not territorial units as in Europe. Your textbook notes that this speech is thought to have given the intellectual justification for the later demand for Pakistan. Why leaders differed: some saw separate electorates as a safeguard for minorities; others (like Gandhiji for dalits) saw them as dividing people and slowing integration.

Worked example 1

Why did the rich peasants step back?

Question. Why did many rich peasants who had joined the Civil Disobedience Movement with enthusiasm refuse to join it again in 1932? (2 marks)

Asked: the reason for their change.

Concept used: Rich peasants in the Civil Disobedience Movement (section 1). Why it applies: their reason for joining explains their reason for leaving.

  1. 1

    Why they had joined: falling prices left them unable to pay revenue; for them swaraj meant a struggle against high revenue.

  2. 2

    Why they left: when the movement ended in 1931, revenue rates had not been changed — their main demand was not met, so they felt let down.

  3. Check: the reason for leaving must connect to revenue, their own demand. "Because the Congress did not support no-rent campaigns" fails — that was the poor peasants' grievance.

  4. Answer They had joined to get revenue reduced; since it ended in 1931 with revenue unchanged, they were disappointed and stayed out in 1932.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Rich peasants such as the Patidars and Jats joined because falling prices made it impossible to pay the government's revenue demand. (1)
    2. The movement was called off in 1931 without revising revenue rates, so, disappointed, many refused to join in 1932. (1)
    Common trap: writing "rent" for rich peasants. Rich peasants paid revenue to the government; poor tenants paid rent to landlords.

Worked example 2

Separate electorate or reserved seats?

Question. Explain the disagreement between Gandhiji and Dr B. R. Ambedkar at the second Round Table Conference and how the Poona Pact settled it. (3 marks)

Asked: the disagreement; the settlement.

Concept used: The dalit question (section 2).

  1. 1

    Ambedkar's demand: separate electorates for dalits — he believed political empowerment would end their social disabilities.

  2. 2

    Gandhiji's objection: separate electorates would slow down dalits' integration into society; when the British conceded the demand, he fasted unto death.

  3. 3

    The settlement — Poona Pact, September 1932: dalits got seats reserved for them in the provincial and central councils, but all voters (the general electorate) would elect those members.

  4. Check: the Pact must be a compromise — it gives dalits something (reserved seats) and keeps Gandhiji's point (a common electorate). An answer saying "dalits got separate electorates" makes it a defeat for Gandhiji, not a compromise, so it fails.

  5. Answer Ambedkar wanted separate electorates; Gandhiji opposed them and fasted; the Poona Pact (1932) gave reserved seats elected by the general electorate.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. At the second Round Table Conference, Ambedkar demanded separate electorates for dalits, believing political empowerment would end their social disabilities. (1)
    2. Gandhiji felt this would slow their integration into society; when the British conceded it, he began a fast unto death. (1)
    3. The Poona Pact (September 1932) gave them reserved seats, voted in by the general electorate. (1)
    Common trap: saying Ambedkar fasted. It was Gandhiji who fasted unto death.

Worked example 3

The Congress and the workers

Question. Why did the Congress hesitate to include workers' demands in its programme during the Civil Disobedience Movement? (2 marks)

Asked: the Congress's reasons.

Concept used: Industrial workers in the Civil Disobedience Movement (section 1).

  1. 1

    Industrialists were now close to the Congress and were supporting the movement with money and boycotts.

  2. 2

    Fear: taking up workers' demands (wages, conditions) would turn the industrialists away and split the forces fighting British rule.

  3. Check: both reasons are about keeping the united front against British rule; an answer that says "because workers did not want swaraj" fails — workers in Chotanagpur, on the railways and in the docks did join protests.

  4. Answer To avoid alienating the industrialists who supported it, and to avoid dividing the anti-imperial forces.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Industrialists had come close to the Congress and supported the movement; workers' demands about wages and conditions would alienate them. (1)
    2. The Congress feared such demands would divide the anti-imperial forces fighting British rule. (1)
    Common trap: writing that "industrial workers joined in large numbers everywhere". They did so mainly in the Nagpur region.

Common mistakes!

  • Mixing rich and poor peasants' aims.

    Rich peasants: revenue cut. Poor tenants: rent remitted.

    Revenue → government; rent → landlord.

  • "Industrial workers joined in large numbers everywhere."

    Only in the Nagpur region; elsewhere they stayed aloof.

    Write "except in the Nagpur region".

  • "The Congress backed the no-rent campaigns."

    It was unwilling, in most places, to upset rich peasants and landlords.

    Explain the uncertain relationship.

  • "The Poona Pact gave dalits separate electorates."

    It gave reserved seats, voted in by the general electorate.

    Reserved seats + general electorate.

  • "Ambedkar went on a fast."

    Gandhiji fasted unto death.

    Ambedkar demanded; Gandhiji fasted.

Key points

  • Rich peasants (Patidars, Jats): against high revenue; disappointed in 1931; many stayed out in 1932.
  • Poor peasants: wanted rent remitted; the Congress would not back "no rent" campaigns.
  • Business classes: profits in the war; wanted protection from imports and a helpful exchange ratio; Indian Industrial and Commercial Congress (1920), FICCI (1927), Purshottamdas Thakurdas, G. D. Birla; less keen after the Round Table Conference failed.
  • Industrial workers: few, except the Nagpur region; the Congress kept their demands out. Women: large-scale participation, but no radical change in their position.
  • Dalits: Gandhiji (harijan, temple entry) vs Ambedkar (Depressed Classes Association 1930, separate electorates) → fast → Poona Pact, September 1932 (reserved seats, general electorate).
  • Muslims: alienated after Khilafat; Congress linked with the Hindu Mahasabha; 1927 unity talks; Jinnah's offer; 1928 compromise fails (Jayakar); fear of a Hindu majority (Source D, Iqbal).
