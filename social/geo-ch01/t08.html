Topic 8 of 8

Soil erosion and soil conservation

About 30 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain soil erosion, its types (gully, sheet and wind erosion; bad land and ravines) and its causes.
  • Match each soil conservation method (contour ploughing, terrace cultivation, strip cropping, shelter belts) to the problem and place it suits.

After a heavy storm, a muddy stream runs down a bare hillside, carrying the soil with it. On a dry, windy day, dust blows off a ploughed field. Soil that took nature millions of years to build can disappear in one season. But farmers have old, simple ways to hold it in place — with the shape of their fields, lines of grass and rows of trees.

  1. What is soil erosion?

  • Soil erosion is the denudation of the soil cover and its subsequent washing down.
  • Soil formation and erosion go on simultaneously, and generally there is a balance between the two.
  • The balance is disturbed by human activities — deforestation, over-grazing, construction and mining, etc. — and by natural forces — wind, glacier and water.

  1. Types of soil erosion

Type What happens Result / example
Gully erosion running water cuts through clayey soils and makes deep channels called gullies the land becomes unfit for cultivation — bad land; in the Chambal basin such lands are called ravines
Sheet erosion water flows as a sheet over large areas down a slope, and the top soil is washed away thin top layer lost over a wide area
Wind erosion wind blows loose soil off flat or sloping land dust and drifting sand
Defective farming ploughing up and down the slope forms channels for the quick flow of water soil erosion on slopes

  1. Soil conservation methods

Method How it works Fights Where (your textbook)
Contour ploughing ploughing along the contour lines decelerates the flow of water down the slopes water erosion on slopes —
Terrace cultivation steps are cut out on the slopes, making terraces; terrace cultivation restricts erosion water erosion on slopes western and central Himalayas have well developed terrace farming
Strip cropping large fields are divided into strips; strips of grass are left to grow between the crops; this breaks up the force of the wind wind erosion —
Shelter belts planting lines of trees to create shelter; rows of such trees are called shelter belts; they work like strip cropping wind erosion contributed to the stabilisation of sand dunes and the desert in western India

Memory hook — "Slopes need steps and lines; winds need strips and trees":

  • water running down a slope → contour ploughing, terraces;
  • wind → strip cropping, shelter belts.

Contour line (new word). A line joining places at the same height on a slope. Ploughing along it (across the slope) makes ridges that slow water down; ploughing up and down the slope makes channels that speed it up.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

The hill farmer

Question. A farmer on a hill slope ploughs his field from the top of the slope straight down to the bottom. After every heavy rain, muddy water rushes down and his field loses soil. Explain the cause and suggest two methods from your textbook to stop it. (3 marks)

Asked: the cause (one point) and two methods (two points).

Concept used: Defective farming and conservation methods (sections 2–3).

  1. 1

    Cause: ploughing up and down the slope forms channels for the quick flow of water, which carries soil away.

  2. 2

    Method 1 — contour ploughing: plough along the contour lines; this slows the flow of water down the slope.

  3. 3

    Method 2 — terrace cultivation: cut steps (terraces) on the slope; terrace cultivation restricts erosion, as in the western and central Himalayas.

  4. Check: both methods fight water on a slope, which is the farmer's problem. Strip cropping and shelter belts break the wind, so they do not suit this problem.

  5. Answer Cause: ploughing up and down the slope makes channels for fast water. Methods: contour ploughing and terrace cultivation.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Ploughing up and down the slope forms channels for the quick flow of water, washing soil away. (1)
    2. Contour ploughing, along the contour lines, slows the flow of water down the slope. (1)
    3. Cutting terraces on the slope restricts erosion, as in the western and central Himalayas. (1)
    Common trap: writing "plant shelter belts" for a hill field. Shelter belts break the force of the wind; the hill farmer's problem is running water.

Worked example 2

At the edge of the desert

Question. Villages at the edge of a desert find loose sand blowing onto their fields every dry season. Which two methods from your textbook suit them, and why? (2 marks)

Asked: two methods and the reason.

Concept used: Conservation methods against wind (section 3).

  1. 1

    Shelter belts: rows of trees break the force of the wind; your textbook says they helped stabilise sand dunes and the desert in western India.

  2. 2

    Strip cropping: strips of grass between crops break up the force of the wind, so less soil blows away.

  3. Check: both methods act on wind, the cause here. Terraces would not help on flat, windy land.

  4. Answer Shelter belts and strip cropping — both break the force of the wind.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Shelter belts — rows of trees break the wind and have stabilised sand dunes in western India. (1)
    2. Strip cropping — strips of grass between crops break up the force of the wind. (1)
    Common trap: choosing contour ploughing. It slows water on slopes, not wind.

Common mistakes!

  • "Strip cropping stops water running down hills."

    Strip cropping breaks the force of the wind.

    Wind → strips, shelter belts; slopes → contour ploughing, terraces.

  • "Ravines are found in the Thar desert."

    Your textbook names the Chambal basin for ravines.

    Ravines = bad land made by gullies, Chambal basin.

  • "Terrace farming is well developed in Punjab."

    The textbook names the western and central Himalayas.

    Terraces = hill slopes.

  • Mixing gully and sheet erosion.

    Gully: deep channels in clayey soils; sheet: a sheet of water removes top soil over large areas.

    Channel → gully; sheet → sheet.

Key points

  • Soil erosion: denudation of the soil cover and its washing down; formation and erosion usually balance, but deforestation, over-grazing, construction, mining and wind, glacier, water upset it.
  • Gully erosion → deep channels → bad land; in the Chambal basin, ravines. Sheet erosion → top soil washed off large areas. Wind erosion → loose soil blown off. Ploughing up and down the slope → channels for quick water flow.
  • Contour ploughing slows water on slopes; terraces restrict erosion (western and central Himalayas); strip cropping and shelter belts break the wind (shelter belts stabilised sand dunes and the desert in western India).
