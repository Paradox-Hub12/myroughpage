Topic 4 of 8

Land resources and land use

About 45 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe India's land under relief features and the land-use categories, including fallow land by years, net sown area and gross cropped area.
  • Read the land-use pie charts (NCERT Fig. 1.4) and explain India's land-use pattern, the forest target and the net sown area by state.

We live on land, grow food on it, build roads and factories on it — and there is only so much of it. Every new house, road or field takes land from something else: a forest, a pasture, a patch of wasteland. This topic shows how India uses its land, and how that use has — and has not — changed in more than fifty years.

Quick reminder (pie chart and per cent)

A pie chart shows a whole (100 per cent) cut into slices. The bigger the slice, the bigger the share. To compare two years, compare the same slice in both charts: if 18 per cent became 23 per cent, that share rose by about 5 percentage points.

  1. Land — a resource of finite size

Land is a natural resource of utmost importance: it supports natural vegetation, wild life, human life, economic activities, transport and communication systems. But land is an asset of a finite magnitude, so we must use the available land for various purposes with careful planning.

  1. India's land under relief features (NCERT Fig. 1.3)

India has mountains, plateaus, plains and islands.

Relief Share of the land area Why it matters (your textbook)
Plains about 43 per cent facilities for agriculture and industry
Mountains 30 per cent of the total surface area perennial flow of some rivers, tourism and ecological aspects
Plateau about 27 per cent rich reserves of minerals, fossil fuels and forests

Memory hook: 43 · 30 · 27 — plains, mountains, plateau, from the biggest share to the smallest. The three add up to 100.

Quick reminder (Class 9, Physical Features of India). The northern plains lie between the Himalayas and the peninsular plateau; the Deccan plateau is the large plateau of the south.

  1. Land-use categories

Your textbook lists land use under five heads:

Category Includes
1. Forests —
2. Land not available for cultivation (a) barren and waste land; (b) land put to non-agricultural uses — buildings, roads, factories, etc.
3. Other uncultivated land (excluding fallow land) (a) permanent pastures and grazing land; (b) land under miscellaneous tree crops groves (not included in net sown area); (c) culturable waste land — left uncultivated for more than 5 agricultural years
4. Fallow lands (a) current fallow — left without cultivation for one or less than one agricultural year; (b) other than current fallow — left uncultivated for the past 1 to 5 agricultural years
5. Net sown area the physical extent of land on which crops are sown and harvested

Fallow by years — memory hook "1 · 5 · more than 5":

  • up to 1 year → current fallow;
  • 1 to 5 years → other than current fallow;
  • more than 5 years → culturable waste land.

Net sown area and gross cropped area.

  • Net sown area (NSA): the physical extent of land on which crops are sown and harvested — each field counted once.
  • Gross cropped area: area sown more than once in an agricultural year plus net sown area — a field sown twice is counted twice.

Note on the textbook's wording. Item 5 is printed "the physical extent of land on which crops are sown harvested"; read it as "sown and harvested". The textbook also prints "Cultruable" for culturable.

  1. What decides land use?

The use of land is determined by:

  • physical factors — topography, climate, soil types; and
  • human factors — population density, technological capability, culture and traditions, etc.

  1. Reading NCERT Fig. 1.4 — land use 1960-61 and 2014-15

Fig. 1.4 has two pie charts, "General land use categories" for 1960-61 and 2014-15 (reporting area 100 per cent; source: Directorate of Economics and Statistics, Ministry of Agriculture, 2017). The shares, exactly as printed:

Category 1960-61 (%) 2014-15 (%)
Forest 18.11 23.3
Barren and unculturable waste land 12.01 5.5
Area under non-agricultural uses 4.95 8.7
Permanent pasture and grazing land 4.71 3.3
Area under misc. tree crops and groves 1.50 1
Culturable waste land 6.23 4.0

Note on the figure. The 1960-61 shares add up to 100.00 per cent, but the nine 2014-15 shares as printed add up to 99.8 per cent, probably because of rounding in the source data. Use the numbers exactly as printed.

Three questions for any chart — What does it show? What changed? What does the textbook say about it?

  • Shows: how India's reporting area was used in two years.
  • Changed: the biggest rise is forest (18.11 → 23.3); the biggest fall is barren and unculturable waste land (12.01 → 5.5); non-agricultural uses rose (4.95 → 8.7); permanent pasture fell (4.71 → 3.3); net sown area hardly changed (45.26 → 45.5).
  • Textbook says: forest area is still far lower than the desired 33 per cent of geographical area outlined in the National Forest Policy (1952), considered essential for ecological balance; the livelihood of millions living on the fringes of forests depends on it.

  1. More facts about India's land use

  • Total geographical area: 3.28 million sq km. Land-use data are available only for 93 per cent of it, because land-use reporting for most north-east states except Assam has not been done fully, and some areas of Jammu and Kashmir occupied by Pakistan and China have not been surveyed.
  • Permanent pasture has decreased. Your textbook asks how we feed our huge cattle population on this land, and what the consequences are (see the NCERT solutions).
  • Other than current fallow lands are mostly of poor quality or costly to cultivate, so they are cultivated once or twice in about two to three years. If they are included in net sown area, NSA comes to about 54 per cent of the total reporting area.
  • NSA varies greatly by state: over 80 per cent of the total area in Punjab and Haryana; less than 10 per cent in Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Manipur and Andaman Nicobar Islands.
  • Waste land includes rocky, arid and desert areas; land put to other non-agricultural uses includes settlements, roads, railways, industry, etc.
  • Continuous use of land over a long period without measures to conserve and manage it has resulted in land degradation (Topic 5).

Worked example 1

What rose most, what fell most?

Question. Using NCERT Fig. 1.4, name the land-use category that rose most between 1960-61 and 2014-15 and the one that fell most. Give the figures.

Asked: the biggest rise and the biggest fall, with numbers.

Concept used: Reading Fig. 1.4 (section 5).

  1. 1

    Rises: forest 18.11 → 23.3 (up 5.19 points); non-agricultural uses 4.95 → 8.7 (up 3.75); current fallow 3.73 → 4.9 (up 1.17); others rose by less.

  2. 2

    Falls: barren and unculturable waste land 12.01 → 5.5 (down 6.51 points); culturable waste land 6.23 → 4.0 (down 2.23); permanent pasture 4.71 → 3.3 (down 1.41).

  3. 3

    Pick the largest of each: biggest rise = forest; biggest fall = barren and unculturable waste land.

  4. Check: 23.3 − 18.11 = 5.19, bigger than 8.7 − 4.95 = 3.75 ✔; 12.01 − 5.5 = 6.51, bigger than 6.23 − 4.0 = 2.23 ✔.

  5. Answer Rose most: forest (18.11 → 23.3 per cent). Fell most: barren and unculturable waste land (12.01 → 5.5 per cent).

    Common trap: saying "net sown area rose most" because it is the biggest slice. It is the largest share, but it changed least (45.26 → 45.5).

Worked example 2

Four plots, four names

Question. A village record shows four plots. Plot P has not been cultivated for 7 agricultural years. Plot Q was left without cultivation in the last agricultural year only. Plot R has been left uncultivated for the past 3 agricultural years. Plot S is the land on which the village school and road stand. Name the land-use category of each.

Asked: the category of each plot.

Concept used: Land-use categories and "1 · 5 · more than 5" (section 3).

  1. 1

    P — 7 years: more than 5 → culturable waste land.

  2. 2

    Q — one year or less: → current fallow.

  3. 3

    R — 3 years: between 1 and 5 → other than current fallow.

  4. 4

    S — school and road: → land put to non-agricultural uses (part of "land not available for cultivation").

  5. Check: go along the hook "1 · 5 · more than 5": Q (≤ 1) → R (3) → P (7) gives current → other than current → culturable waste ✔. S is not fallow at all — buildings and roads are a separate category.

  6. Answer P culturable waste land; Q current fallow; R other than current fallow; S land put to non-agricultural uses.

    Common trap: calling P "barren land". Barren land cannot be cultivated; P can be (it is culturable) but has been left for more than 5 years.

Worked example 3

Net sown area and gross cropped area

Question. A farmer owns 10 hectares. In one agricultural year he sows 8 hectares with a kharif crop; after the harvest he sows 5 of those same 8 hectares again with a rabi crop. The other 2 hectares stay unsown. Find his net sown area and gross cropped area.

Asked: NSA and gross cropped area.

Concept used: Definitions (section 3).

  1. 1

    Net sown area = the land on which crops are sown and harvested, each field counted once = 8 hectares.

  2. 2

    Area sown more than once = 5 hectares.

  3. 3

    Gross cropped area = area sown more than once + net sown area = 5 + 8 = 13 hectares.

  4. Check: gross cropped area can never be smaller than net sown area, and here 13 > 8 ✔. The 2 unsown hectares are in neither figure.

  5. Answer Net sown area 8 hectares; gross cropped area 13 hectares.

    Common trap: adding the 2 unsown hectares, or writing gross cropped area = 10 (the size of the farm). Count sowings, not the farm's size.

Common mistakes!

  • Gross cropped area = net sown area.

    Gross cropped area = area sown more than once + net sown area.

    A field sown twice counts twice in gross cropped area.

  • Mixing up the fallow names and years.

    Current fallow ≤ 1; other than current fallow 1–5; culturable waste land > 5 agricultural years.

    "1 · 5 · more than 5".

  • Relief shares swapped (plateau 43, plains 27).

    Plains 43, mountains 30, plateau 27.

    "43 · 30 · 27".

  • "Plateaus ensure the perennial flow of rivers."

    Mountains do; plateaus have minerals, fossil fuels and forests.

    Match each relief to its use.

  • "Forest has reached the 33 per cent target."

    Forest is 23.3 per cent (2014-15), far lower than the 33 per cent of the National Forest Policy (1952).

    Quote 18.11 → 23.3, "far lower than 33".

  • "Net sown area doubled."

    45.26 → 45.5 per cent — almost no change.

    Quote both numbers.

Key points

  • Land is of finite magnitude; use it with careful planning.
  • Relief: plains ~43% (agriculture, industry), mountains 30% (perennial rivers, tourism, ecology), plateau ~27% (minerals, fossil fuels, forests).
  • Five land-use categories; fallow by years: current ≤ 1, other than current 1–5, culturable waste > 5. Gross cropped area = area sown more than once + NSA.
  • Land use depends on physical (topography, climate, soil) and human (population density, technology, culture and traditions) factors.
  • Fig. 1.4: forest 18.11 → 23.3; barren 12.01 → 5.5; non-agricultural uses 4.95 → 8.7; pasture 4.71 → 3.3; NSA 45.26 → 45.5. The 2014-15 shares as printed add to 99.8.
  • 3.28 million sq km; data for 93 per cent; NSA over 80% in Punjab and Haryana, under 10% in Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Manipur, Andaman and Nicobar; forest far below 33% (National Forest Policy, 1952).
