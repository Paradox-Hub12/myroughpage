Topic 6 of 8

Soil as a resource and the soils of India

About 50 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain how soil forms — the factors, the forces of nature and the soil profile (NCERT Fig. 1.5).
  • Describe the six soil types — where they are found, their features and their crops — and tell them apart.

Pick up a handful of soil. It took nature millions of years to make a layer only a few centimetres thick. It is alive with tiny organisms, and it feeds almost every plant you eat. India has many kinds of soil — black, red, yellow, sandy, fine and coarse — and a farmer who knows his soil knows what to grow.

  1. Soil — a living resource

  • Soil is the most important renewable natural resource. It is the medium of plant growth and supports different types of living organisms on the earth.
  • The soil is a living system. It takes millions of years to form soil up to a few cm in depth.

  1. How soil forms

Factors in the formation of soil: relief, parent rock or bed rock, climate, vegetation and other forms of life, and time.

Forces of nature that contribute: change in temperature, actions of running water, wind and glaciers, activities of decomposers, etc.

Chemical and organic changes in the soil are equally important. Soil consists of organic (humus) and inorganic materials.

Memory hook for the factors — "Really Patient Clay Values Time": Relief, Parent rock, Climate, Vegetation (and other forms of life), Time.

Quick reminder (Class 9, Climate). India's rainfall comes mostly in the monsoon; some places have alternate wet and dry seasons, and the north-west is very dry.

  1. The soil profile (NCERT Fig. 1.5)

From the top down, NCERT Fig. 1.5 shows four layers:

  1. Top soil — the upper soil layer;
  2. Subsoil — weathered rocks, sand, silt and clay;
  3. Substratum — weathered parent rock material;
  4. Unweathered parent bed rock.

  1. How soils are classified

On the basis of the factors responsible for soil formation, colour, thickness, texture, age, chemical and physical properties, the soils of India are classified into different types. India's varied relief features, landforms, climatic realms and vegetation types have produced these different soils.

  1. The six soils

(1) Alluvial soils (NCERT Fig. 1.6)

  • The most widely spread and important soil. The entire northern plains are made of alluvial soil, deposited by three Himalayan river systems — the Indus, the Ganga and the Brahmaputra.
  • Also extend into Rajasthan and Gujarat through a narrow corridor, and are found in the eastern coastal plains, particularly the deltas of the Mahanadi, the Godavari, the Krishna and the Kaveri.
  • Consist of various proportions of sand, silt and clay. Moving inland towards the river valleys, particles become somewhat bigger; in the upper reaches of the valley, near the break of slope, the soils are coarse — common in piedmont plains such as Duars, Chos and Terai.
  • By age: old alluvial (bangar) and new alluvial (khadar). Bangar has a higher concentration of kankar nodules than khadar. Khadar has more fine particles and is more fertile than bangar.
  • Very fertile as a whole; mostly contain adequate potash, phosphoric acid and lime; ideal for sugarcane, paddy, wheat and other cereal and pulse crops.
  • Because of high fertility, alluvial regions are intensively cultivated and densely populated. Soils in drier areas are more alkaline and can be productive after proper treatment and irrigation.

Memory hook (bangar and khadar): Bangar is Bygone — the old alluvium, with more kankar nodules; Khadar is the Kid — the new alluvium, finer and more fertile.

(2) Black soil (NCERT Fig. 1.7)

  • Black in colour; also called regur soil; ideal for growing cotton, so also called black cotton soil.
  • Formed by climatic conditions along with the parent rock material; typical of the Deccan trap (basalt) region spread over the north-west Deccan plateau, made up of lava flows.
  • Covers the plateaus of Maharashtra, Saurashtra, Malwa, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh, and extends south-east along the Godavari and Krishna valleys.
  • Made of extremely fine (clayey) material; well known for its capacity to hold moisture.
  • Rich in soil nutrients — calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime; generally poor in phosphoric contents.
  • Develops deep cracks in hot weather, which help aeration; sticky when wet, difficult to work unless tilled immediately after the first shower or during the pre-monsoon period.

(3) Red and yellow soils

  • Red soil develops on crystalline igneous rocks in areas of low rainfall in the eastern and southern parts of the Deccan plateau.
  • Yellow and red soils are also found in parts of Odisha, Chhattisgarh, the southern parts of the middle Ganga plain and along the piedmont zone of the Western Ghats.
  • Reddish colour comes from the diffusion of iron in crystalline and metamorphic rocks; it looks yellow when it occurs in a hydrated form.

(4) Laterite soil (NCERT Fig. 1.8)

  • From the Latin word 'later' — brick.
  • Develops under tropical and subtropical climate with alternate wet and dry season; the result of intense leaching due to heavy rain.
  • Mostly deep to very deep, acidic (pH < 6.0), generally deficient in plant nutrients.
  • Occurs mostly in southern states, the Western Ghats region of Maharashtra, Odisha, some parts of West Bengal and North-east regions.
  • Humus rich where it supports deciduous and evergreen forests; humus poor under sparse vegetation and in semi-arid environments. Prone to erosion and degradation.
  • After soil conservation techniques, particularly in the hilly areas of Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu, very useful for tea and coffee. Red laterite soils in Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Kerala are more suitable for cashew nut.

(5) Arid soils (NCERT Fig. 1.9)

  • Red to brown in colour; generally sandy in texture and saline in nature; in some areas the salt content is so high that common salt is obtained by evaporating the water.
  • Due to the dry climate and high temperature, evaporation is faster and the soil lacks humus and moisture.
  • The lower horizons are occupied by kankar because calcium content increases downwards; the kankar layer restricts the infiltration of water.
  • After proper irrigation these soils become cultivable, as in western Rajasthan.

(6) Forest soils

  • Found in hilly and mountainous areas where sufficient rain forests are available.
  • Texture varies with the mountain environment: loamy and silty on valley sides, coarse grained on the upper slopes.
  • In the snow-covered areas of the Himalayas they experience denudation and are acidic with low humus content.
  • Soils in the lower parts of the valleys, particularly on river terraces and alluvial fans, are fertile.

Spelling notes from the textbook. It prints "kanker" on p. 7 and "Kankar" on p. 9 (the same word), and "Yelllow" with three l's on p. 9 (read: yellow).

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Which soil is it?

Question. A farmer from the hills of Kerala says: "My soil is reddish, deep and acidic. Heavy rain washes the goodness out of it, so I had to use soil conservation methods before I could grow coffee on it." Name the soil and give one reason. (1 mark + reason)

Asked: the soil and the clue that proves it.

Concept used: The six soils (section 5).

  1. 1

    Clues: heavy rain washing nutrients out (= intense leaching), acidic, deep, coffee after soil conservation in the hilly areas of Kerala.

  2. 2

    Match: every clue is in the laterite paragraph.

  3. Check: acidity (pH < 6.0) and leaching are laterite clues; black soil is known for cotton and holding moisture; arid soil belongs to dry areas, not heavy rain. Only laterite fits all the clues.

  4. Answer Laterite soil — it is the result of intense leaching due to heavy rain, and after soil conservation it is used for coffee in the hilly areas of Kerala.

    Common trap: choosing "red soil" because of the colour. Red soil develops in areas of low rainfall; heavy rain and leaching point to laterite.

Worked example 2

Advising a cotton farmer

Question. A farmer can buy land either on the black soils of the Malwa plateau or on the alluvial soils of the northern plains. He wants to grow cotton. Compare the two soils and advise him. (3 marks)

Asked: a comparison and advice for cotton.

Concept used: Black and alluvial soils (section 5).

  1. 1

    Black soil — fit for cotton: your textbook says black soil is ideal for growing cotton (black cotton soil); it is clayey and holds moisture.

  2. 2

    Alluvial soil — fit for other crops: very fertile, with potash, phosphoric acid and lime, but your textbook names sugarcane, paddy, wheat, cereals and pulses as its crops.

  3. 3

    Advice and its condition: choose the Malwa black soil, but remember it is sticky when wet and must be tilled immediately after the first shower or in the pre-monsoon period.

  4. Check: the three points do three jobs — why black soil suits cotton, what alluvial soil is best known for, and how to work the chosen soil. No point repeats another.

  5. Answer Buy the black soil of Malwa: it is ideal for cotton and holds moisture; alluvial soil is best known for sugarcane, paddy, wheat, cereals and pulses. Till the black soil early because it is sticky when wet.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Black soil is ideal for cotton; it is clayey and holds moisture well. (1)
    2. Alluvial soil is very fertile but is known for sugarcane, paddy, wheat, cereals and pulses. (1)
    3. So choose Malwa's black soil, tilling it after the first shower as it is sticky when wet. (1)
    Common trap: advising alluvial soil "because it is the most fertile". The question is about cotton, and the textbook links cotton with black soil.

Common mistakes!

  • "Bangar is new alluvium; khadar has more kankar."

    Bangar = old, more kankar nodules; khadar = new, finer, more fertile.

    "Bangar is bygone; khadar is the kid."

  • "Black soil is rich in phosphorus."

    Black soil is rich in calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime but poor in phosphoric contents.

    Learn the "rich in" and "poor in" lists together.

  • Laterite linked with cotton, or black soil with tea.

    Black → cotton; laterite → tea and coffee (after conservation), cashew (red laterite); alluvial → sugarcane, paddy, wheat.

    Learn soil ↔ crop pairs from the table.

  • "Red soil forms in heavy rain."

    Red soil develops in areas of low rainfall; heavy rain and intense leaching form laterite.

    Heavy rain with intense leaching → laterite; red soil on crystalline rocks in low-rainfall parts of the Deccan.

  • Forgetting the kankar layer of arid soils.

    Arid soils have kankar in the lower horizons, which restricts infiltration.

    Arid = sandy, saline, kankar below.

Key points

  • Soil is the most important renewable natural resource, a living system; millions of years make a few cm.
  • Factors: relief, parent (bed) rock, climate, vegetation and other forms of life, time. Forces: temperature change, running water, wind, glaciers, decomposers. Profile: top soil → subsoil → substratum → unweathered parent bed rock.
  • Alluvial: northern plains + eastern deltas; sand, silt, clay; bangar (old, more kankar) and khadar (new, finer, more fertile); potash, phosphoric acid, lime; sugarcane, paddy, wheat.
  • Black (regur): Deccan trap, lava; clayey, holds moisture; rich in calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash, lime; poor in phosphoric contents; cracks; sticky; cotton.
  • Red and yellow: low rainfall, eastern and southern Deccan; iron → red; hydrated → yellow.
  • Laterite: 'later' = brick; intense leaching; acidic (pH < 6.0); tea, coffee, cashew.
  • Arid: red to brown; sandy, saline; lacks humus and moisture; kankar layer; cultivable with irrigation (western Rajasthan).
  • Forest: hills and mountains; loamy/silty in valleys, coarse on upper slopes; acidic, low humus in snowy Himalayas; fertile on river terraces.
