P26●●● AdvancedLong answer · 5 marksOriginal

"Mere availability of resources may even hinder development." Examine the statement with reference to your textbook.

Full solution

Asked: an examination — what the statement means, evidence, and the conclusion.

Concept used: Is having resources enough? (Topic 3, section 3); regional examples (Topic 3, section 1).

  1. 1

    Necessary but not enough: availability of resources is a necessary condition for development.

  2. 2

    What else is needed: without corresponding changes in technology and institutions, availability may hinder development.

  3. 3

    Regional evidence: Arunachal Pradesh has abundant water but lacks infrastructural development; many resource-rich regions are economically backward, while some resource-poor regions are developed.

  4. 4

    Historical evidence: rich resources attracted colonisers, whose higher technology let them exploit colonies and establish supremacy.

  5. 5

    Conclusion for India: development depends not only on resources but also on technology, the quality of human resources and the historical experiences of the people.

  6. Check: five separate points — the condition, the missing factors, a regional example, the colonial example, and the conclusion. None repeats another; together they "examine" the statement.

  7. Answer Availability is necessary but not sufficient; without technology and institutions, resource-rich regions can stay backward (Arunachal Pradesh) or be exploited (colonies); development also needs skilled people and depends on history.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Availability of resources is a necessary condition for development, but it is not enough by itself. (1)
    2. Without corresponding changes in technology and institutions, mere availability may even hinder development. (1)
    3. Arunachal Pradesh has abundant water but lacks infrastructure; many resource-rich regions remain economically backward. (1)
    4. Colonisers with higher technology exploited the rich resources of their colonies and established supremacy over them. (1)
    5. So in India, development depends also on technology, the quality of human resources and people's historical experiences. (1)
    Common trap: writing five examples of resource-rich states. "Examine" needs the reason (technology and institutions) as well as evidence.

P27●●● AdvancedLong answer · 5 marksOriginal

Compare black soil and laterite soil under five heads: (a) how each forms, (b) texture or reaction, (c) nutrients, (d) where each is found, (e) crops.

Full solution

Asked: five heads, each comparing both soils.

Concept used: Black and laterite soils (Topic 6).

  1. 1

    (a) Formation: black — climatic conditions with parent rock of the Deccan trap (lava flows); laterite — intense leaching due to heavy rain under tropical and subtropical climate with alternate wet and dry seasons.

  2. 2

    (b) Texture or reaction: black — extremely fine (clayey), holds moisture, sticky when wet; laterite — deep to very deep, acidic (pH < 6.0).

  3. 3

    (c) Nutrients: black — rich in calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime, poor in phosphoric contents; laterite — generally deficient in plant nutrients.

  4. 4

    (d) Where: black — plateaus of Maharashtra, Saurashtra, Malwa, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh; laterite — southern states, Western Ghats region of Maharashtra, Odisha, parts of West Bengal and the north-east.

  5. 5

    (e) Crops: black — cotton; laterite — tea and coffee after soil conservation, cashew nut (red laterite).

  6. Check: every head mentions both soils; an answer that describes black soil fully and laterite in one line does not compare.

  7. Answer Black: lava, clayey, rich except in phosphoric contents, Deccan trap, cotton. Laterite: leaching, acidic, nutrient-poor, southern states and the Western Ghats region of Maharashtra, tea, coffee, cashew.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Formation: black soil forms from lava parent rock of the Deccan trap; laterite from intense leaching due to heavy rain. (1)
    2. Texture: black soil is clayey and holds moisture; laterite is deep and acidic (pH below 6.0). (1)
    3. Nutrients: black soil is rich in calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime but poor in phosphoric contents; laterite is generally nutrient-deficient. (1)
    4. Where: black — Maharashtra, Malwa, Saurashtra; laterite — southern states, Western Ghats region of Maharashtra, Odisha. (1)
    5. Crops: black — cotton; laterite — tea and coffee after conservation, and cashew nut. (1)
    Common trap: writing five points about one soil. A comparison needs both soils in every point.

Meera's class studied the land records of their (imaginary) district, Sonagarh. Over thirty years, the share of land under houses, roads and a new factory estate went up from 5 to 11 per cent. The share of barren, rocky land fell from 10 to 6 per cent after some of it was levelled for building. Forest stayed at about 15 per cent. Net sown area stayed close to half of the district's land, but more and more fields were sown twice a year with canal water. Meera said, "Our district's forest is like India's — far from the target."

(Sonagarh and its numbers are imagined for practice; the India figures you need are from NCERT Fig. 1.4.)

(a) Name the land-use category to which "houses, roads and a new factory estate" belong. (1)
(b) If more fields are sown twice a year while net sown area stays the same, what happens to the gross cropped area? Why? (1)
(c) Is Meera right to compare her district's forest with India's? Use the national figure and target from your textbook. (2)

Full solution

Asked: the category; the effect on gross cropped area; a check of Meera's comparison.

Concept used: Land-use categories, gross cropped area, Fig. 1.4 and the forest target (Topic 4).

  1. 1

    (a) Land put to non-agricultural uses (part of "land not available for cultivation").

  2. 2

    (b) It rises, because gross cropped area = net sown area + area sown more than once; more double-sown fields add to it even if net sown area stays the same.

  3. 3

    (c) Yes: India's forest was 23.3 per cent in 2014-15, far lower than the desired 33 per cent of the National Forest Policy (1952). Sonagarh's 15 per cent is even further below that target, so both are far from it.

  4. Check: (c) uses the textbook's figures (23.3 and 33), not the imagined district's, for the national side; 15 < 23.3 < 33 ✔.

  5. Answer (a) Non-agricultural uses. (b) It rises, because double-sown land is added to net sown area. (c) Yes — India's forest (23.3 per cent) is also far below the 33 per cent target.

    Write it in the exam (4 marks):

    (a) Land put to non-agricultural uses. (1)
    (b) It rises, because gross cropped area adds the area sown more than once to the net sown area. (1)
    (c) Yes: India's forest was 23.3 per cent in 2014-15, far below the 33 per cent of the National Forest Policy (1952) (1); Sonagarh's 15 per cent is even further below that target. (1)

    Common trap: saying gross cropped area "stays the same because net sown area did not change". Double sowing adds to gross cropped area.

Three points are marked on an outline map of India. Identify the soil at each, giving one reason from your textbook:
(a) a point on the Malwa plateau in Madhya Pradesh;
(b) a point in the delta of the Mahanadi on the coast of Odisha;
(c) a point high in the Himalayan mountains of Uttarakhand.

Full solution

Asked: the soil at each of three points, with a reason each.

Concept used: Where each soil occurs (Topic 7).

  1. 1

    (a) Malwa plateau: black soil — black soils cover the plateaus of Maharashtra, Saurashtra, Malwa, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh.

  2. 2

    (b) Mahanadi delta: alluvial soil — alluvial soil is found in the deltas of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri.

  3. 3

    (c) Himalayas of Uttarakhand: forest soil — forest soils are found in the hilly and mountainous areas.

  4. Check: (b) is on the coast, not inland Odisha, where red and yellow soils occur; the delta clue makes it alluvial. Each point gives its own reason.

  5. Answer (a) Black soil (b) Alluvial soil (c) Forest soil

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. (a) Black soil — it covers the Malwa plateau, part of the Deccan trap region. (1)
    2. (b) Alluvial soil — it is found in the deltas of the eastern coast, including the Mahanadi. (1)
    3. (c) Forest soil — it is found in the hilly and mountainous areas of the Himalayas. (1)
    Common trap: writing "red and yellow" for (b) because Odisha has red and yellow soils. The delta on the coast is alluvial.

P30●●● AdvancedCase/source-based · 4 marksOriginal

Read the passage and answer the questions.

In the village of Pahadpur, high on a hillside, farmers plough their small fields straight down the slope. Each monsoon, rainwater rushes along the furrows and carries the soil to the stream below. Far to the west, in Retgaon, there are no hills at all — only open land at the edge of the desert, where the dry wind lifts loose soil and drops sand on the fields.

(Both villages are imagined for practice.)

(a) Why is soil lost in Pahadpur? (1)
(b) Suggest two methods for Pahadpur. (2)
(c) Suggest one method for Retgaon and say how it works. (1)

Full solution

Asked: the cause for Pahadpur, two methods for Pahadpur, one method for Retgaon.

Concept used: Soil erosion and conservation (Topic 8).

  1. 1

    (a) Ploughing up and down the slope forms channels for the quick flow of water, which carries the soil away.

  2. 2

    (b) Contour ploughing (along the contour lines, which slows water down the slope) and terrace cultivation (steps cut on the slope, which restrict erosion).

  3. 3

    (c) Shelter belts — lines of trees that break the force of the wind (or strip cropping — strips of grass between crops that break the wind).

  4. Check: (b) gives measures for water on slopes, (c) a measure for wind — each village gets the cure for its own problem. Shelter belts in Pahadpur or terraces in Retgaon would not fit.

  5. Answer (a) Ploughing down the slope makes channels for fast water. (b) Contour ploughing; terraces. (c) Shelter belts, which break the wind.

    Write it in the exam (4 marks):

    (a) Ploughing up and down the slope forms channels for the quick flow of water, which washes soil away. (1)
    (b) Contour ploughing slows the water down the slope (1); terraces cut on the slope restrict erosion. (1)
    (c) Shelter belts — rows of trees — break the force of the wind and hold the sand. (1)

    Common trap: answering (b) with "stop ploughing". Farmers must plough; they should plough along the contours.
