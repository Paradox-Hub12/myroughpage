Topic 1 of 8

What is a resource?

About 35 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Define a resource with its three conditions and explain the relationship between nature, technology and institutions (NCERT Fig. 1.1).
  • Name the four bases of classifying resources and read the classification chart (NCERT Fig. 1.2).

Look around your classroom. The desk was a tree. The fan is copper and steel that once lay under the ground. The light comes from coal, water or the sun. A tree, a lump of ore or a flowing river becomes useful to us only when people know how to use it, can afford to use it, and agree to use it. That is what turns a thing into a resource.

  1. The textbook's definition

Everything available in our environment which can be used to satisfy our needs, provided, it is technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable can be termed as 'Resource'.

So a thing must pass three tests before it is a resource:

Test Question to ask If the answer is "no"…
T — Technologically accessible Do we have the tools and know-how to get it and use it? it stays out of our reach
E — Economically feasible Is it worth the cost of getting and using it? it costs more than it gives
C — Culturally acceptable Will people agree to use it in this way? people refuse to use it

Memory hook: T-E-C. A thing becomes a resource only when it passes T, E and C — all three.

  1. Nature, technology and institutions (NCERT Fig. 1.1)

NCERT Fig. 1.1 is a triangle. At its three corners are Physical Environment (Nature), Technology and Institutions; Human Beings stand in the middle, with arrows linking them to all three.

What it means, in the textbook's words:

  • The process of turning things in our environment into resources involves an interactive relationship between nature, technology and institutions.
  • Human beings interact with nature through technology and create institutions to accelerate their economic development.

  1. Are resources free gifts of nature?

Your textbook asks: "Do you think that resources are free gifts of nature as is assumed by many?" Its answer is "They are not."

  • Resources are a function of human activities.
  • Human beings themselves are essential components of resources.
  • People transform material available in the environment into resources and use them.

Example: iron ore lying in a hill is only rock until people find it, mine it, and make steel from it.

  1. Four ways to classify resources

Your textbook gives four bases. Learn them exactly as printed:

Basis Classes (as printed in your textbook)
(a) Origin biotic and abiotic
(b) Exhaustibility renewable and non-renewable
(c) Ownership individual, community, national and international
(d) Status of development potential, developed stock and reserves

Memory hook: O-E-O-S — Origin, Exhaustibility, Ownership, Status of development.

Note on the textbook's wording. The textbook prints "(d) … potential, developed stock and reserves". Read as printed, this looks like three classes. The CBSE Sample Question Paper 2026-27 (Q10) lists "Developed resource", "Potential resource" and "Reserve" as separate options, so you may meet the names in that form too.

Word hints (not textbook definitions). Your textbook names these classes but does not explain them. These hints come from the words themselves and help you sort examples; do not write them as textbook definitions:

  • Biotic — "bio" means life: things that are or were living (forests, wildlife). Abiotic — "a-" means not: non-living things (metals, rocks).
  • Renewable — comes back through nature's flows or growth if used with care (wind, water, forests). Non-renewable — a stock that gets used up and is not replaced (metals, fossil fuels).
  • Individual · community · national · international — who owns it: one person, a whole village or town, the country, or the world together.
  • Potential — the CBSE Sample Question Paper 2026-27 uses this word for a resource that is known but cannot be used now because the technology is lacking. Learn it in that sense for the exam.

  1. Reading NCERT Fig. 1.2 (Classification of resources)

NCERT Fig. 1.2 is a tree chart:

Branch Splits into Textbook's examples
Natural → Renewable Continuous or flow · Biological wind, water · natural vegetation (forests), wildlife
Natural → Non-renewable Recyclable · Non-recyclable metals · fossil fuels
Human Structures and institutions · Quantity and quality —

Read it this way: metals are non-renewable but recyclable; fossil fuels are non-renewable and cannot be recycled. Human resources are counted by quantity and quality, and by the structures and institutions people build.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Passing the three tests

Question. Villagers pipe clean water from a mountain spring down to their homes. Near the same village is a grove of old trees that the villagers treat as sacred and never cut. Using the three conditions in your textbook, explain why the spring water is used as a resource but the grove is not cut for timber. (2 marks)

Asked: apply the T-E-C test to the spring water and to the grove's timber.

Concept used: The definition of a resource (this topic, section 1).

  1. 1

    Spring water: the villagers can reach it with pipes (technologically accessible), it is worth the cost (economically feasible) and people are happy to use it (culturally acceptable) → it is a resource.

  2. 2

    Timber from the grove: cutting the trees is possible and could pay, but the community will not accept it → it fails cultural acceptability, so the grove is not used for timber.

  3. Check: each point names which test is passed or failed. If you said the timber fails because "trees are not useful", the check fails — trees are useful; the reason here is cultural.

  4. Answer The spring water passes all three tests and is a resource; the grove's timber fails the "culturally acceptable" test, so it is not used.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The spring water is technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable, so it is a resource. (1)
    2. Timber from the sacred grove is not culturally acceptable to the villagers, so it is not used. (1)
    Common trap: writing only "it is useful". Usefulness alone does not make a resource — name the condition.

Worked example 2

Sort by the right basis

Question. Sort these into the correct classes, and name the basis each time: (i) wind and metals — by how long they last; (ii) a village pond and the oceans beyond any country's waters — by who owns them; (iii) forests and rocks — by origin.

Asked: the basis and the class for each pair.

Concept used: The four bases and NCERT Fig. 1.2 (this topic, sections 4–5).

  1. 1

    (i) Exhaustibility: wind is renewable (Fig. 1.2: continuous or flow); metals are non-renewable (Fig. 1.2: recyclable).

  2. 2

    (ii) Ownership: a village pond used by all villagers is a community resource; oceans beyond any country's waters belong to no single nation → international (word hint: owned by the world together).

  3. 3

    (iii) Origin: forests are living → biotic; rocks are non-living → abiotic (word hint: "bio" = life).

  4. Check: every pair is sorted by one basis only. If "renewable" appears in (iii), you have mixed origin with exhaustibility.

  5. Answer (i) wind renewable, metals non-renewable — exhaustibility; (ii) pond community, open oceans international — ownership; (iii) forests biotic, rocks abiotic — origin.

    Common trap: calling metals "renewable" because they can be recycled. In Fig. 1.2, metals are non-renewable — recyclable is a sub-group of non-renewable.

Common mistakes!

  • "Resources are free gifts of nature."

    Your textbook says they are not: resources are a function of human activities, and humans are essential components of resources.

    Write: resources become resources through human activity, technology and institutions.

  • "Renewable and non-renewable is classification by origin."

    Origin gives biotic and abiotic; renewable/non-renewable is by exhaustibility.

    Learn O-E-O-S and the classes under each basis.

  • Defining a resource with only one or two of the conditions.

    The definition needs all three: technologically accessible, economically feasible, culturally acceptable.

    Write T-E-C in full.

  • "Metals are renewable because they can be recycled."

    Fig. 1.2 puts metals under non-renewable → recyclable.

    Recyclable ≠ renewable.

Key points

  • A resource is anything in our environment that can satisfy our needs, provided it is technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable (T-E-C).
  • NCERT Fig. 1.1: an interactive relationship between nature, technology and institutions, with human beings at the centre.
  • Resources are not free gifts of nature; they are a function of human activities, and humans are essential components of resources.
  • Four bases (O-E-O-S): origin (biotic, abiotic); exhaustibility (renewable, non-renewable); ownership (individual, community, national, international); status of development (potential, developed stock and reserves).
  • NCERT Fig. 1.2: natural renewable (flow: wind, water; biological: forests, wildlife), natural non-renewable (recyclable: metals; non-recyclable: fossil fuels), human (structures and institutions; quantity and quality).
