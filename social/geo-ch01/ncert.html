About the question text

Each NCERT question is shown in full above its solution, with its textbook location. The solutions are our own.

How every solution is laid out: Asked → Concept used → Understand it (the reasoning, one step per line) → Check → ✅ Final answer → Write it in the exam (numbered points sized to the marks, with the mark for each point) → Common trap. In the board exam, answers must stay within the word limits: 2 marks — up to 40 words, 3 marks — up to 60 words, 5 marks — up to 120 words.

Marks shown in "Write it in the exam" are our estimate of how a board question of this kind is usually marked; NCERT does not give marks. Where a question asks for your own observation or opinion, the answer is marked "One possible answer".

Box p. 1Items that make life comfortable2 marks

Can you identify and name the various items used in making life comfortable in our villages and towns. List the items and name the material used in their making.

Asked: everyday items and the materials they are made of.

Concept used: What a resource is (Topic 1): materials from the environment become useful through human activity.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Look around your home and street. List things you use every day.

  2. 2

    For each, name the material and where it comes from — a tree, a mine, a field, a river.

  3. 3

    Notice the pattern: every material came from the environment and was transformed by people using technology.

  4. Check: each item should have a material, not just a use. "Fan — for air" names a use; "fan — metal and plastic" names the materials.

  5. Answer One possible answer: wooden door and furniture (wood); steel bucket, fan and utensils (metals); clay pots and bricks (soil); cotton clothes (cotton grown on soil); a cement house (limestone); a plastic chair (made from fossil fuels).

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. One possible answer: furniture and doors — wood; buckets, fans and utensils — metals; bricks and pots — clay. (1)
    2. Clothes — cotton; houses — cement from limestone. All come from our environment, transformed by human activity. (1)
    Common trap: listing only machines. Include simple things — a pot, a cot, a brick — and always name the material.
Question p. 1Are resources free gifts of nature?3 marks

Do you think that resources are free gifts of nature as is assumed by many?

Asked: whether resources are free gifts of nature, with reasons.

Concept used: Topic 1, section 3.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    The textbook's answer: "They are not."

  2. 2

    Reason 1: resources are a function of human activities — people transform materials in the environment into resources.

  3. 3

    Reason 2: human beings themselves are essential components of resources.

  4. 4

    Reason 3: a thing is a resource only when it is technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable — conditions that depend on people.

  5. Check: the answer must say no and give reasons; "yes, nature gives everything free" contradicts the textbook.

  6. Answer No. Resources are a function of human activities; humans are essential components of resources, and things become resources only when they meet the T-E-C conditions.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. No. Resources are not free gifts of nature; they are a function of human activities. (1)
    2. Human beings transform materials in the environment into resources and are themselves essential components of resources. (1)
    3. A thing becomes a resource only when it is technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable. (1)
    Common trap: writing "yes, because nature gives them to us". The textbook's answer is no.
Activity 1, p. 2If the oil supply runs out3 marks

Imagine, if the oil supply gets exhausted one day, how would this affect our life style?

Asked: your own reasoned picture of life without oil.

Concept used: Non-renewable resources (Topic 1, Fig. 1.2: fossil fuels are non-renewable and non-recyclable); depletion (Topic 2).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Why it can run out: oil is a fossil fuel — non-renewable and non-recyclable (Fig. 1.2).

  2. 2

    Think of everyday uses: vehicles, buses, tractors and pumps that run on petrol or diesel; things made from oil.

  3. 3

    Think of the effects: travel and transport of goods would become hard and costly; farming and factories that use such machines would slow down; we would have to depend on other sources of energy.

  4. Check: this is an "imagine" question — your answer is an opinion, so present it as one possible answer and keep the reasoning clear.

  5. Answer One possible answer: since oil is non-renewable, its exhaustion would make transport, farming and industry that depend on it difficult and costly, and we would have to change how we travel and use energy.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. One possible answer: oil is a non-renewable, non-recyclable fossil fuel, so once used up it cannot be replaced. (1)
    2. Vehicles, tractors and pumps running on petrol or diesel would stop; travel and transport of goods would become difficult and costly. (1)
    3. We would have to save energy and depend on other sources, changing our daily life. (1)
    Common trap: saying "nothing will change because oil is renewable". Fig. 1.2 shows fossil fuels as non-renewable.
Activity 2, p. 2A survey on recycling3 marks

Plan a survey in your colony/village to investigate people's attitude towards recycling of the domestic/agricultural wastes. Ask questions about : (a) What do they think about resources they use? (b) What is their opinion about the wastes, and its utilisation?

Asked: a plan for a survey (a classroom activity, not a board question).

  2. 2

  3. 3

    Record the answers in a table and count how many people already reuse or recycle.

  4. 4

    Conclude with two or three findings and one suggestion.

  5. Check: the questions must cover both parts — resources (a) and wastes (b).

  6. Answer A guided plan: sample households, questions for parts (a) and (b), a results table, and short conclusions with a suggestion.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Not asked in the board exam. Select 10–15 households and ask which resources they use and whether these can run out. (1)

Prepare a list of resources found in your state and also identify the resources that are important but deficit in your state.

Asked: what your state has and what it lacks.

    Link to planning: these differences are why planning is needed at national, state, regional and local levels.

  4. Check: the list must have two parts — "found" and "important but deficit".

    Common trap: listing only what the state has. The activity also asks what is deficit.
Box p. 3Resources developed by communities2 marks

What resources are being developed in your surroundings by the community/village panchayats/ward level communities with the help of community participation?

Asked: community-developed resources near you.

Concept used: Ownership — community resources (Topic 1); local planning (Topic 2, Agenda 21; Topic 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Look for shared things your panchayat or ward has built or looks after with people's help: a village pond, a well or water tank, a playground, a park, a community hall, a grazing ground.

  2. 2

    Why they count: they are used by all members of the community — community-owned resources.

  3. Check: name things that are shared and developed by the community, not by one family.

  4. Answer One possible answer: a village pond or water tank, a community park or playground, and a common grazing ground, developed and looked after by the panchayat or ward with people's participation.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. One possible answer: our panchayat, with villagers' help, has developed a village pond and a water tank. (1)
    2. A playground and a common grazing ground are also maintained; all are community-owned resources. (1)
    Common trap: naming private property, such as a family's farm. That is an individual resource.
Question p. 3Rich but backward, poor but developed3 marks

Can you name some resource rich but economically backward regions and some resource poor but economically developed regions? Give reasons for such a situation.

Asked: examples of both kinds and the reasons.

Concept used: Is having resources enough? (Topic 3, section 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    The textbook's rule: availability is necessary, but without matching technology and institutions it may hinder development; development also depends on the quality of human resources and historical experiences.

  2. 2

    A resource-rich region held back: the textbook gives Arunachal Pradesh — abundant water, but it lacks infrastructural development.

  3. 3

    History: colonies were rich in resources, yet colonisers with higher technology exploited them.

  4. 4

    Resource-poor but developed regions: your textbook names none. One possible view: a region with few natural resources can still develop if it has good technology, skilled people and strong institutions. Give examples your teacher agrees with.

  5. Check: the reasons must come from the textbook (technology, institutions, human resources, history); do not call a named state "backward" or "developed" as a textbook fact.

  6. Answer One possible answer: Arunachal Pradesh has abundant water but lacks infrastructure; colonies were resource-rich but exploited. The reasons are differences in technology, institutions, quality of human resources and historical experiences.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. One possible answer: Arunachal Pradesh has abundant water but lacks infrastructure, so its resources are not fully developed. (1)
    2. Resources lead to development only with appropriate technology and institutions; colonies were resource-rich but exploited by technologically advanced countries. (1)
    3. Regions with few resources can develop through technology, skilled people and good institutions. (1)
    Common trap: saying a region is poor "because it has no resources". The textbook stresses technology and institutions, not resources alone.
Activity p. 5Comparing the two pie charts3 marks

Try to do a comparison between the two pie charts (Fig. 1.4) given for land use and find out why the net sown area and the land under forests have changed from 1960-61 to 2014-15 very marginally.

Asked: the changes in net sown area and forest, and reasons for the small change.

Concept used: Reading Fig. 1.4 and land-use factors (Topic 4).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Net sown area: 45.26 → 45.5 per cent — almost no change.

  2. 2

    Forest: 18.11 → 23.3 per cent — a rise of about 5 points, but still far below the 33 per cent target.

  3. 3

    Why net sown area could not grow much (one possible answer, built from the textbook's facts): much of the remaining land is waste land (rocky, arid, desert), or fallow land of poor quality or high cost of cultivation; land under non-agricultural uses (settlements, roads, railways, industry) grew from 4.95 to 8.7 per cent.

  4. 4

    Why forest could not grow much: land is needed for farming, settlements and industry; land use depends on population density, technology and culture and traditions; mining has caused deforestation in some states.

  5. Check: quote both pairs of numbers. Note that the textbook calls the forest change "very marginal", although forest rose by about 5 points; it remained far below the 33 per cent target.

  6. Answer Net sown area 45.26 → 45.5 and forest 18.11 → 23.3 per cent. One possible answer for why: remaining land is poor or costly to farm, more land went to non-agricultural uses, and demand for land kept forest far below 33 per cent.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Net sown area changed from 45.26 to 45.5 per cent and forest from 18.11 to 23.3 per cent. (1)
    2. One possible answer: remaining waste and fallow land is poor or costly to cultivate, so net sown area could hardly grow. (1)
    3. Settlements, roads and industry took more land (4.95 to 8.7 per cent), leaving forest far below 33 per cent. (1)
    Common trap: saying "forest has not changed at all". It rose from 18.11 to 23.3 per cent — but stays far below the target.
Question p. 5Feeding cattle on less pasture3 marks

The land under permanent pasture has also decreased. How are we able to feed our huge cattle population on this pasture land and what are the consequences of it?

Asked: how cattle are fed with less pasture, and the consequences.

Concept used: Fig. 1.4 (pasture 4.71 → 3.3 per cent); overgrazing as a cause of land degradation (Topic 5).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    The fact: permanent pasture and grazing land fell from 4.71 to 3.3 per cent (Fig. 1.4).

  2. 2

    How cattle are fed (one possible answer): the same small pastures are grazed again and again; cattle also graze on fallow land and in forests, and are fed fodder and crop waste from farms.

  3. 3

    The consequence (your textbook): overgrazing is one of the main reasons for land degradation — in Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra; over-grazing also upsets the balance of soil formation and erosion.

  4. Check: the consequence must come from the textbook (overgrazing → land degradation, soil erosion); the feeding methods are "one possible answer", since the textbook does not list them.

  5. Answer One possible answer: cattle are fed by grazing the shrinking pastures heavily, using fallow and forest land and farm fodder; the consequence is overgrazing, which degrades land and causes soil erosion.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Permanent pasture fell from 4.71 to 3.3 per cent, so less grazing land remains. (1)
    2. One possible answer: cattle overgraze the remaining pastures and fallow land, and are fed fodder and crop waste. (1)
    3. Overgrazing degrades land, as in Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra, and causes soil erosion. (1)
    Common trap: writing only how cattle are fed. The question also asks for the consequences.
Box p. 5Low net sown area in some states3 marks

Find out reasons for the low proportion of net sown area in these states.

(The states are those named just before the box: Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Manipur and Andaman Nicobar Islands, where net sown area is less than 10 per cent of the total area.)

Asked: reasons for net sown area below 10 per cent.

Concept used: Land use is decided by physical and human factors (Topic 4, section 4); incomplete reporting (section 6).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Physical factors: the textbook says land use depends on topography, climate and soil types. These states are hilly or forested islands, where flat land for crops is limited (one possible answer, using the textbook's factors).

  2. 2

    Human factors: population density, technological capability, culture and traditions also decide how much land is sown.

  3. 3

    Reporting: land-use reporting for most north-east states except Assam has not been done fully, so the figures are incomplete.

  4. Check: the reasons should use the textbook's factors; any extra detail you add is your reasoning, so label it "one possible answer".

  5. Answer One possible answer: hilly topography, forests and climate leave little flat land for cultivation, human factors such as population density and traditions differ, and land-use reporting in most north-east states is incomplete.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. One possible answer: hilly topography, dense forests and climate leave little flat land for crops. (1)
    2. Human factors — population density, technological capability, culture and traditions — also limit the sown area. (1)
    3. Land-use reporting for most north-east states except Assam is incomplete. (1)
    Common trap: saying "these states have no soil". Use the textbook's factors.

Exercise 2 (ii)Soil of the eastern deltas3 marks

What type of soil is found in the river deltas of the eastern coast? Give three main features of this type of soil.

Asked: the soil type and three features.

Concept used: Alluvial soils (Topic 6).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Soil: alluvial soil is found in the deltas of the Mahanadi, the Godavari, the Krishna and the Kaveri.

  2. 2

    Feature 1 — composition: various proportions of sand, silt and clay.

  3. 3

Exercise 2 (iii)Controlling erosion in hilly areas3 marks

What steps can be taken to control soil erosion in the hilly areas?

Asked: steps for hill slopes.

Concept used: Soil conservation (Topic 8); causes of erosion (Topic 8, section 1).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Contour ploughing: ploughing along the contour lines decelerates the flow of water down the slopes (instead of ploughing up and down the slope).

  2. 2

    Terrace cultivation: steps cut on the slopes make terraces, which restrict erosion — well developed in the western and central Himalayas.

  3. 3

    Protect the cover: erosion speeds up with deforestation and over-grazing, so afforestation and proper management of grazing help hold the soil (the textbook's measures against land degradation).

  4. Check: all three steps fit slopes and running water. Strip cropping and shelter belts break the force of wind, the problem of flat, dry land.

  5. Answer Contour ploughing, terrace cultivation, and afforestation with controlled grazing.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Contour ploughing along the contour lines slows the flow of water down the slopes. (1)
    2. Terraces cut as steps on slopes restrict erosion, as in the western and central Himalayas. (1)
    3. Afforestation and proper management of grazing protect the soil cover on slopes. (1)
    Common trap: writing "shelter belts". They break the wind, mainly in dry areas such as western India.

Exercise 3 — Answer in about 120 words

Exercise 3 (i)Land-use pattern and forest land5 marks

Explain land use pattern in India and why has the land under forest not increased much since 1960-61?

Asked: two parts — (a) the land-use pattern; (b) why forest land has not increased much.

Concept used: Land use (Topic 4); land degradation (Topic 5).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    (a) What decides land use: physical factors (topography, climate, soil types) and human factors (population density, technological capability, culture and traditions).

  2. 2

    (a) The pattern in 2014-15 (Fig. 1.4): net sown area 45.5 per cent, forest 23.3, non-agricultural uses 8.7, barren land 5.5, current fallow 4.9; net sown area is over 80 per cent in Punjab and Haryana but under 10 per cent in Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Manipur and Andaman Nicobar.

  3. 3

    (b) The forest figures: forest rose only from 18.11 (1960-61) to 23.3 per cent — far lower than the desired 33 per cent of the National Forest Policy (1952).

  4. 4

    (b) Why: land is finite, and people's needs for farming, settlements, roads and industry compete with forests — non-agricultural uses rose from 4.95 to 8.7 per cent and net sown area stayed at about 45 per cent.

  5. 5

    (b) Why, continued: deforestation due to mining in Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha removes forest cover, so forest grows slowly.

  6. Check: both parts are answered — points 1–2 describe the pattern; points 3–5 explain the slow growth of forest, with the textbook's figures. The textbook gives these facts; joining them into reasons is your explanation.

  7. Answer Land use depends on physical and human factors; in 2014-15 about 45.5 per cent was net sown area and 23.3 per cent forest. Forest rose only from 18.11 to 23.3 per cent — far below 33 per cent — because farming, settlements and industry compete for limited land, and mining removes forest cover.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Land use depends on physical factors (topography, climate, soil) and human factors (population density, technology, culture and traditions). (1)
    2. In 2014-15, net sown area was 45.5 per cent and forest 23.3 per cent; net sown area exceeds 80 per cent in Punjab and Haryana. (1)
    3. Forest grew only from 18.11 to 23.3 per cent, far below the 33 per cent of the National Forest Policy (1952). (1)
    4. Land is limited and is needed for farming, settlements, roads and industry; non-agricultural uses rose from 4.95 to 8.7 per cent. (1)
    5. Deforestation due to mining in Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha removes forest cover, so forest grows slowly. (1)

    Note on the textbook's figures. NCERT's Activity on p. 5 calls the change in forest land "very marginal", although Fig. 1.4 shows a rise of about 5 percentage points. The answer above gives the actual figures and stresses that forest is still far below the 33 per cent target.

    Common trap: answering only one part. The question has two: the pattern and the reasons for slow forest growth.
Exercise 3 (ii)Development and the consumption of resources5 marks

How have technical and economic development led to more consumption of resources?

Asked: how technology and economic growth raised the use of resources.

Concept used: Nature–technology–institutions (Topic 1); misuse (Topic 2); colonisation and Gandhiji (Topic 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Technology turns more things into resources: humans interact with nature through technology and create institutions to accelerate economic development (Fig. 1.1) — the more technology, the more materials become usable.

  2. 2

    Colonial exploitation: the higher level of technological development of colonising countries helped them exploit the resources of colonies.

  3. 3

    Mass production: Gandhiji blamed the exploitative nature of modern technology and greedy individuals; he opposed mass production, which uses resources on a huge scale.

  4. 4

    Indiscriminate use: treating resources as free gifts led to depletion for the greed of a few and accumulation in few hands.

  5. 5

    Global effects: over-utilisation has caused global warming, ozone layer depletion, environmental pollution and land degradation.

  6. Check: every point links technology or economic growth to more use of resources; a list of crises without that link would not answer "how".

  7. Answer Technology made more materials usable and sped up development; it let colonisers exploit colonies and enabled mass production; resources were used indiscriminately, leading to depletion, accumulation in few hands and global ecological crises.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Humans interact with nature through technology to speed up economic development, so more materials become usable resources. (1)
    2. Colonising countries used their higher technology to exploit the rich resources of their colonies. (1)
    3. Modern technology made mass production possible; Gandhiji called it exploitative and a root cause of resource depletion. (1)
    4. Treating resources as free gifts led to indiscriminate use, depletion for a few people's greed and accumulation in few hands. (1)
    5. Over-utilisation caused global warming, ozone layer depletion, pollution and land degradation. (1)
    Common trap: only listing the problems. Show how technology and economic growth increased consumption.
