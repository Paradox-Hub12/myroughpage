Topic 2 of 7

Causes of scarcity and the need to conserve

About 35 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain the causes of water scarcity your textbook gives — population, irrigation, industrialisation, urbanisation and pollution.
  • Explain why we must conserve water, and describe the Jal Jeevan Mission and the Atal Bhujal Yojana.

Count the taps, pumps and pipes you pass on your way to school — in homes, shops, fields and factories. Every one of them draws on the same rivers and the same water underground. This topic follows the water to its biggest users and asks what happens when everyone takes a little more.

Quick reminder (Chapter 1)

A renewable resource can be renewed or replenished, but it can still be used up faster than it is renewed. Conservation means using a resource carefully so that it lasts for future generations.

  1. More people, more demand

  • A large and growing population creates greater demands for water — and access to it is unequal.
  • A large population needs more water not only for domestic use (drinking, cooking, washing) but also to produce more food.
  • To grow more food grain, water is over-exploited to expand irrigated areas for dry-season agriculture.
  • Irrigated agriculture is the largest consumer of water.
  • Your textbook's answer: we need to revolutionise agriculture by developing drought resistant crops and dry farming techniques.

  1. Wells and tube-wells on farms

Many farmers have their own wells and tube-wells to irrigate and increase their produce. The textbook warns that this may lead to falling groundwater levels, adversely affecting water availability and food security of the people.

  1. Industries

After Independence, India saw intensive industrialisation and urbanisation. Large industrial houses and the units of many MNCs (Multinational Corporations) are now common. The ever-increasing number of industries puts pressure on existing freshwater resources in two ways:

  1. industries are heavy users of water;
  2. they need power to run, and much of this energy comes from hydroelectric power — which also depends on water.

  1. Cities

Multiplying urban centres with large and dense populations and urban lifestyles have added to water and energy requirements. In cities, most housing societies or colonies have their own groundwater pumping devices. So fragile water resources are being over-exploited, and this has caused their depletion in several cities.

  1. Quality: polluted water

Even where there is ample water, much of it may be polluted by domestic and industrial wastes, chemicals, pesticides and fertilisers used in agriculture, making it hazardous for human use (Topic 1).

  1. Cause → effect at a glance

Cause (your textbook) What it does to water
Large and growing population More water needed for homes and for food
Irrigation for dry-season agriculture Water over-exploited; irrigated agriculture is the largest consumer
Own wells and tube-wells on farms May lead to falling groundwater levels; harms water availability and food security
More industries Heavy use of water; power needs, much of it hydroelectric
Growing cities and urban lifestyles More water and energy needed; colonies pump their own groundwater → depletion
Domestic and industrial wastes, chemicals, pesticides, fertilisers Water polluted and hazardous for human use

  1. Why conserve — the need of the hour

Your textbook says we must conserve and manage our water resources to:

  • safeguard ourselves from health hazards,
  • ensure food security,
  • ensure the continuation of our livelihoods and productive activities, and
  • prevent degradation of our natural ecosystems.

Over-exploitation and mismanagement of water will impoverish this resource and cause an ecological crisis that may have a profound impact on our lives.

  1. Two government programmes in this topic

Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM) Atal Bhujal Yojana (Atal Jal)
About Safe drinking water at home Groundwater in water-stressed areas
Who Every rural household 8220 water-stressed Gram Panchayats of 229 blocks/talukas in 80 districts of seven states: Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh
Aim (textbook's words in bold) Assured supply of potable piped water at 55 litres per capita per day, regularly and long-term, by ensuring functionality of the tap water connections One of its key aspects: behavioural changes in the community — from consumption to conservation and smart water management
Why To improve the quality of life and ease of living, especially in rural areas The selected states hold about 37 per cent of India's water-stressed (over-exploited, critical and semi-critical) blocks

A third programme, the Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchaee Yojana, is about irrigation on farms — you meet it in Topic 4.

Memory hook: "JJM taps, Atal Jal digs, PMKSY irrigates" — JJM brings piped drinking water to homes; Atal Jal looks after groundwater; PMKSY brings water to fields.

Worked example 1

Who uses the most?

Question. In an imagined village, Sonkheda, a survey divided the water used in a year into 800 units: farms 560 units, homes 90 units and a sugar factory 150 units. (a) Which user takes the most, and what share is that? (b) Does this agree with your textbook?

  1. (Sonkheda and its numbers are imagined for practice; the conclusion is from your textbook.)

    Asked: the largest user and its share; a comparison with the textbook.

    Concept used: More people, more demand (section 1).

  2. 1

    Add the parts: 560 + 90 + 150 = 800 units ✔ (the total given).

  3. 2

    Largest user: farms, with 560 units.

  4. 3

    Share: 560 ÷ 800 = 0.7, so farms use 70 per cent of the water.

  5. 4

    Compare: your textbook says irrigated agriculture is the largest consumer of water — Sonkheda agrees.

  6. Check: the other two together are 90 + 150 = 240 units, which is 30 per cent; 70 + 30 = 100 ✔.

  7. Answer (a) Farms, 70 per cent; (b) yes — irrigated agriculture is the largest consumer.

    Common trap: naming the factory because it looks "big". Read the numbers: the farms use far more.

Worked example 2

Which programme is it?

Question. Name the programme in each description: (a) "Every rural home should get safe drinking water through a working tap." (b) "Villages in water-stressed areas should learn to save groundwater instead of only using it." (c) "Every field should get water, and each drop should grow more crop."

Asked: three programme names.

Concept used: The programmes table (section 8).

  1. 1

    (a) Piped drinking water for every rural household → Jal Jeevan Mission.

  2. 2

    (b) Groundwater; from consumption to conservation → Atal Bhujal Yojana (Atal Jal).

  3. 3

    (c) "Har khet ko pani" and "per drop more crop" → Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchaee Yojana.

  4. Check: use the hook — taps (JJM), digging into groundwater (Atal Jal), irrigating fields (PMKSY). Each description fits one only.

  5. Answer (a) Jal Jeevan Mission; (b) Atal Bhujal Yojana; (c) Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchaee Yojana.

    Common trap: writing JJM for (c) because both are about water. JJM is for drinking water in homes, not fields.

Worked example 3

Factories and colonies

Question. "Industries and growing cities have added to India's water problem." Explain the statement in three points, using your textbook. (3 marks)

Asked: three ways industries and cities increase water scarcity.

Concept used: Industries; cities (sections 3 and 4).

  1. 1

    Industries use water: they are heavy users of water.

  2. 2

    Industries use power: they need power to run, and much of it comes from hydroelectric power.

  3. 3

    Cities pump groundwater: most housing societies have their own groundwater pumping devices, so groundwater is over-exploited and depleted.

  4. Check: two points on industries and one on cities, and each is a different way water is used. "Factories pollute rivers" would be a fourth idea, but the question asks how they add to the problem of supply; three different points are enough for 3 marks.

  5. Answer Industries use much water and hydroelectric power; urban colonies pump their own groundwater.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. The growing number of industries are heavy users of water, putting pressure on freshwater resources. (1)
    2. Industries need power, and much of it comes from hydroelectric power, which also depends on water. (1)
    3. In cities, most housing societies pump their own groundwater, so it is over-exploited and depleted. (1)
    Common trap: writing only "cities have more people". Say what the people and colonies do to water: they pump and use more of it.

Common mistakes!

  • "Industry is the largest consumer of water."

    Your textbook says irrigated agriculture is the largest consumer. Industries are heavy users and also need hydroelectric power.

    Write "irrigated agriculture" for the largest consumer; give industry's two pressures separately.

  • "Wells and tube-wells solve scarcity on farms."

    They may lead to falling groundwater levels, adversely affecting water availability and food security.

    Say what over-pumping does to groundwater.

  • "Cities only add people."

    Urban lifestyles add to water and energy needs, and colonies run their own groundwater pumps → depletion.

    Name what city colonies do: pump groundwater.

  • Writing causes ("population", "pollution") when the question asks how to conserve water.

    A cause is not a cure.

    For measures, write cures: drought resistant crops and dry farming, rainwater harvesting, saving and reusing water, community conservation.

  • JJM, Atal Jal and PMKSY mixed up.

    JJM = piped drinking water for every rural household (55 litres per capita per day); Atal Jal = groundwater, behavioural change, seven states; PMKSY = irrigation ("har khet ko pani", "per drop more crop").

    Use the hook: JJM taps, Atal Jal digs, PMKSY irrigates.

Key points

  • A growing population needs water for homes and food; water is over-exploited to irrigate dry-season crops — irrigated agriculture is the largest consumer. The answer: drought resistant crops and dry farming.
  • Farm wells and tube-wells may cause falling groundwater levels, hurting water availability and food security.
  • Industries are heavy users of water and need power, much of it hydroelectric; city colonies pump their own groundwater, causing depletion.
  • We must conserve water to avoid health hazards, ensure food security, keep livelihoods going and protect ecosystems.
  • JJM: potable piped water, 55 litres per capita per day, every rural household. Atal Jal: groundwater in 8220 water-stressed Gram Panchayats of seven states; from consumption to conservation.
