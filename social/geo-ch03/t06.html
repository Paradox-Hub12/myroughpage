Topic 6 of 7

Modern adaptations of rainwater harvesting

About 35 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe modern adaptations of rainwater harvesting — Gendathur, Shillong, Tamil Nadu and rooftop recharge of groundwater (NCERT Fig. 3.3).
  • Explain the bamboo drip irrigation system of Meghalaya.

A town that sits next to some of the wettest places on earth runs short of water. A village in the south becomes famous for being "rich in rainwater". A whole state makes catching rain on rooftops the law. Old ideas, it turns out, can solve new problems. This topic shows how.

  1. An old idea, adapted

Your textbook says that in many parts of rural and urban India, rooftop rainwater harvesting is being successfully adapted to store and conserve water. Here are its examples.

Example Where What the textbook says
Gendathur A remote backward village in Mysuru, Karnataka Villagers installed rooftop rainwater harvesting to meet their water needs; nearly 200 households have it; the village has earned the rare distinction of being rich in rainwater
Shillong Meghalaya (the state capital) Rooftop harvesting is the most common practice; nearly every household has a structure; it gives 15–25 per cent of a household's total water need
Tamil Nadu The whole state The first state in India to make rooftop rainwater harvesting structures compulsory for all houses, with legal provisions to punish defaulters

  1. Gendathur's numbers

  • Gendathur receives about 1,000 mm of rain a year.
  • With 80 per cent collection efficiency and about 10 fillings, every house can collect and use about 50,000 litres of water a year.
  • The textbook then says: "From the 200 houses, the net amount of rainwater harvested annually amounts to 1,00,000 litres."

Note on the textbook's numbers. 200 houses × 50,000 litres = 1,00,00,000 litres (one crore), which is a hundred times the printed total of 1,00,000 litres. If you quote Gendathur in the exam, quote the 50,000 litres per house, which your textbook states clearly.

  1. Shillong's puzzle

Cherapunjee and Mawsynram, only 55 km from Shillong, receive the highest rainfall in the world — yet Shillong, the state capital, faces acute shortage of water. Rooftop harvesting helps fill the gap. This is the lesson of Topic 1 in one town: plenty of rain nearby does not mean enough water at home.

  1. Rooftop rainwater that recharges groundwater (NCERT Fig. 3.3)

NCERT Fig. 3.3 ("Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting") draws two houses: one recharges groundwater through a hand pump, the other through an abandoned dugwell. Its six steps:

  1. Rooftop rainwater is collected using a PVC pipe.
  2. It is filtered using sand and bricks.
  3. An underground pipe takes water to a sump for immediate use.
  4. Excess water from the sump is taken to the well.
  5. Water from the well recharges the underground water.
  6. Water is taken from the well later.

  1. Bamboo drip irrigation (NCERT box, Meghalaya)

  • In Meghalaya, a 200-year-old system of tapping stream and spring water using bamboo pipes is still in use. It is an old system, not a modern adaptation: do not list it under "modern adaptations".
  • About 18–20 litres of water enters the bamboo pipe system, is carried over hundreds of metres, and finally reduces to 20–80 drops per minute at the site of the plant.
  • How it works (NCERT Fig. 3.7):
  • bamboo pipes divert perennial springs on the hilltops to the lower reaches by gravity;
  • channel sections made of bamboo carry water to the plant site, where it is distributed into branches; the flow is controlled by changing the pipe positions;
  • where the pipes cross a road, they are taken high above the land;
  • reduced channel sections and diversion units at the last stage let water drop near the roots of the plant.

Reading the numbers — three questions:

  • Shows: water at the start (18–20 litres entering the system) and at the end (20–80 drops per minute at each plant).
  • Changed: a large flow at the spring becomes a slow drip at the roots, after travelling hundreds of metres.
  • Textbook says: this is a 200-year-old system that is still prevalent in Meghalaya.

Worked example 1

A village's rain harvest

Question. In an imagined village, Neerpura, 120 houses each harvest about 30,000 litres of rooftop rainwater a year. (a) How much water does the whole village harvest in a year? Write it in lakh litres. (b) Use the same method on your textbook's Gendathur numbers (200 houses, about 50,000 litres each). What do you notice?

  1. (Neerpura and its numbers are imagined for practice; the Gendathur figures are your textbook's.)

    Asked: a village total, and a check of the textbook's total.

    Concept used: Gendathur's numbers (section 2).

  2. 1

    (a) 120 × 30,000 = 36,00,000 litres = 36 lakh litres.

  3. 2

    (b) 200 × 50,000 = 1,00,00,000 litres (one crore).

  4. 3

    Notice: your textbook prints the Gendathur total as 1,00,000 litres — a hundred times smaller than 200 × 50,000.

  5. Check: 36 lakh ÷ 120 = 30,000 ✔; 1 crore ÷ 200 = 50,000 ✔.

  6. Answer (a) 36 lakh litres; (b) 1 crore litres — the printed total does not match the per-house figure.

    Common trap: copying "1,00,000 litres" as the total without checking it. Quote the 50,000 litres per house, which the textbook states clearly.

Worked example 2

Rain next door, dry taps at home

Question. "Shillong faces a shortage of water even though it lies close to the places with the highest rainfall in the world." Explain the statement, and say how Shillong's people have responded. (3 marks)

Asked: the puzzle, and the response.

Concept used: Shillong's puzzle (section 3); Topic 1.

  1. 1

    The puzzle: Cherapunjee and Mawsynram, about 55 km away, get the highest rainfall in the world, yet the state capital Shillong faces acute shortage of water.

  2. 2

    One possible explanation (your textbook gives no reason for Shillong's shortage): heavy rain nearby does not by itself remove scarcity, which in most cases comes from over-exploitation, excessive use and unequal access (Topic 1).

  3. 3

    The response: rooftop harvesting is the most common practice in Shillong; nearly every household has a structure, giving 15–25 per cent of its water.

  4. Check: point 1 states the puzzle, point 2 offers one possible explanation in Topic 1's textbook words, point 3 gives the response — three different ideas for three marks.

  5. Answer Heavy rain falls nearby, but Shillong still lacks water; nearly every household now harvests rooftop rain for 15–25 per cent of its needs.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Cherapunjee and Mawsynram, about 55 km from Shillong, receive the world's highest rainfall, yet Shillong faces acute water shortage. (1)
    2. One possible explanation: rain nearby does not remove scarcity, which mostly comes from over-exploitation, excessive use and unequal access. (1)
    3. Nearly every household has rooftop harvesting, which meets 15–25 per cent of its water needs. (1)
    Common trap: writing "Shillong gets no rain". The point is that heavy rain falls close by, yet the town is short of water.

Common mistakes!

  • "Rajasthan was the first state to make rooftop harvesting compulsory."

    Your textbook names Tamil Nadu — compulsory for all houses, with legal provisions to punish defaulters.

    Rajasthan = tankas (tradition); Tamil Nadu = the law (modern).

  • Gendathur placed in Meghalaya, or Shillong in Karnataka.

    Gendathur — Mysuru, Karnataka; Shillong — Meghalaya, 55 km from Cherapunjee and Mawsynram.

    Learn each example with its state.

  • Bamboo drip numbers swapped — "20–80 litres in, 18–20 drops out".

    About 18–20 litres enter; 20–80 drops per minute reach the plant, after hundreds of metres; the system is 200 years old, in Meghalaya.

    Litres at the start, drops at the end.

  • Quoting the Gendathur total "1,00,000 litres" as if it were 200 × 50,000.

    200 × 50,000 = 1,00,00,000; the printed total does not match.

    Quote "about 50,000 litres per house".

  • For "describe modern adaptations", writing only one example.

    A 5-mark answer needs five different points — rooftop recharge steps and several places.

    Use the steps of Fig. 3.3 and the three examples.

Key points

  • Rooftop rainwater harvesting is being successfully adapted in many parts of rural and urban India.
  • Gendathur (Mysuru, Karnataka): nearly 200 households, about 50,000 litres per house a year; "rich in rainwater".
  • Shillong (Meghalaya): close to Cherapunjee and Mawsynram (highest rainfall), yet acute shortage; rooftop harvesting gives 15–25 per cent of household water.
  • Tamil Nadu: the first state to make rooftop harvesting compulsory for all houses, with penalties for defaulters.
  • Fig. 3.3: PVC pipe → sand and brick filter → sump → well → recharge of groundwater (through a hand pump or an abandoned dugwell).
  • Bamboo drip (Meghalaya): 200 years old; 18–20 litres in → 20–80 drops per minute at the plant, over hundreds of metres, by gravity.
