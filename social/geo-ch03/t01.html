Topic 1 of 7

Water: a renewable resource, yet scarce

About 35 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain how the hydrological cycle makes water a renewable resource, and how little of the earth's water is usable freshwater.
  • Explain why a region with ample water can still face water scarcity — of quantity and of quality.

A monsoon cloudburst floods the lanes of a big city, and the very next morning families queue at a tanker for drinking water. Water everywhere, and not a drop to drink. How can a place have too much water and too little at the same time? This topic answers that puzzle.

Quick reminder (rain over India)

You learnt in Class 9 that rain does not fall evenly: some regions get heavy rain, others very little, and most of it comes in a few monsoon months. Keep this in mind — but this topic shows that rain alone does not decide who has water.

  1. Water is renewable

  • About three-fourth of the earth's surface is covered with water, but only a small proportion of it is freshwater that can be put to use.
  • Freshwater comes mainly from surface run off and ground water.
  • Both are continually renewed and recharged through the hydrological cycle: water moves from the sea and land into the air, falls as rain or snow, runs off or soaks in, and returns again.
  • Because all water moves within the hydrological cycle, water is a renewable resource.

Renewable does not mean unlimited. The cycle renews water over time, but the usable share is small and unevenly spread. That is why your textbook asks: if water is renewable, why do countries and regions suffer from water scarcity? It also says it was predicted that by 2025, nearly two billion people will live in absolute water scarcity.

Note on the textbook's wording. The "by 2025" figure is a prediction printed in your textbook. Write it as the textbook's prediction.

  1. What we picture — and what usually causes scarcity

When we hear "water shortage", we picture low-rainfall or drought-prone regions — the deserts of Rajasthan and women carrying many 'matkas' (earthen pots) over long distances.

Your textbook agrees that the availability of water varies over space and time, mainly because seasonal and annual precipitation varies. But it says that in most cases water scarcity is caused by:

  1. over-exploitation of water,
  2. excessive use of water, and
  3. unequal access to water among different social groups.

So scarcity is often made by how people use and share water, not only by how much rain falls.

  1. Scarcity where water is ample

Freshwater can be obtained directly from precipitation, surface run off and groundwater. Yet your textbook says many of our cities have ample water and still face scarcity. Two kinds of scarcity explain this:

Kind of scarcity What it means Example from your textbook
Scarcity of quantity Not enough water for everyone's needs, because demand is high or access is unequal Large and growing populations in cities; over-exploitation for irrigation
Scarcity of quality Water is available, but bad quality makes it unsafe Water polluted by domestic and industrial wastes, chemicals, pesticides and fertilisers used in agriculture becomes hazardous for human use

NCERT Fig. 3.1 ("Water Scarcity") shows the puzzle in photographs: after a record overnight rain in Kolkata, a boy collects drinking water from a flooded street; a survivor of an earthquake in Kashmir carries water through snow; a line of women waits with metal pots.

  1. Reading the puzzle — three questions

What does it show? What changed? What does the textbook say about it?

  • Shows: places with plenty of water (heavy rain, snow, big cities) where people still lack safe water.
  • Changed: the water did not disappear — what changed is how much is demanded, who can reach it and how clean it is.
  • Textbook says: scarcity in most cases comes from over-exploitation, excessive use and unequal access, and it can also come from bad quality of water.

Worked example 1

Heavy rain, dry taps

Question. Megharpur, a hill town, gets heavy rain every monsoon, yet many of its families buy water from tankers in summer. Using your textbook, give three reasons why a place with ample water can still face water scarcity. (3 marks)

  1. (Megharpur is imagined for practice; the reasons are from your textbook.)

    Asked: three reasons for scarcity where water is plentiful.

    Concept used: What usually causes scarcity; scarcity of quality (sections 2 and 3).

  2. 1

    Use: scarcity in most cases is caused by over-exploitation and excessive use of water.

  3. 2

    Sharing: unequal access to water among social groups leaves some families short.

  4. 3

    Quality: water that is available may be polluted by wastes and chemicals, and so be unsafe for human use.

  5. Check: each reason is a different idea — using too much, sharing unequally, unsafe water. "The town gets little rain" would contradict the question, so it cannot be a reason here.

  6. Answer Over-exploitation and excessive use; unequal access; bad quality (pollution).

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Water is over-exploited and used excessively, so supplies run short even after heavy rain. (1)
    2. Access to water is unequal among social groups, so some families get too little. (1)
    3. Available water may be polluted by wastes and chemicals, making it hazardous for human use. (1)
    Common trap: writing "low rainfall" as a reason. The question says the town gets heavy rain; the textbook's point is that scarcity is mostly caused by use and access.

Worked example 2

Sort the four places

Question. Sort these imagined places into "scarcity of quantity", "scarcity of quality" or "no scarcity": (a) a river town where factories drain waste into the only river; (b) a hamlet whose clean, protected spring meets every family's needs all year; (c) a fast-growing city whose colonies pump out more groundwater every year; (d) a farming district where tube-wells are drying up.

Asked: the kind of scarcity, if any, in each place.

Concept used: Two kinds of scarcity (section 3).

  1. 1

    (a) Water is there but polluted → scarcity of quality.

  2. 2

    (b) Clean water meets every need → no scarcity.

  3. 3

    (c) Demand outruns supply; groundwater is over-exploited → scarcity of quantity.

  4. 4

    (d) Wells drying up show falling groundwater → scarcity of quantity.

  5. Check: only (a) mentions dirty water, so only (a) is quality; (b) is the one place where needs are met with safe water.

  6. Answer (a) quality; (b) none; (c) quantity; (d) quantity.

    Common trap: putting (d) under "no scarcity" because the district once had plenty of groundwater. Falling water levels show that use is outrunning supply.

Common mistakes!

  • "Water scarcity happens only where it rains little."

    Your textbook says availability varies with rainfall, but scarcity in most cases is caused by over-exploitation, excessive use and unequal access; many cities with ample water face scarcity.

    Name the three usual causes before you mention rainfall.

  • "If a place has enough water, it has no scarcity."

    Water polluted by domestic and industrial wastes, chemicals, pesticides and fertilisers is hazardous for human use — scarcity of quality.

    Give both kinds of scarcity: quantity and quality.

  • "Water is renewable, so it can never run short."

    Only a small proportion of the earth's water is usable freshwater, and it can be over-used and polluted.

    Say "renewable through the hydrological cycle", then add that usable freshwater is limited.

Key points

  • About three-fourth of the earth's surface is water, but only a small proportion is usable freshwater, from surface run off and ground water.
  • The hydrological cycle keeps renewing freshwater, so water is a renewable resource.
  • Water availability varies over space and time, but scarcity in most cases comes from over-exploitation, excessive use and unequal access.
  • Scarcity can be of quantity (not enough for the demand) or of quality (water polluted and hazardous for human use).
  • NCERT Fig. 3.1 shows people short of safe water even in floods and snow.
