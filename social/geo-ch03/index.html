Water Resources

7 topicsAbout 9 hoursBoard exam
Why this chapter matters

Three-fourth of the earth is covered with water, yet in India many cities queue for tankers and many fields wait for rain. This chapter explains why water is scarce even though it is renewable, why we must conserve it, how India has built dams and multi-purpose projects — and what problems they bring — and how people have harvested rain for centuries, from the tankas of Rajasthan to the rooftops of Shillong. It ends with the eight dams you must locate and label on the map of India.

You will learn the hydrological cycle and the two kinds of water scarcity (of quantity and of quality), the causes of scarcity, the Jal Jeevan Mission and the Atal Bhujal Yojana, ancient hydraulic structures, what a dam is, multi-purpose projects (Bhakra–Nangal, Hirakud, Sardar Sarovar), their benefits and problems, the Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchaee Yojana, the floods safety box, traditional rainwater harvesting (guls and kuls, khadins, johads, tankas), its modern adaptations (Gendathur, Shillong, Tamil Nadu), and Meghalaya's 200-year-old bamboo drip irrigation, an old system still in use. It is one of the Geography chapters in the board exam (Geography carries 17 marks + 3 map marks), and it has a map item: locate and label Salal, Bhakra Nangal, Tehri, Rana Pratap Sagar, Sardar Sarovar, Hirakud, Nagarjuna Sagar and Tungabhadra. In the board papers of 2024–2026 it was asked 31 times — most often as a map item on a dam (ten times), then as dam–river matching, source questions on the textbook's boxes (Sardar Sarovar, PMKSY, floods) and rainwater harvesting.

What you'll learn

  • Explain why water is renewable, and why places with ample water can still face scarcity.
  • Explain the causes of scarcity and why we must conserve water; describe the Jal Jeevan Mission and the Atal Bhujal Yojana.
  • Describe ancient water works and what a dam is; explain multi-purpose projects with the textbook's examples.
  • Explain the problems of big dams, compare both sides, describe PMKSY and read the floods box.
  • Name each traditional method with its region, and explain how tankas work.
  • Describe Gendathur, Shillong, Tamil Nadu and rooftop recharge; explain Meghalaya's 200-year-old bamboo drip system (an old system still in use).
  • Locate and label the eight dams and name each one's river.
  • Put the chapter on four cards and answer mixed board-style questions.

Most of the marks in this chapter come from six skills, and every topic practises them:

  1. Locate and label a dam — the eight dams, each with its river.
  2. Explain why — why water is scarce where it is plentiful; why we must conserve.
  3. Weigh both sides — the benefits and problems of multi-purpose projects.
  4. Match a method with its region — kuls, khadins, johads, tankas, bamboo drip; Gendathur, Shillong, Tamil Nadu.
  5. Read a source — the textbook's boxes on Sardar Sarovar, PMKSY and floods.
  6. Write to the marks — 2 marks ≈ two points (up to 40 words), 3 marks ≈ three points (up to 60), 5 marks ≈ five points (up to 120), each on its own line; measures when measures are asked.

The spaced-review sets come back to you after 1 day, 1 week and 1 month. Don't skip them — remembering something after a gap is what makes it stick.

Not in the board exam

The textbook's eight Activities — a proposal to conserve water (p. 21), a traditional method of building dams (p. 22), inter-state water disputes and recent floods (p. 23), flood-prone areas (p. 25), rainwater harvesting near you (p. 27), how industries pollute water and a water-dispute role play (p. 28) — are classroom activities; they get short guided answers in the NCERT solutions and are not part of the board exam. Everything else in the chapter — including the boxes on Atal Jal, ancient hydraulic structures, the dam, Sardar Sarovar, PMKSY, the Krishna-Godavari dispute, floods safety, Shillong, Tamil Nadu and bamboo drip irrigation, and Figs 3.1–3.7 and the dam map — can be asked in the board exam.
