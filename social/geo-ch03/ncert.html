About the question text

Each NCERT question is shown in full above its solution, with its textbook location. The solutions are our own.

How every solution is laid out: Asked → Concept used → Understand it (the reasoning, one step per line) → Check → ✅ Final answer → Write it in the exam (numbered points sized to the marks, with the mark for each point) → Common trap. In the board exam, answers must stay within the word limits: 2 marks — up to 40 words, 3 marks — up to 60 words, 5 marks — up to 120 words.

Marks shown in "Write it in the exam" are our estimate of how a board question of this kind is usually marked; NCERT does not give marks. Where a question asks for your own observation or opinion, the answer is marked "One possible answer".

Question p. 19Renewable, yet scarce3 marks

You might wonder that if three-fourth of the world is covered with water and water is a renewable resource, then how is it that countries and regions around the globe suffer from water scarcity? Why is it predicted that by 2025, nearly two billion people will live in absolute water scarcity?

Asked: why there is scarcity when water is plentiful and renewable, and why so many people were predicted to face absolute scarcity.

Concept used: Water is renewable; what usually causes scarcity (Topic 1); causes of scarcity (Topic 2).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Usable water is little: only a small proportion of the earth's water is freshwater that can be put to use.

  2. 2

    It is unevenly spread: availability varies over space and time with seasonal and annual rain.

  3. 3

    It is misused: in most cases scarcity comes from over-exploitation, excessive use and unequal access.

  4. 4

    The prediction (beyond your textbook: the textbook gives no reason for its prediction; this links it to the causes it does give): a large and growing population demands more water for homes and food, and much of the available water may be polluted — so the number of people without enough safe water was expected to rise.

  5. Check: points 1–3 answer "how is it that…"; point 4 answers "why is it predicted…". Points 1–3 are from the textbook; point 4 is our link between the textbook's causes and its prediction, which it printed for the year 2025.

  6. Answer Usable freshwater is a small, unevenly spread share, and it is over-exploited, overused, unequally shared and polluted; with a growing population, more people were expected to face absolute scarcity.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Only a small proportion of the earth's water is usable freshwater, and its availability varies over space and time. (1)
    2. Over-exploitation, excessive use and unequal access cause scarcity in most cases. (1)
    3. A growing population needs more water for homes and food, while much water is polluted, so scarcity was expected to spread (one possible reason). (1)
    Common trap: answering only "because it rains little". Your textbook says scarcity in most cases is caused by how water is used and shared.
Question p. 20Scarcity where water is ample3 marks

Where is then water scarcity likely to occur? As you have read in the hydrological cycle, freshwater can be obtained directly from precipitation, surface run off and groundwater. Is it possible that an area or region may have ample water resources but is still facing water scarcity?

Asked: where scarcity is likely, and whether a well-watered region can still be short of water.

Concept used: Scarcity where water is ample (Topic 1); causes of scarcity (Topic 2).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Where: freshwater comes from precipitation, surface run off and groundwater, so scarcity is likely wherever these are over-used or unequally shared — not only in dry regions.

  2. 2

    Yes, it is possible: your textbook says many of our cities have ample water and still face scarcity.

  3. 3

    Why (quantity): a large and growing population creates greater demand and unequal access.

  4. 4

    Why (quality): ample water may be polluted by domestic and industrial wastes, chemicals, pesticides and fertilisers, making it hazardous for human use.

  5. Check: the answer says "yes" and gives two different reasons — one about how much water, one about how clean it is.

  6. Answer Yes. Scarcity can occur wherever water is over-used or unequally shared; many cities with ample water face scarcity because of high demand, unequal access and polluted water.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Yes; many Indian cities have ample water resources but still face water scarcity. (1)
    2. A large, growing population creates greater demand for water, and access to it is unequal. (1)
    3. Available water may be polluted by wastes, chemicals, pesticides and fertilisers, making it hazardous for human use. (1)
    Common trap: answering "No — only deserts face scarcity". The textbook's own examples are cities.
Question p. 21Wells and tube-wells on every farm2 marks

You may have seen in many television advertisements that most farmers have their own wells and tube-wells in their farms for irrigation to increase their produce. But have you ever wondered what this could result in?

Asked: the result of many farmers pumping water from their own wells and tube-wells.

Concept used: Wells and tube-wells on farms (Topic 2).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Many pumps draw the same groundwater, so it is used faster than it is recharged.

  2. 2

    Your textbook: this may lead to falling groundwater levels.

  3. 3

    Falling levels adversely affect water availability and the food security of the people.

  4. Check: the answer gives the effect on water (falling levels) and the effect on people (availability, food security) — two parts for two marks.

  5. Answer It may lead to falling groundwater levels, adversely affecting water availability and food security.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Too many wells and tube-wells may lead to falling groundwater levels. (1)
    2. This adversely affects water availability and the food security of the people. (1)
    Common trap: writing "farmers grow more food" as the result. The question asks what the over-pumping could result in — the textbook's warning.
Question p. 22What dams are and how they help3 marks

What are dams and how do they help us in conserving and managing water?

Asked: the meaning of a dam, and how dams help conserve and manage water.

Concept used: What is a dam?; from one use to many uses (Topic 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Meaning: a dam is a barrier across flowing water that obstructs, directs or retards the flow, often creating a reservoir, lake or impoundment.

  2. 2

    Conserving: dams impound rivers and rainwater that can be used later, for example to irrigate fields.

  3. 3

    Managing: today the stored water is used for electricity, domestic and industrial water supply, flood control, recreation, inland navigation and fish breeding — the uses are integrated in multi-purpose projects.

  4. Check: one point defines, two points explain how dams help — three different ideas.

  5. Answer A dam is a barrier that obstructs, directs or retards flowing water, creating a reservoir; it stores water for later use and serves many integrated uses.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. A dam is a barrier across flowing water that obstructs, directs or retards the flow, often creating a reservoir. (1)
    2. Dams impound rivers and rainwater for later use, such as irrigating fields. (1)
    3. The stored water also gives electricity, water supply, flood control, navigation and fish breeding, integrated in multi-purpose projects. (1)
    Common trap: defining a dam as "a wall" and stopping. Your textbook stresses what it does to the flow and the reservoir it creates.

Exercise 1 — Multiple choice questions

Exercise 1 (i)Classify the four situations2 marks

Based on the information given below classify each of the situations as 'suffering from water scarcity' or 'not suffering from water scarcity'.
(a) Region with high annual rainfall.
(b) Region having high annual rainfall and large population.
(c) Region having high annual rainfall but water is highly polluted.
(d) Region having low rainfall and low population.

Asked: a label for each of the four situations.

Concept used: Two kinds of scarcity (Topic 1); more people, more demand (Topic 2).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Situation (a): high rainfall, and nothing else is given → not suffering (no cause of scarcity is stated).

  2. 2

    Situation (b): a large population creates greater demand and unequal access, even with high rainfall → suffering.

  3. 3

    Situation (c): water is there but highly polluted, so it is hazardous for human use → suffering (scarcity of quality).

  4. 4

    Situation (d): low rainfall but low population, so demand is low → not suffering.

  5. Check: only (b) and (c) contain a cause of scarcity your textbook names — large population and pollution.

  6. Answer (a) not suffering; (b) suffering; (c) suffering; (d) not suffering.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    (a) Not suffering from water scarcity. (½)
    (b) Suffering from water scarcity — large population. (½)
    (c) Suffering from water scarcity — polluted water. (½)
    (d) Not suffering from water scarcity — low population. (½)

    Note on the question. Situation (a) gives only the rainfall. The chapter shows that even high-rainfall places can face scarcity (Shillong, Topic 6), so read (a) strictly as "nothing else is wrong". NCERT gives no answer key; this is our answer.

    Common trap: marking (d) as suffering because of low rainfall. With a low population, the little water can meet the need.

Exercise 1 (iii)Correct the false statements3 marks

Here are some false statements. Identify the mistakes and rewrite them correctly.
(a) Multiplying urban centres with large and dense populations and urban lifestyles have helped in proper utilisation of water resources.
(b) Regulating and damming of rivers does not affect the river's natural flow and its sediment flow.
(c) Today in Rajasthan, the practice of rooftop rainwater water harvesting has gained popularity despite high water availability due to the Indira Gandhi Canal.

Asked: the mistake in each statement, and the corrected statement.

Concept used: Cities (Topic 2); why big dams came under opposition (Topic 4); the Rajasthan decline (Topic 5).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Statement (a): the mistake is "helped in proper utilisation". Cities have added to water and energy requirements and aggravated the problem; colonies over-exploit groundwater.

  2. 2

    Statement (b): the mistake is "does not affect". Damming affects the natural flow, causing poor sediment flow and excess sedimentation in the reservoir.

  3. 3

    Statement (c): the mistake is "has gained popularity". In western Rajasthan the practice is on the decline, because the perennial Indira Gandhi Canal gives plenty of water.

  4. Check: each corrected statement says the opposite of the false one, in the textbook's words.

  5. Answer (a) Cities have aggravated water problems; (b) damming affects natural flow and sediment flow; (c) rooftop harvesting is declining in western Rajasthan because of the Indira Gandhi Canal.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    (a) Multiplying urban centres with dense populations and urban lifestyles have added to water and energy needs and aggravated the problem. (1)
    (b) Regulating and damming rivers affects their natural flow and causes poor sediment flow. (1)
    (c) In western Rajasthan, rooftop rainwater harvesting is declining because the perennial Indira Gandhi Canal provides plenty of water. (1)

    Note on the textbook's wording. Statement (c) is printed "rooftop rainwater water harvesting"; it is quoted as printed.

    Common trap: only adding "not" to each sentence. Give the correct reason as well.

Exercise 2 (i)How water is renewable2 marks

Explain how water becomes a renewable resource.

Asked: how water keeps getting renewed.

Concept used: Water is renewable (Topic 1).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Freshwater comes mainly from surface run off and ground water.

  2. 2

    Both are continually renewed and recharged through the hydrological cycle.

  3. 3

    Since all water moves within the hydrological cycle, water is a renewable resource.

  4. Check: the answer names the process (hydrological cycle) and what it renews (run off and ground water).

  5. Answer Surface run off and ground water are continually renewed and recharged through the hydrological cycle, so water is renewable.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Freshwater comes mainly from surface run off and ground water. (1)
    2. These are continually renewed and recharged through the hydrological cycle, so water is a renewable resource. (1)
    Common trap: writing "because there is a lot of water on earth". Renewal comes from the cycle, not the amount.
Exercise 2 (ii)Water scarcity and its causes3 marks

What is water scarcity and what are its main causes?

Asked: the meaning of water scarcity and its main causes.

Concept used: Two kinds of scarcity (Topic 1); causes (Topic 2).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Main causes (textbook): over-exploitation, excessive use and unequal access among social groups.

Exercise 2 (iii)Advantages and disadvantages of multi-purpose projects3 marks

Compare the advantages and disadvantages of multi-purpose river projects.

Asked: advantages and disadvantages, side by side.

Concept used: Both sides at a glance (Topic 4).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Advantages: irrigation, electricity, water supply for homes and industries, flood control, recreation, inland navigation and fish breeding.

  2. 2

    Disadvantages — the river: natural flow and sediment flow affected; rockier stream beds; rivers fragmented, so aquatic fauna cannot migrate for spawning; land submerged.

    Common trap: writing only advantages. "Compare" needs both sides.

Exercise 3 — Answer in about 120 words

Exercise 3 (i)Rainwater harvesting in semi-arid Rajasthan5 marks

Discuss how rainwater harvesting in semi-arid regions of Rajasthan is carried out.

Asked: how people in semi-arid Rajasthan harvest rainwater.

Concept used: Method · region; how a tanka works; the Rajasthan decline (Topic 5).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Fields: in arid and semi-arid regions, fields were turned into rain fed storage structures — khadins in Jaisalmer and johads in other parts of Rajasthan — where water stands and moistens the soil.

  2. 2

    Tankas: in Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer, almost every house had an underground tank (tanka) for drinking water, built inside the house or courtyard.

  3. 3

    How they fill: tankas are connected to sloping roofs through a pipe; the first spell of rain is not collected (it cleans roofs and pipes); later showers are stored.

  4. 4

    Why they matter: water lasts till the next rainfall; palar pani is the purest natural water; rooms beside the tanka stay cool.

  5. 5

    Today: in western Rajasthan the practice is declining because of the Indira Gandhi Canal, though some houses keep tankas for the taste.

  6. Check: five points — fields, tankas, filling, value, today — each a different idea for five marks.

  7. Answer Through khadins and johads in fields, and rooftop harvesting into underground tankas in Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer, where the first rain is let go and the water lasts till the next rain; the practice is now declining because of the Indira Gandhi Canal.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Agricultural fields were turned into rain fed storage structures — khadins in Jaisalmer and johads elsewhere in Rajasthan — that hold water and moisten the soil. (1)
    2. In Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer, almost every house had an underground tanka for drinking water. (1)
    3. Tankas were connected to sloping roofs through a pipe; the first spell of rain, which cleaned roofs and pipes, was not collected. (1)
    4. Stored rainwater, palar pani, the purest natural water, lasted till the next rainfall; rooms beside tankas stayed cool. (1)
    5. Today the practice is declining in western Rajasthan because of the perennial Indira Gandhi Canal. (1)
    Common trap: writing only about tankas. Khadins and johads are also Rajasthan's methods.

    Spread: rooftop harvesting is being successfully adapted in many parts of rural and urban India.

  2. 2

    Gendathur (Mysuru, Karnataka): nearly 200 households harvest rooftop rain; each can collect about 50,000 litres a year; the village is "rich in rainwater".

  3. 3

    Shillong (Meghalaya): despite heavy rain nearby, the city faces shortage; nearly every household harvests rooftop rain for 15–25 per cent of its needs.

  4. 4

    Tamil Nadu: the first state to make rooftop harvesting compulsory for all houses, with penalties for defaulters.

  5. 5

    Groundwater recharge (Fig. 3.3): rooftop water is collected through a PVC pipe, filtered with sand and bricks, stored in a sump, and the excess sent to a well to recharge the groundwater.

  6. Check: five points — one on the spread and four on different places or methods.

  7. Answer Rooftop harvesting is spreading: Gendathur and Shillong collect rooftop rain, Tamil Nadu made it compulsory, and rooftop water is filtered and used to recharge groundwater.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Rooftop rainwater harvesting is being successfully adapted in many parts of rural and urban India. (1)
    2. In Gendathur, Mysuru (Karnataka), nearly 200 households harvest rooftop rain; each house collects about 50,000 litres a year. (1)
    3. In Shillong, nearly every household harvests rooftop rain, meeting 15–25 per cent of its water needs. (1)
    4. Tamil Nadu has made rooftop harvesting compulsory for all houses, with penalties for defaulters. (1)
    5. Rooftop water is filtered through sand and bricks, stored in a sump, and its excess recharges groundwater through wells. (1)

    Note on the textbook's numbers. The textbook's Gendathur total ("1,00,000 litres" from 200 houses) does not match 200 × 50,000 = 1,00,00,000; the answer quotes the per-house figure.

    Common trap: describing only traditional methods (tankas, khadins). The question asks for modern adaptations.
