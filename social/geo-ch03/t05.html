Topic 5 of 7

Traditional rainwater harvesting

About 35 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the traditional methods of water harvesting with their regions — guls or kuls, rooftop harvesting, inundation channels, khadins, johads and tankas.
  • Explain how tankas work in Rajasthan and why rooftop rainwater harvesting is declining in western Rajasthan.

In a desert town where it may rain on only a few days a year, a family drinks clean water in the hottest week of summer. The water fell on their roof months ago. How did it last so long — and stay the "purest" water they know? This topic shows how people across India learnt to catch the rain.

Quick reminder (Mathematics)

The volume of a cuboid = length × breadth × height. 1 cubic metre = 1,000 litres.

  1. An alternative to big dams

Given the disadvantages of multi-purpose projects and rising resistance against them (Topic 4), many thought that water harvesting was a viable alternative, both socio-economically and environmentally.

Ancient India had, along with its hydraulic structures, an extraordinary tradition of water harvesting. People had in-depth knowledge of rainfall regimes and soil types, and developed techniques to harvest rainwater, groundwater, river water and flood water in keeping with the local ecological conditions and their water needs.

  1. Method · region · how it works

Method Region (your textbook) How it works
Guls or kuls Western Himalayas (hill and mountainous regions) Diversion channels for agriculture
Rooftop rainwater harvesting Commonly practised, particularly in Rajasthan To store drinking water
Inundation channels Flood plains of Bengal To irrigate fields
Khadins Jaisalmer (arid and semi-arid regions) Agricultural fields turned into rain fed storage structures: water stands and moistens the soil
Johads Other parts of Rajasthan Like khadins: rain fed storage structures in fields
Tankas Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer (semi-arid and arid Rajasthan) Underground tanks for storing drinking water, fed from the roof

Memory hook: "Kuls in the hills, channels in Bengal's floods, khadins in Jaisalmer, johads elsewhere in Rajasthan, tankas in Bikaner–Phalodi–Barmer."

NCERT Fig. 3.5 ("Traditional method of rainwater harvesting") shows a kul leading to a circular village tank in the Kaza village, from which water is released as and when required.

  1. How a tanka works

  • In Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer, almost all the houses traditionally had underground tanks or tankas for storing drinking water.
  • A tanka could be as large as a big room. One household in Phalodi had a tank 6.1 metres deep, 4.27 metres long and 2.44 metres wide.
  • Tankas were part of a well-developed rooftop rainwater harvesting system, built inside the main house or the courtyard.
  • They were connected to the sloping roofs of the houses through a pipe. Rain falling on the roof travelled down the pipe into the tanka.
  • The first spell of rain was usually not collected, because it cleaned the roofs and the pipes. Rain from the later showers was collected.
  • The water could be stored till the next rainfall, making the tanka an extremely reliable source of drinking water when all other sources had dried up, particularly in summer.
  • Rainwater, called palar pani here, is considered the purest form of natural water.
  • Many houses built underground rooms next to the tanka to beat the summer heat, because the tanka kept the room cool.

NCERT Fig. 3.6 shows rooftop harvesting in a Thar town: water taken from a neighbour's roof through a long pipe, and a hole through which it flows into an underground tanka.

Reading the numbers — three questions:

  • Shows: the size of one Phalodi tanka: 6.1 m × 4.27 m × 2.44 m.
  • Works out to: 6.1 × 4.27 × 2.44 ≈ 63.6 cubic metres, or about 63,600 litres when full (1 cubic metre = 1,000 litres).
  • Textbook says: such a tank was "as large as a big room" and could last till the next rainfall.

  1. Why rooftop harvesting is declining in western Rajasthan

  • Today, in western Rajasthan, rooftop rainwater harvesting is on the decline, because plenty of water is available from the perennial Indira Gandhi Canal.
  • Some houses still maintain the tankas, because they do not like the taste of tap water.

Worked example 1

Which method, where?

Question. Match each method with its region in your textbook: (a) khadins; (b) guls or kuls; (c) inundation channels; (d) tankas. Regions: P. flood plains of Bengal; Q. Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer; R. Jaisalmer; S. Western Himalayas.

Asked: four method–region pairs.

Concept used: Method · region · how it works (section 2).

  1. 1

    (a) Khadins → R. Jaisalmer.

  2. 2

    (b) Guls or kuls → S. Western Himalayas.

  3. 3

    (c) Inundation channels → P. flood plains of Bengal.

  4. 4

    (d) Tankas → Q. Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer.

  5. Check: each method suits its land — channels to divert hill streams (kuls), channels to spread flood water (Bengal), fields that hold rain in a desert (khadins), tanks under houses in dry towns (tankas). Four different regions are used once each.

  6. Answer (a) R; (b) S; (c) P; (d) Q.

    Common trap: matching khadins with Bengal because both use fields. Khadins are in Jaisalmer, where water is scarce; Bengal's flood plains use inundation channels.

Worked example 2

How much can a tanka hold?

Question. An imagined family in a Thar village builds a tanka 3.0 m long, 2.5 m wide and 2.0 m deep. (a) How many litres can it hold? (b) The family drinks and cooks with 60 litres a day. For how many days will a full tanka last? (c) Why does your textbook call the tanka an "extremely reliable" source?

  1. (The tanka's size and the family's use are imagined for practice; (c) is from your textbook.)

    Asked: a volume in litres, a number of days, and the textbook's reason.

    Concept used: How a tanka works (section 3).

  2. 1

    (a) Volume = 3.0 × 2.5 × 2.0 = 15 cubic metres = 15 × 1,000 = 15,000 litres.

  3. 2

    (b) 15,000 ÷ 60 = 250 days.

  4. 3

    (c) Water can be stored till the next rainfall, so the tanka gives drinking water when all other sources have dried up, particularly in summer.

  5. Check: 250 days × 60 litres = 15,000 litres ✔ — most of a year, which matches "till the next rainfall".

  6. Answer (a) 15,000 litres; (b) 250 days; (c) it stores water till the next rain, when other sources are dry.

    Common trap: forgetting to change cubic metres into litres. Multiply by 1,000.

Worked example 3

Why a family valued its tanka

Question. Give two reasons, from your textbook, why families in the Thar valued the water and the space around their tankas. (2 marks)

Asked: two reasons.

Concept used: How a tanka works (section 3).

  1. 1

    The water: rainwater, or palar pani, is considered the purest form of natural water.

  2. 2

    The space: underground rooms built next to the tanka stayed cool, a relief from the summer heat.

  3. Check: one reason is about the water and one about the room — two different ideas for two marks.

  4. Answer Palar pani is the purest natural water; rooms beside the tanka stay cool in summer.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Rainwater stored in the tanka, called palar pani, is considered the purest form of natural water. (1)
    2. Underground rooms built next to the tanka stayed cool and helped families beat the summer heat. (1)
    Common trap: writing "tankas are big" as a reason. Size is a fact, not a reason families valued them.

Common mistakes!

  • Khadins placed in Bengal, kuls in Rajasthan, tankas in the Himalayas.

    Guls or kuls — Western Himalayas; inundation channels — flood plains of Bengal; khadins — Jaisalmer; johads — other parts of Rajasthan; tankas — Bikaner, Phalodi, Barmer.

    Learn each method with its region, using the memory hook.

  • "All the rain is collected in the tanka, including the first shower."

    The first spell of rain is not collected: it cleans the roofs and pipes.

    Say why the first rain is let go.

  • "Palar pani" called groundwater or canal water.

    Palar pani is rainwater, considered the purest form of natural water.

    Write "palar pani = rainwater".

  • "Rooftop harvesting is growing in western Rajasthan because of the Indira Gandhi Canal."

    It is declining there, because the perennial Indira Gandhi Canal gives plenty of water; some houses keep tankas because they do not like the taste of tap water.

    Canal → decline (not growth).

Key points

  • Water harvesting was seen as a viable alternative to big dams, socio-economically and environmentally; ancient methods suited local rainfall, soil and water needs.
  • Guls or kuls (Western Himalayas) — diversion channels; rooftop harvesting (Rajasthan) — drinking water; inundation channels (flood plains of Bengal); khadins (Jaisalmer) and johads (other parts of Rajasthan) — fields that hold rain.
  • Tankas (Bikaner, Phalodi, Barmer): underground tanks, as large as a big room, fed from sloping roofs through a pipe; the first spell of rain is not collected.
  • Tanka water lasts till the next rainfall; palar pani is the purest natural water; underground rooms beside it stay cool.
  • Rooftop harvesting is declining in western Rajasthan because of the perennial Indira Gandhi Canal.
