Topic 3 of 10

Liberal nationalism and the new middle class

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the society of Europe (aristocracy, peasantry, new middle classes) and the patchwork Habsburg Empire.
  • Explain what liberalism stood for politically, socially and economically, and its limits (suffrage, women).
  • Explain the Zollverein and economic nationalism (Source B).

A cloth merchant sets out in 1833 from Hamburg to Nuremberg with his wagon. Every time he crosses into another little German state, an officer stops him, measures his cloth and takes a duty. He stops eleven times. At each stop the length called an "elle" is different. By the end of the trip he is tired, poorer, and convinced of one thing: Germany should be one country with one market.

  1. Europe in the mid-eighteenth century: no nation-states

On a map of mid-eighteenth-century Europe there were no "nation-states" as we know them today. What we now call Germany, Italy and Switzerland were divided into kingdoms, duchies and cantons, each ruler with his own territory. Eastern and Central Europe were under autocratic monarchies ruling diverse peoples who did not share a collective identity — often they spoke different languages and belonged to different ethnic groups.

The Habsburg Empire (Austria-Hungary) — a patchwork:

Part Who lived there
Alpine regions — the Tyrol, Austria, the Sudetenland — and Bohemia In Bohemia the aristocracy was mainly German-speaking
Lombardy and Venetia Italian-speaking provinces
Hungary Half the people spoke Magyar, half a variety of dialects
Galicia The aristocracy spoke Polish
Subject peasant peoples Bohemians and Slovaks (north), Slovenes in Carniola, Croats (south), Roumans in Transylvania (east)

The only tie binding these groups was a common allegiance to the emperor.

  1. The aristocracy and the new middle class

Group Features
Landed aristocracy Dominant socially and politically; a common way of life across regions; estates in the countryside and town-houses; spoke French for diplomacy and high society; families linked by marriage; but numerically small
Peasantry The majority. In the west, land farmed by tenants and small owners; in Eastern and Central Europe, vast estates cultivated by serfs
New social groups (with industrialisation) A working class, and middle classes of industrialists, businessmen and professionals. England industrialised first, in the second half of the eighteenth century; France and parts of the German states followed only in the nineteenth century. In Central and Eastern Europe these groups stayed small until the late nineteenth century

Key link: ideas of national unity, along with the abolition of aristocratic privileges, became popular among the educated, liberal middle classes.

  1. What did liberal nationalism stand for?

The word liberalism comes from the Latin root liber, meaning free.

Sphere Liberalism stood for…
Social (for the new middle classes) Freedom for the individual and equality of all before the law
Political Government by consent. Since the French Revolution this meant: no autocracy and no clerical privileges, a constitution, and representative government through parliament. Private property was inviolable (could not be taken away)
Economic Freedom of markets and the abolition of state-imposed restrictions on the movement of goods and capital

Its limits — who could vote? Equality before the law did not mean universal suffrage:

  • In revolutionary France the right to vote and be elected went only to property-owning men; men without property and all women were excluded.
  • Only for a brief period under the Jacobins did all adult males enjoy suffrage.
  • The Napoleonic Code went back to limited suffrage and reduced women to the status of a minor, subject to fathers and husbands.
  • Across the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, women and non-propertied men built opposition movements to demand equal political rights.

New word. Suffrage — the right to vote.

  1. The economic road to the nation: the Zollverein

The problem. Napoleon's measures had turned countless small principalities into a confederation of 39 states, each with its own currency and its own weights and measures:

  • A merchant travelling in 1833 from Hamburg to Nuremberg passed 11 customs barriers and paid about 5 per cent duty at each.
  • Duties were often charged by weight or measurement, and every region had its own system — so calculations took time. The elle (a measure of cloth) was 54.7 cm in Frankfurt, 55.1 cm in Mainz, 65.6 cm in Nuremberg and 53.5 cm in Freiburg.

The new commercial classes saw these as obstacles and argued for a unified economic territory allowing the unhindered movement of goods, people and capital.

The solution.

  • In 1834 a customs union, or zollverein, was formed at the initiative of Prussia and joined by most of the German states.
  • It abolished tariff barriers and reduced the number of currencies from over thirty to two.
  • A network of railways increased mobility, harnessing economic interests to national unification.
  • A wave of economic nationalism strengthened the wider nationalist feeling of the time.

Reading Source B — Friedrich List (1834). List, Professor of Economics at the University of Tübingen, wrote that the aim of the zollverein was "to bind the Germans economically into a nation". It would strengthen the nation materially — by protecting its interests outside and stimulating productivity inside — and it would awaken national sentiment by fusing individual and provincial interests. What it tells us: economists now thought in terms of a national economy; for List, a free economic system was the way to create national feeling.

Worked example 1

The elle as an argument

Question. A Frankfurt trader sells "ten elle" of cloth to a buyer from Nuremberg. Using the textbook's figures, explain what problem this shows and how the commercial classes wanted to solve it. (3 marks)

Asked: the problem shown by the elle, and the solution the commercial classes wanted.

Concept used: The Zollverein (this topic, section 4). Why it applies: the textbook uses the elle to show the cost of different measures.

  1. 1

    The problem: an elle bought in Frankfurt gave 54.7 cm of cloth but in Nuremberg an elle meant 65.6 cm, so "ten elle" meant 547 cm to one and 656 cm to the other — every deal needed slow, confusing calculation.

  2. 2

    Why it mattered: with 39 states, each with its own currency, weights and measures, and customs duties at every border, such conditions were obstacles to economic exchange and growth.

  3. 3

    The solution: the new commercial classes wanted a unified economic territory with unhindered movement of goods, people and capital — achieved partly by the Zollverein of 1834.

  4. Check: 10 × 54.7 = 547 cm and 10 × 65.6 = 656 cm — a difference of 109 cm, more than one whole Nuremberg elle, in a single small sale. If the two lengths were equal, there would be no problem to explain.

  5. Answer Different measures (54.7 cm vs 65.6 cm per elle) made trade slow and uncertain; the commercial classes wanted one economic territory, which the Zollverein began to create.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. An elle meant 54.7 cm in Frankfurt but 65.6 cm in Nuremberg, so every sale needed slow, confusing calculation. (1)
    2. With 39 states, each with its own currency, weights and customs duties, such differences obstructed trade and growth. (1)
    3. The commercial classes demanded a unified economic territory; the Zollverein of 1834 abolished tariff barriers and cut currencies to two. (1)
    Common trap: saying the Zollverein created one German state. It created a customs union; political unification came only in 1871 (Topic 7).

Worked example 2

Free markets, but not free votes

Question. "The liberals of nineteenth-century Europe wanted equality, yet not for everyone." Explain with two examples. (2 marks)

Asked: two examples of the limits of liberal equality.

Concept used: Liberalism and suffrage (this topic, section 3).

  1. 1

    Property: in revolutionary France only property-owning men could vote and be elected; men without property were excluded.

  2. 2

    Women: all women were excluded, and the Napoleonic Code reduced women to the status of a minor, subject to fathers and husbands.

  3. Check: both examples show equality before the law without universal suffrage — the gap the statement points to. An example like "they wanted a constitution" shows what liberals wanted, not its limit.

  4. Answer Votes went only to property-owning men, and women were excluded and treated as minors.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. In revolutionary France only property-owning men could vote and be elected; men without property were excluded. (1)
    2. All women were excluded, and the Napoleonic Code reduced women to the status of a minor under fathers and husbands. (1)
    Common trap: writing that liberals wanted votes for all. In revolutionary France, all adult males could vote only for a brief period under the Jacobins; otherwise only property-owning men voted.

Quick check · 3 of 3MCQ1 mark

What was the only tie that bound the many peoples of the Habsburg Empire together?

The textbook says the only tie was a common allegiance to the emperor.
They spoke many languages — German, Italian, Magyar, Polish and others.
The textbook does not give religion as the tie.
The empire was an autocratic monarchy, not ruled by an elected parliament.

Common mistakes!

  • "Liberals wanted to keep the monarchy, the Church and social hierarchy."

    That is conservatism (Topic 4). Liberals wanted an end to autocracy and clerical privileges.

    Learn liberal = freedom, consent, constitution, free markets.

  • "Liberal equality meant every adult could vote."

    Only property-owning men voted; women were excluded.

    Separate equality before the law from suffrage.

  • "The Zollverein was formed after Germany was unified."

    It came in 1834, long before unification in 1871, and helped build national feeling.

    Put the customs union first, the state later.

  • "The Zollverein was formed by Austria."

    It was formed at the initiative of Prussia.

    Remember: Prussia → Zollverein → later leads unification.

Key points

  • Mid-18th-century Europe had no nation-states; Germany, Italy and Switzerland were divided; the Habsburg Empire was a patchwork held together only by allegiance to the emperor.
  • A small landed aristocracy dominated; most people were peasants; industrialisation created a working class and middle classes. The educated, liberal middle classes spread ideas of national unity.
  • Liberalism (liber = free): freedom for the individual, equality before the law, government by consent, constitution and parliament, private property, free markets. Limit: votes only for property-owning men; women excluded and treated as minors.
  • Zollverein, 1834 (Prussia): tariff barriers abolished, currencies 30+ → 2, railways → economic nationalism. Source B (List, 1834): bind the Germans economically into a nation.
