About the question text

Each NCERT question is shown in full above its solution, with its textbook location. The solutions are our own.

How every solution is laid out: Asked → Concept used → Understand it (the reasoning, one step per line) → Check → ✅ Final answer → Write it in the exam (numbered points sized to the marks, with the mark for each point) → Common trap. In the board exam, answers must stay within the word limits: 2 marks — up to 40 words, 3 marks — up to 60 words, 5 marks — up to 120 words.

Marks shown in "Write it in the exam" are our estimate of how a board question of this kind is usually marked; NCERT does not give marks.

Activity p. 3Sorrieu's utopian vision3 marks

In what way do you think this print (Fig. 1) depicts a utopian vision?

Asked: why Sorrieu's print shows an ideal, not a real, world.

Concept used: Reading Sorrieu's print (Topic 1, section 1); the meaning of "utopian".

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Utopian means a vision of a society so ideal that it is unlikely to actually exist.

  2. 2

    The dream: all the peoples of Europe and America, of every age and class, march together as free nations, honouring Liberty; Christ, saints and angels bless their fraternity.

  3. 3

    The old order gone: the symbols of absolutist institutions lie shattered — no absolute kings remain.

  4. 4

    The reality of 1848: most of these peoples were not free nation-states; Germany, for example, was not yet united. So the print shows a hope, not a fact.

  5. Check: each point contrasts the picture with the real Europe of 1848; an answer that only describes the picture does not explain "utopian".

  6. Answer It shows a world of free, democratic, brotherly nations with absolutism destroyed — an ideal that did not exist in 1848, when most peoples were still ruled by monarchs and Germany was not even united.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Peoples of all ages and classes march as free nations towards the statue of Liberty, united in fraternity. (1)
    2. The symbols of absolutist institutions lie shattered, as if monarchy had ended everywhere. (1)
    3. In 1848 most peoples were still under monarchs and Germany was not united, so this ideal was unlikely to exist. (1)
    Common trap: calling the print "a picture of the French Revolution". It is a dream of the future drawn in 1848.
Discuss p. 4Renan's nation3 marks

Summarise the attributes of a nation, as Renan understands them. Why, in his view, are nations important?

Asked: (i) the attributes of a nation; (ii) why nations are important.

Concept used: Source A (Topic 1, section 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Not these: a nation is not formed by a common language, race, religion or territory.

  2. 2

    Attributes: a long past of endeavours, sacrifice and devotion; a heroic past, great men and glory; common glories in the past and a common will in the present; deeds done together and the wish to do more; a large-scale solidarity — "a daily plebiscite".

  3. 3

    Respect for the people: a province is its inhabitants; a nation has no real interest in annexing a country against its will.

  4. 4

    Why important: nations are a good thing, even a necessity — a guarantee of liberty, which would be lost if the world had only one law and one master.

  5. Check: the answer has two parts, matching the two questions; leaving out "guarantee of liberty" leaves the second part unanswered.

  6. Answer For Renan a nation is a shared heroic past and a present will to live together, renewed daily by consent; nations matter because they guarantee liberty.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. A nation is not language, race, religion or territory, but a long past of sacrifice, devotion and shared glory. (1)
    2. Its people share a common will to live together — a large-scale solidarity, a "daily plebiscite". (1)
    3. Nations guarantee liberty, which would be lost if the world had only one law and one master. (1)
    Common trap: writing that Renan wanted one world government. He warns against one law and one master.
Discuss p. 10List's political ends2 marks

Describe the political ends that List hopes to achieve through economic measures.

Asked: the political goals behind List's economic plan.

Concept used: Source B (Topic 3, section 4).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    The measure: the zollverein (customs union) and a free economic system.

  2. 2

    Political end 1: "to bind the Germans economically into a nation" — to strengthen the nation by protecting its interests outside and stimulating productivity inside.

  3. 3

    Political end 2: to awaken and raise national sentiment by fusing individual and provincial interests — a free economic system as "the only means to engender national feeling".

  4. Check: both points are political (a nation, national feeling), reached through economic means; "lower prices for traders" alone would be an economic end.

  5. Answer List hoped the customs union would bind the Germans into one nation and awaken national feeling.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. List wanted the zollverein to bind the Germans economically into a nation, strengthening it at home and abroad. (1)
    2. By fusing individual and provincial interests, a free economy would awaken national feeling among Germans. (1)
    Common trap: writing about railways and currencies only. The question asks for the political ends.
Activity p. 11Mapping the Vienna settlement3 marks

Plot on a map of Europe the changes drawn up by the Vienna Congress.

Asked: the changes made by the Vienna Congress, placed on a map of Europe.

Concept used: The Treaty of Vienna (Topic 4, section 2) and the map in Topic 4.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    France: Bourbon dynasty restored; France loses the territories annexed under Napoleon (shade France).

  2. 2

    North of France: the kingdom of the Netherlands, including Belgium.

  3. 3

    South-east of France: Genoa added to Piedmont.

  4. 4

    Prussia: new territories on its western frontiers, and a portion of Saxony.

  5. 5

    Austria: control of northern Italy.

  6. 6

    Russia: part of Poland.

  7. 7

    Unchanged: the German confederation of 39 states (draw a dashed outline).

  8. Check: the two new states (Netherlands with Belgium; Piedmont with Genoa) must sit on the boundaries of France — that was their purpose; if they are drawn far from France, the map has missed the point.

  9. Answer A labelled map showing the seven changes above (see the Vienna map in Topic 4).

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Around France: Bourbons restored; the Netherlands (with Belgium) set up in the north and Genoa added to Piedmont in the south. (1)
    2. Prussia gained western territories and part of Saxony; Austria gained control of northern Italy. (1)
    3. Russia gained part of Poland; the 39-state German confederation was left untouched. (1)
    Common trap: marking a united Germany on the map. In 1815 there was only the 39-state confederation.

Discuss p. 15Language and popular traditions3 marks

Discuss the importance of language and popular traditions in the creation of national identity.

Asked: how language and folk traditions helped build national identity.

Concept used: The Romantic imagination and Poland (Topic 5, sections 4–5).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Popular traditions: Herder said true German culture lay among the common people (das volk); folk songs, poetry and dances spread the volksgeist; the Grimm brothers collected folktales as a pure German spirit, against French domination.

  2. 2

    Reaching everyone: the vernacular and folklore carried the nationalist message to large audiences who were mostly illiterate.

  3. 3

    Music: in Poland, Kurpinski turned the polonaise and mazurka into nationalist symbols.

  4. 4

    Language as resistance: after the 1831 rebellion, Polish clergy used Polish in church; it became a symbol of the struggle against Russian dominance.

  5. Check: the answer includes both language and popular traditions, each with an example; an answer only about the Grimms leaves out language.

  6. Answer Folk culture gave nations a shared heritage that even illiterate people could feel, and language — as in Poland — became a weapon of resistance.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Herder and the Grimm brothers saw folk songs, dances and tales as the true national spirit, a shared heritage. (1)
    2. Folklore, music and the vernacular carried the nationalist message to mostly illiterate people, as Kurpinski's polonaise and mazurka did in Poland. (1)
    3. After 1831 Polish clergy used Polish in church, making it a symbol of resistance to Russian dominance. (1)
    Common trap: writing that a nation needs one language (Renan rejects that). The point is how language and traditions built feeling, not a rule.
Discuss p. 16The Silesian weavers3 marks

Describe the cause of the Silesian weavers' uprising. Comment on the viewpoint of the journalist.

Asked: (i) the cause; (ii) the journalist's point of view.

Concept used: The Silesian weavers, 1845 (Topic 6, section 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Cause: contractors supplied raw material and ordered finished textiles but drastically reduced their payments; with the weavers' extreme misery and need for jobs, contractors cut prices further.

  2. 2

    The event (4 June 1845): weavers marched to the contractor's mansion demanding higher wages, met scorn and threats, broke in; the army came back with the contractor and eleven weavers were shot.

  3. 3

    The journalist (Wilhelm Wolff): he stresses the workers' misery and the contractors' exploitation, and notes that a village refused the contractor shelter — his account is sympathetic to the weavers, though he reports their violence too.

  4. Check: the cause is reduced payments, not "a bad harvest"; the comment is about Wolff's attitude, not only the events.

  5. Answer Contractors cut payments to desperate weavers; Wolff's account sympathises with the weavers' misery and blames the contractors' exploitation.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Contractors supplied raw material but drastically reduced payments for finished cloth, exploiting the weavers' need for work. (1)
    2. On 4 June 1845 the weavers demanded higher wages, were scorned, broke in; the army returned and eleven weavers were shot. (1)
    3. Wolff stresses the weavers' extreme misery and the contractors' exploitation, so his account is sympathetic to the weavers. (1)
    Common trap: saying the journalist supported the contractor. Wolff's words about "extreme" misery show where his sympathy lies.
Activity p. 16A weaver's report5 marks

Imagine you are a weaver who saw the events as they unfolded. Write a report on what you saw.

Asked: an eyewitness report, in the first person, of the events of 4 June 1845.

Concept used: Wolff's account (Topic 6, section 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Set the scene: our village of cotton weavers; extreme misery; contractors cutting payments.

  2. 2

    The march: 4 June, 2 p.m.; marching in pairs to the mansion; demanding higher wages.

  3. 3

    The response: scorn and threats.

  4. 4

    The anger: windowpanes, furniture, porcelain smashed; the storehouse plundered.

  5. 5

    The end: the contractor fled, was refused shelter, returned with the army 24 hours later; eleven of us shot.

  6. Check: every fact in the report must come from Wolff's account — the time, the pairs, the demand, the army, eleven shot; adding "a hundred were killed" would break the report.

  7. Answer A sample first-person report is given below.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Report — Silesia, June 1845. Our village lives by cotton weaving, but our misery is extreme: the contractors keep cutting what they pay us. (1)
    2. On 4 June at 2 p.m. we marched in pairs to our contractor's mansion to ask for higher wages. (1)
    3. We were met with scorn and threats. (1)
    4. Angry men broke in, smashed windowpanes, furniture and porcelain, and plundered the storehouse of cloth. (1)
    5. The contractor fled, but the next village refused him shelter; he returned with the army after 24 hours, and eleven of us were shot. (1)
    Common trap: writing an essay about the 1848 revolution. The report is about what one weaver saw in 1845.
Discuss p. 18Three writers on women's rights5 marks

Compare the positions on the question of women's rights voiced by the three writers cited above. What do they reveal about liberal ideology?

Asked: (i) the three positions compared; (ii) what they show about liberal ideology.

Concept used: Source C (Topic 6, section 6).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Carl Welcker (Frankfurt Parliament): men and women have different natural functions; man is the protector and provider; woman's sphere is the home; equality would destroy family harmony — against political equality.

  2. 2

    Louise Otto-Peters (editorial, 21 April 1849): men who fight for Liberty fight for only half of humanity; "Liberty is indivisible" — for equal rights.

  3. 3

    Anonymous reader (letter, 25 June 1850): women hold property and duties, yet even the stupidest cattle-herder votes just because he is a man — for women's political rights, on grounds of fairness and property.

  4. 4

    What it reveals: liberals preached liberty and equality, but many limited political rights to men; liberalism was divided, and women had to fight for liberty to include them.

  5. Check: the comparison must show one against, two for, and then a conclusion about liberalism as a whole; describing each writer without the conclusion answers only half.

  6. Answer Welcker opposed equality; Otto-Peters and the reader demanded it; liberal ideology spoke of universal liberty but often excluded women.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Carl Welcker held that men and women had different natural functions; a woman's sphere was the home, and equality would destroy family harmony. (1)
    2. Louise Otto-Peters argued that men fighting for liberty fought for only half of humanity: "Liberty is indivisible". (1)
    3. An anonymous reader found it unjust that property-owning women had no vote while any man could vote. (1)
    4. So liberals were divided: one denied women equality, two demanded it. (1)
    5. This reveals that liberal ideology preached liberty and equality yet often limited political rights to men. (1)
    Common trap: saying all three writers supported women's suffrage. Welcker opposed equality between the sexes.
Activity p. 20Bismarck in the Reichstag3 marks

Describe the caricature. How does it represent the relationship between Bismarck and the elected deputies of Parliament? What interpretation of democratic processes is the artist trying to convey?

Asked: (i) a description of Fig. 13; (ii) the relationship it shows; (iii) the artist's view of democracy.

Concept used: Reading Fig. 13 (Topic 7, section 2).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Description: Bismarck stands in the Reichstag cracking a whip; the deputies duck low behind their benches (Figaro, Vienna, 5 March 1870).

  2. 2

    Relationship: Bismarck dominates; the elected deputies are cowed and obey.

  3. 3

    Interpretation: the artist suggests that in Prussian-led Germany democratic processes were weak — parliament existed, but real power lay with the chief minister and the state.

  4. Check: three questions, three points; the third must be about democracy, not about Bismarck's appearance.

  5. Answer Bismarck whips the ducking deputies: he controls parliament, and the artist suggests democracy in Germany was only a show.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. The caricature shows Bismarck cracking a whip over the Reichstag while the deputies duck behind their benches. (1)
    2. He dominates the elected deputies, who are shown as frightened and obedient. (1)
    3. The artist suggests democratic processes were weak: real power lay with Bismarck and the Prussian state, not parliament. (1)
    Common trap: writing only "Bismarck is angry". Say what the picture means for parliament and democracy.
Activity p. 21Italy before and after unification3 marks

Look at Fig. 14(a). Do you think that the people living in any of these regions thought of themselves as Italians? Examine Fig. 14(b). Which was the first region to become a part of unified Italy? Which was the last region to join? In which year did the largest number of states join?

Asked: (i) whether people felt Italian; (ii) first region; (iii) last region; (iv) year with the most states joining.

Concept used: Italy unified and reading Figs 14(a)–(b) (Topic 7, section 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Fig. 14(a): in 1858 the peninsula was split into states under the Habsburgs, the Pope, the Bourbon kings and Sardinia-Piedmont; the Italian language had no common form, illiteracy was high, and southern peasants had never heard of Italia. So most people probably did not think of themselves as Italians — the educated elite did.

  2. 2

    First region (Fig. 14(b)): Sardinia-Piedmont (marked 1858).

  3. 3

    Last region: the Papal State (Rome), 1870.

  4. 4

    Largest number: in 1858–60, when the states of the north and centre joined (and the Two Sicilies in 1860).

  5. Check: the map's years run 1858 → 1858–60 → 1860 → 1866 → 1870; the earliest is Sardinia-Piedmont and the latest Rome, so no other answer fits.

  6. Answer Most people probably did not feel Italian; first Sardinia-Piedmont, last the Papal State (1870); most states joined in 1858–60.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Probably not: Italy was split among dynasties, the language had no common form, and peasants had never heard of Italia. (1)
    2. The first region was Sardinia-Piedmont; the last was the Papal State (Rome), in 1870. (1)
    3. The largest number of states joined in 1858–60. (1)
    Common trap: choosing 1861 for "largest number". 1861 is when the king was proclaimed; the map's joining years are 1858–60, 1860, 1866 and 1870.

    Note: the map marks 1858 and 1858–60, but the textbook's text says Sardinia-Piedmont defeated Austria in 1859, and its dates box gives 1859–1870 for the unification of Italy. Read the map as printed for this Activity; for dates, learn the text: 1859, 1860, 1861, 1866, 1870.

Activity p. 22Pulling on the boot2 marks

The artist has portrayed Garibaldi as holding on to the base of the boot, so that the King of Sardinia-Piedmont can enter it from the top. Look at the map of Italy once more. What statement is this caricature making?

Asked: the message of Fig. 15.

Concept used: Box 2 and Fig. 15 (Topic 7, section 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    The boot is Italy: the map of Italy is shaped like a boot; the king is stepping into it — he will rule Italy.

  2. 2

    Who does the work: Garibaldi, kneeling, holds the boot steady — his volunteers won the south (1860), and he then backed the king.

  3. 3

    The statement: Garibaldi made unification possible, but the King of Sardinia-Piedmont gets to "wear" Italy — the revolutionary serves the monarchy.

  4. Check: the answer links the boot to the map (as the question asks) and says who gains; "Garibaldi is a shoemaker" misses the symbol.

  5. Answer Garibaldi did the hard work of uniting Italy, but it was the King of Sardinia-Piedmont who took Italy as his own.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The boot is Italy, shaped like a boot on the map; the king stepping into it will rule Italy. (1)
    2. Garibaldi, kneeling to hold it, did the work of unification, yet the King of Sardinia-Piedmont gained the kingdom. (1)
    Common trap: saying Garibaldi became king. Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed king of united Italy in 1861.
Activity p. 24Veit's Germania and Box 33 marks

With the help of the chart in Box 3, identify the attributes of Veit's Germania and interpret the symbolic meaning of the painting. In an earlier allegorical rendering of 1836, Veit had portrayed the Kaiser's crown at the place where he has now located the broken chain. Explain the significance of this change.

Asked: (i) the attributes; (ii) the painting's meaning; (iii) the significance of the change.

Concept used: Box 3 and section 3 of Topic 9.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Attributes (Box 3): broken chains — being freed; breastplate with eagle — strength; crown of oak leaves — heroism; sword — readiness to fight; olive branch around the sword — willingness to make peace; black, red and gold tricolour — the liberal-nationalists' flag of 1848; rays of the rising sun — a new era.

  2. 2

    Meaning: a free, strong, heroic German nation, ready to fight yet willing to make peace, beginning a new era under the liberal flag — the hope of the Frankfurt parliament, where the banner hung.

  3. 3

    The change: the Kaiser's crown (rule by an emperor) was replaced by broken chains (freedom) — the nation was now imagined as freed from autocracy, not ruled by a monarch.

  4. Check: every meaning comes from Box 3; reading the olive branch as "victory" would contradict the chart.

  5. Answer Veit's Germania stands for a free, strong, peace-loving German nation entering a new era; replacing the Kaiser's crown with broken chains shows the shift from monarchy to liberal freedom.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Broken chains (freedom), eagle breastplate (strength), oak crown (heroism), sword (ready to fight), olive branch (peace), tricolour, rising sun. (1)
    2. They show a free, strong German nation, ready to fight yet willing to make peace, under the liberal flag of 1848. (1)
    3. Broken chains replacing the Kaiser's crown show hope of freedom from autocracy. (1)
    Common trap: listing the attributes but not explaining the change from 1836 to 1848.
Activity p. 24The fallen Germania3 marks

Describe what you see in Fig. 17. What historical events could Hübner be referring to in this allegorical vision of the nation?

Note: Hübner's painting is NCERT Fig. 18, "The fallen Germania" (1850); the textbook's Activity says "Fig. 17". This answer describes Fig. 18.

Asked: (i) a description of Hübner's painting; (ii) the events it may refer to.

Concept used: Section 4 of Topic 9; the Frankfurt parliament (Topic 6).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Description: Germania, the allegory of the German nation, lies fallen on the ground, her symbols lying beside her.

  2. 2

    The date: painted in 1850, two years after 1848.

  3. 3

    The events: the failure of the liberal revolution of 1848 — the Frankfurt parliament's crown rejected by the King of Prussia, the assembly disbanded by troops, and conservative forces suppressing the liberal movements.

  4. Check: the events must be before 1850 and about German liberal hopes; German unification (1871) came later and cannot be what a painting of 1850 refers to.

  5. Answer A fallen Germania, painted in 1850, mourns the crushing of the 1848 liberal dream of a united, free Germany.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Hübner's The fallen Germania (1850) shows the allegory of the German nation lying fallen on the ground. (1)
    2. It was painted just after the liberal hopes of 1848 for a united, constitutional Germany had failed. (1)
    3. It may refer to the King of Prussia rejecting the Frankfurt crown and troops disbanding the parliament. (1)
    Common trap: linking the picture to Bismarck's wars. Those came after 1850.
Activity p. 25Two seats in the Frankfurt parliament5 marks

Look once more at Fig. 10. Imagine you were a citizen of Frankfurt in March 1848 and were present during the proceedings of the parliament. How would you (a) as a man seated in the hall of deputies, and (b) as a woman observing from the galleries, relate to the banner of Germania hanging from the ceiling?

Note: the textbook dates the opening of the parliament to 18 May 1848; the Activity and the Fig. 17 caption say "March 1848".

Asked: two imagined reactions to Veit's banner.

Concept used: The Frankfurt parliament (Topic 6) and Veit's Germania (Topic 9).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    (a) A male deputy: he sees his own hopes in the banner — a free German nation (broken chains), strong and heroic (eagle, oak crown), under the liberal tricolour; he feels proud to be writing its constitution.

  2. 2

    (b) A woman in the gallery: women had formed associations, founded newspapers and joined demonstrations, yet were denied suffrage and could only observe.

  3. 3

    Her view: she sees the nation pictured as a woman above the hall, while real women are kept out of its decisions — she may feel the irony, like Louise Otto-Peters: "Liberty is indivisible".

  4. Check: the two answers must differ, because the two people had different rights; two identical proud reactions would miss the point of the question.

  5. Answer The man would feel the banner stood for his liberal hopes; the woman would see the irony of a female figure for a nation that denied women a vote.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. (a) As a deputy, I would see my hopes in the banner: a free German nation, with broken chains and the liberal black, red and gold flag. (1)
    2. Its oak crown and sword would make me proud that we were drafting a constitution for a strong, heroic nation. (1)
    3. (b) As a woman, I had joined associations and demonstrations, yet I was denied the vote and allowed only to observe. (1)
    4. I would see the irony: the nation is pictured as a woman above the hall, while women are kept out of its decisions. (1)
    5. Like Louise Otto-Peters, I would feel that liberty is indivisible and must include women. (1)
    Common trap: giving the woman the vote. Women were denied suffrage and admitted only as observers.

Write in brief Q1 (a)Giuseppe Mazzini3 marks

Write a note on: a) Guiseppe Mazzini

The textbook's exercise spells the name "Guiseppe"; the chapter text spells it Giuseppe.

Asked: a short note on Mazzini.

Concept used: Mazzini (Topic 4, section 5) and Italy unified (Topic 7).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Life: born in Genoa in 1805; a member of the Carbonari; exiled in 1831 for attempting a revolution in Liguria.

  2. 2

    Societies: founded Young Italy (Marseilles) and Young Europe (Berne); secret societies on his model spread to Germany, France, Switzerland and Poland.

  3. 3

    Ideas: nations are the natural units of mankind; Italy must be a single unified republic; Metternich called him "the most dangerous enemy of our social order".

  4. Check: the note covers who he was, what he did, what he believed; it should not say he became ruler of Italy — he was exiled after his attempted revolution in Liguria (1831), and the revolutionary uprisings of 1831 and 1848 failed.

  5. Answer Mazzini was an Italian revolutionary who founded Young Italy and Young Europe and wanted a united Italian republic.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Born in Genoa (1805), Mazzini joined the Carbonari and was exiled in 1831 after attempting a revolution in Liguria. (1)
    2. He founded Young Italy in Marseilles and Young Europe in Berne; similar societies spread across Europe. (1)
    3. He believed nations were natural units and Italy must be one republic; Metternich called him "the most dangerous enemy of our social order". (1)
    Common trap: mixing Mazzini with Garibaldi. Garibaldi led the Red Shirts; Mazzini founded the societies.
Write in brief Q1 (b)Count Camillo de Cavour3 marks

Write a note on: b) Count Camillo de Cavour

Asked: a short note on Cavour.

Concept used: Italy unified (Topic 7, section 3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Position: Chief Minister of Sardinia-Piedmont, who led the movement to unify the regions of Italy.

  2. 2

    Character: neither a revolutionary nor a democrat; like many of the wealthy, educated elite, he spoke French better than Italian.

  4. Check: Cavour's tool is diplomacy, not volunteers (Garibaldi) or secret societies (Mazzini).

  5. Answer Cavour, Sardinia-Piedmont's chief minister, unified Italy by diplomacy and war, not revolution.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Cavour, Chief Minister of Sardinia-Piedmont, led the movement to unify the regions of Italy. (1)
    2. He was neither a revolutionary nor a democrat, and spoke French better than Italian. (1)
    3. Through a tactful diplomatic alliance with France, Sardinia-Piedmont defeated the Austrian forces in 1859. (1)
    Common trap: calling Cavour a republican. He served the monarchy of Sardinia-Piedmont.
Write in brief Q1 (c)The Greek war of independence3 marks

Write a note on: c) The Greek war of independence

Asked: a short note on the war.

Concept used: Topic 5, section 3.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Background: Greece had been part of the Ottoman Empire since the fifteenth century; revolutionary nationalism sparked a struggle that began in 1821.

  2. 2

    Support: Greeks in exile and West Europeans who admired ancient Greek culture; poets and artists praised Greece as the cradle of European civilisation; Lord Byron raised funds, went to fight, and died of fever in 1824.

  3. 3

Discuss Q1The 1848 revolution of the liberals5 marks

Explain what is meant by the 1848 revolution of the liberals. What were the political, social and economic ideas supported by the liberals?

Asked: (i) the meaning of the 1848 liberal revolution; (ii) the liberals' political, social and economic ideas.

Concept used: Topic 6, section 4; Topic 3, section 3.

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    Meaning: in 1848, parallel to revolts of the poor, the educated middle classes led a revolution; in France the monarch abdicated and a republic with universal male suffrage was proclaimed.

  2. 2

    Where nation-states did not exist (Germany, Italy, Poland, Austria-Hungary), they combined constitutionalism with national unification — e.g. the Frankfurt parliament.

  3. 3

    Political ideas: government by consent; end of autocracy and clerical privileges; a constitution and representative government through parliament; freedom of the press and of association.

  4. 4

    Social ideas: freedom for the individual and equality before the law (though votes only for property-owning men).

  5. 5

    Economic ideas: freedom of markets; abolition of state-imposed restrictions on the movement of goods and capital; inviolability of private property.

  6. Check: the answer must have a definition plus all three spheres; leaving out economics loses a mark.

  7. Answer A middle-class revolution of 1848 for a constitutional nation-state; politically for consent, constitution and parliament, socially for individual freedom and legal equality, economically for free markets.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. In 1848, parallel to revolts of the poor, the educated middle classes led a revolution; in France a republic with universal male suffrage was proclaimed. (1)
    2. Where nation-states did not exist, as in Germany and Italy, they combined demands for a constitution with national unification. (1)
    3. Political ideas: government by consent, an end to autocracy, a constitution, parliament, and freedom of press and association. (1)
    4. Social ideas: freedom for the individual and equality of all before the law. (1)
    5. Economic ideas: free markets, no state restrictions on goods and capital, and the inviolability of private property. (1)
    Common trap: writing only about hunger, barricades and national workshops. The question asks about the middle-class revolution: its demands (constitution, nation-state, free press, association) and the liberals' ideas.

How was the history of nationalism in Britain unlike the rest of Europe?

Asked: how Britain's road differed.

Concept used: Topic 8 (sections 1–3).

  1. 1

    Understand it:

    No upheaval: in Britain the nation-state was not the result of a sudden upheaval or revolution but of a long-drawn-out process.

  2. 2

    One people expands: there was no British nation before the eighteenth century; the English nation, growing in wealth and power, extended its influence through parliament (power seized from the monarchy in 1688).

    Scotland: the Act of Union (1707) let England impose its influence; Scottish culture and institutions were suppressed; Highlanders were forbidden Gaelic and national dress.

    Ireland: English-backed Protestant dominance over a Catholic country; after Wolfe Tone's failed revolt (1798), forced incorporation in 1801.

    1. Unlike France, Germany or Italy, Britain's nation-state came not from a sudden revolution or war but from a long-drawn-out process. (1)

    4. Catholic Ireland was dominated through English-backed Protestants and, after Wolfe Tone's failed revolt (1798), forcibly incorporated in 1801. (1)
    5. The Union Jack, the anthem and English were promoted; the older nations survived only as subordinate partners. (1)
    Common trap: writing a history of Britain without the word "unlike". Each point should show how Britain differed.
