Topic 10 of 10

Nationalism and imperialism

About 30 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain how nationalism became a narrow creed after 1871 and why the Balkans became explosive.
  • Explain how nationalism aligned with imperialism led to 1914, and how anti-imperial movements took up the idea of the nation-state (Fig. 20).

In 1848 Sorrieu dreamed of nations marching side by side in brotherhood. By the end of the century the nations of Europe were glaring at one another over borders, colonies and battleships. In one corner of the continent — the Balkans — small peoples fought for freedom from a crumbling empire, quarrelled with each other over land, and became pawns of the great powers. In 1914 Europe went to war.

  1. A narrow creed

By the last quarter of the nineteenth century, nationalism no longer kept the idealistic liberal-democratic sentiment of the first half of the century. It became a narrow creed with limited ends:

  • nationalist groups became increasingly intolerant of each other and ever ready to go to war;
  • the major European powers manipulated the nationalist aspirations of subject peoples in Europe to further their own imperialist aims.

  1. The Balkans: the most serious source of tension after 1871

What and where. The Balkans had great geographical and ethnic variation. Today the region covers Romania, Bulgaria, Albania, Greece, Macedonia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Slovenia, Serbia and Montenegro. Its people were broadly called the Slavs, and much of the region was ruled by the Ottoman Empire.

Why it became explosive — the chain:

Link What happened
1. Two forces together The spread of romantic nationalism in the Balkans together with the disintegration of the Ottoman Empire made the region very explosive
2. A weak empire All through the nineteenth century the Ottoman Empire tried to strengthen itself through modernisation and internal reforms, with very little success
3. Breaking away One by one, its European subject nationalities broke away and declared independence
4. History as a claim The Balkan peoples based their claims on nationality and used history to prove they had once been independent and later subjugated by foreign powers — so they saw their struggles as attempts to win back long-lost independence
5. Jealous neighbours As the Slavic nationalities struggled to define their identity, the area saw intense conflict; the Balkan states were fiercely jealous of each other, each hoping to gain more territory at the expense of the others
6. Big-power rivalry The Balkans also became the scene of big power rivalry: there was intense rivalry over trade and colonies and naval and military might; each power — Russia, Germany, England, Austro-Hungary — wanted to counter the hold of the others over the Balkans and extend its own control
7. War This led to a series of wars in the region and finally the First World War

The dates box adds: 1905 — Slav nationalism gathers force in the Habsburg and Ottoman Empires.

  1. Nationalism, imperialism and 1914

Nationalism, aligned with imperialism, led Europe to disaster in 1914.

Reading NCERT Fig. 20 — A map celebrating the British Empire. At the top, angels carry the banner of freedom; in the foreground Britannia — the symbol of the British nation — sits triumphantly over the globe; the colonies are shown through images of tigers, elephants, forests and primitive people. What it tells us: the domination of the world was shown as the basis of Britain's national pride — nationalism had joined hands with empire. Its point of view: the colonised peoples appear only as animals, forests and "primitive" figures, as the coloniser saw them.

  1. The idea spreads beyond Europe

Meanwhile, many countries colonised by the European powers in the nineteenth century began to oppose imperial domination:

  • the anti-imperial movements everywhere were nationalist — they struggled to form independent nation-states, inspired by a sense of collective national unity forged in confrontation with imperialism;
  • no one simply copied European nationalism — each people developed its own variety of nationalism;
  • but the idea that societies should be organised into "nation-states" came to be accepted as natural and universal.

(You will see one such variety in Chapter 2, Nationalism in India.)

Worked example 1

From the Balkans to 1914 (an answer plan)

Question. Plan a 5-mark answer: "Local quarrels in one corner of Europe became a European war." Explain with reference to the Balkans.

Asked: the chain from local Balkan conflict to the First World War.

Concept used: The Balkans chain (section 2). Why it applies: the statement describes the chain from local to European.

  1. 1

    Local cause 1: romantic nationalism spread among the Slavic peoples while the Ottoman Empire disintegrated; reforms had little success.

  2. 2

    Local cause 2: subject nationalities broke away one by one, using history to claim long-lost independence.

  3. 3

    Local quarrel: the Balkan states were fiercely jealous, each wanting territory at the others' expense.

  4. 4

    Europe drawn in: Russia, Germany, England and Austro-Hungary, rivals over trade, colonies and military might, each sought to extend control over the Balkans.

  5. 5

    Result: a series of wars in the region and finally the First World War.

  6. Check: the five points move from local to European in order; if the big powers came first, the "local quarrel became a European war" link would be lost.

  7. Answer Balkan nationalism + Ottoman collapse → breakaway states → jealous rivals → great powers step in → wars → 1914.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. Romantic nationalism spread among the Slavic peoples of the Balkans as the Ottoman Empire, despite reforms, disintegrated. (1)
    2. One by one its subject nationalities broke away, using history to claim they were winning back long-lost independence. (1)
    3. The new Balkan states were fiercely jealous, each hoping to gain territory at the others' expense. (1)
    4. Russia, Germany, England and Austro-Hungary, rivals over trade, colonies and military might, each tried to extend control there. (1)
    5. These rivalries led to a series of wars in the region and finally to the First World War. (1)
    Common trap: writing that the Balkans caused the Second World War. The textbook links them to the First World War (1914).

Worked example 2

Reading the map of the British Empire

Question. In NCERT Fig. 20, why is Britannia shown sitting over the globe, and what does the picture tell us about nationalism by the late nineteenth century? (2 marks)

Asked: the meaning of Britannia's position; what it shows about nationalism.

Concept used: Section 3.

  1. 1

    The picture: Britannia, the symbol of the British nation, sits triumphantly over the globe, with colonies shown as tigers, elephants, forests and "primitive" people.

  2. 2

    The meaning: world domination had become the basis of Britain's national pride — nationalism had become aligned with imperialism.

  3. Check: the answer connects the position (over the globe) to empire; "Britannia is a goddess of peace" ignores the triumphant pose and the colonies.

  4. Answer Britannia over the globe shows that ruling the world was the basis of British national pride — nationalism had joined imperialism.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Britannia, symbol of the British nation, sits triumphantly over the globe; the colonies appear as tigers, elephants and forests. (1)
    2. It shows that world domination had become the basis of national pride: nationalism was now aligned with imperialism. (1)
    Common trap: calling the figure Marianne or Germania. In Fig. 20 the figure is Britannia.

Common mistakes!

  • "The Balkans were tense only because the Ottomans ruled them."

    The textbook gives romantic nationalism + Ottoman disintegration + jealousy among Balkan states + big-power rivalry.

    Write the whole chain.

  • "The Balkan conflict led to the Second World War."

    It led to a series of wars and finally the First World War (1914).

    Write 1914.

  • "Late-19th-century nationalism was still liberal and democratic."

    It became a narrow creed, intolerant and ready for war.

    Contrast first half (liberal) with last quarter (narrow).

  • "Colonies copied European nationalism."

    Nowhere replicated; each developed its own variety.

    Write: own variety; nation-state idea universal.

Key points

  • Last quarter of the 19th century: nationalism became a narrow creed; groups intolerant and ready for war; great powers manipulated subject peoples for imperialist aims.
  • Balkans (Romania, Bulgaria, Albania, Greece, Macedonia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Slovenia, Serbia, Montenegro; the Slavs): the most serious source of tension after 1871. Romantic nationalism + Ottoman disintegration → nationalities break away, citing history → states fiercely jealous → big-power rivalry (Russia, Germany, England, Austro-Hungary) → series of wars → First World War.
  • Nationalism aligned with imperialism led Europe to disaster in 1914; Fig. 20 — Britannia over the globe: world domination as national pride.
  • Anti-imperial movements were nationalist, each with its own variety; the nation-state became natural and universal.
