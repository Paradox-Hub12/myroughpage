Topic 2 of 10

The French Revolution and Napoleon

About 35 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why the French Revolution was the first clear expression of nationalism (transfer of sovereignty).
  • Describe the measures that created a collective identity among the French people.
  • Explain how the idea of the nation spread abroad (mission, Jacobin clubs, armies).
  • Describe Napoleon's administrative reforms (Civil Code 1804) and the mixed reactions to French rule.

Before 1789 a French peasant was a subject of the king. His village spoke its own dialect, his town had its own weights for flour, and every few miles a toll-gate charged him duty on his goods. Within a few years he was told he was a citizen, that France was his fatherland, that there was one flag, one assembly, one set of laws and one language for all. A nation was being made — on purpose.

Quick reminder (Class 9, The French Revolution). In 1789 France was ruled by an absolute monarch. The Revolution ended his absolute power; the Jacobins were a radical political club; later Napoleon took power and made himself emperor.

  1. The first clear expression of nationalism (1789)

France in 1789 was already a full-fledged territorial state, ruled by an absolute monarch. What the Revolution changed was who held sovereignty: it passed from the monarchy to a body of French citizens. From now on, the Revolution said, the people would make up the nation and decide its destiny. This is why it is called the first clear expression of nationalism.

  1. How the revolutionaries built a collective identity

From the very beginning, the revolutionaries brought in measures to make the French feel like one people:

Measure What it did
La patrie (the fatherland) and le citoyen (the citizen) Stressed a united community enjoying equal rights under a constitution
A new French flag, the tricolour Replaced the former royal standard
The Estates General Elected by the body of active citizens and renamed the National Assembly
New hymns, oaths, martyrs commemorated All in the name of the nation
A centralised administrative system Made uniform laws for all citizens in its territory
Internal customs duties and dues abolished; a uniform system of weights and measures One economy instead of many local ones
Regional dialects discouraged; French as spoken and written in Paris Became the common language of the nation

Memory hook — "Father, Flag, Forum, Feelings, Form, Freight, French": fatherland/citizen · tricolour · National Assembly · hymns, oaths and martyrs · uniform laws · no internal customs, one system of weights · Paris French.

  1. Carrying the nation abroad

  • The revolutionaries declared it was the mission and destiny of the French nation to liberate the peoples of Europe from despotism — to help other peoples become nations.
  • Students and other educated middle-class people in many European cities formed Jacobin clubs. These clubs prepared the ground for the French armies, which in the 1790s marched into Holland, Belgium, Switzerland and much of Italy.
  • With the revolutionary wars, the French armies carried the idea of nationalism abroad.

Reading NCERT Fig. 2. The cover of a German almanac designed by the journalist Andreas Rebmann in 1798: the storming of the French Bastille is placed next to a similar fortress standing for despotic rule in the German province of Kassel, with the slogan "The people must seize their own freedom!". Rebmann lived in Mainz and belonged to a German Jacobin group. What it tells us: French revolutionary ideas inspired Germans to demand their own freedom.

  1. Napoleon: reforms without democracy

Napoleon destroyed democracy in France by a return to monarchy — but in the administrative field he used revolutionary principles to make the system more rational and efficient.

The Civil Code of 1804 (the Napoleonic Code):

  1. did away with all privileges based on birth;
  2. established equality before the law;
  3. secured the right to property.

The Code was exported to the regions under French control. In the Dutch Republic, Switzerland, Italy and Germany, Napoleon also:

  • simplified administrative divisions;
  • abolished the feudal system and freed peasants from serfdom and manorial dues;
  • in the towns, removed guild restrictions;
  • improved transport and communication.

Peasants, artisans, workers and new businessmen enjoyed a new-found freedom. Businessmen and small producers began to see that uniform laws, standardised weights and measures and a common national currency would make it easier to move and exchange goods and capital across regions — an idea that comes back in Topic 3.

New words. Serfdom — peasants tied to a lord's land; manorial dues — payments to the lord of the estate; guild — an association of craftsmen that controlled a trade.

  1. Mixed reactions to French rule

At first Later
In Holland, Switzerland and cities such as Brussels, Mainz, Milan and Warsaw, the French armies were welcomed as harbingers (messengers) of liberty Enthusiasm turned to hostility: the new administration did not come with political freedom
Increased taxation, censorship and forced conscription into the French armies seemed to outweigh the advantages of the administrative changes

Reading NCERT Fig. 4 — The Planting of Tree of Liberty in Zweibrücken, Germany (a colour print by Karl Kaspar Fritz, a German painter): the French soldiers, dressed in blue, white and red, appear as oppressors. They take a peasant's cart, trouble young women and force a peasant down on his knees. The plaque on the Tree of Liberty says "Take freedom and equality from us, the model of humanity" — a sarcastic dig at the French claim to bring liberty.

Reading NCERT Fig. 5 — The courier of Rhineland loses all that he has on his way home from Leipzig: Napoleon is drawn as a postman heading back to France after his defeat at Leipzig in 1813. Each letter dropping from his bag is labelled with a territory he lost.

Worked example 1

Unity at home or the idea abroad?

Question. Sort these into two groups — (A) steps that made the French one people and (B) ways the idea of the nation reached other countries: (i) the tricolour replaced the royal standard; (ii) students set up Jacobin clubs in other European cities; (iii) French as spoken in Paris became the common language; (iv) French armies moved into Holland, Belgium, Switzerland and much of Italy.

Asked: which items belong to group A and which to group B.

Concept used: Sections 2 and 3 of this topic. Why it applies: section 2 lists measures inside France; section 3 lists how the idea travelled.

  1. 1

    (i) Tricolour — a symbol for the French themselves → A.

  2. 2

    (ii) Jacobin clubs abroad — set up in other cities by students and the educated middle classes → B.

  3. 3

    (iii) Paris French — one language inside France → A.

  4. 4

    (iv) French armies — carried the idea of nationalism abroad in the 1790s → B.

  5. Check: group A items all happen inside France; group B items all happen outside France. If a Jacobin club in Mainz were put in A, the "inside France" test would fail.

  6. Answer A: (i), (iii). B: (ii), (iv).

    Common trap: thinking the Jacobin clubs abroad were French government offices. They were set up by local students and educated middle-class people.

Worked example 2

A businessman of Mainz

Question. Imagine a businessman in Mainz under Napoleon's rule. Explain one way he gained and two reasons he might later turn against French rule. (3 marks)

Asked: one gain and two complaints.

Concept used: Sections 4 and 5 of this topic. Why it applies: Mainz is one of the cities that first welcomed the French.

  1. 1

    Gain: guild restrictions were removed and transport improved; businessmen saw that uniform laws, standardised weights and measures and a common currency would help trade across regions.

  2. 2

    Complaint 1: the new administration did not bring political freedom, and censorship limited what people could say.

  3. 3

    Complaint 2: increased taxation and forced conscription into the French armies outweighed the gains.

  4. Check: the gain must be economic or administrative and the complaints must come from the textbook's list (taxation, censorship, conscription, no political freedom). "He lost his right to vote" is not in the textbook and would not earn a mark.

  5. Answer He gained freer trade and better transport, but disliked the lack of political freedom with censorship, and the higher taxes and forced conscription.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Gain: guild restrictions ended and transport improved; uniform laws, weights and measures and a common currency would ease trade. (1)
    2. Complaint: the new administration brought no political freedom, and censorship controlled what people could say. (1)
    3. Complaint: increased taxation and forced conscription into the French armies outweighed the advantages. (1)
    Common trap: writing that Napoleon brought democracy. He destroyed democracy in France; his changes were administrative.

Worked example 3

Reading a sarcastic plaque

Question. In NCERT Fig. 4, the plaque on the Tree of Liberty reads "Take freedom and equality from us, the model of humanity." Why is this called sarcastic? (2 marks)

Asked: why the words mean the opposite of what they say.

Concept used: Section 5 (Fig. 4). Why it applies: the picture shows what the soldiers are doing while the plaque speaks of freedom.

  1. 1

    What the picture shows: French soldiers seizing a cart, harassing women and forcing a peasant to his knees — acting as oppressors.

  2. 2

    Why it is sarcastic: the French claimed to bring freedom and equality as liberators; the painter mocks that claim by placing the proud words beside scenes of oppression.

  3. Check: the answer links the words to the actions in the picture. An answer that only repeats the plaque has explained nothing.

  4. Answer The plaque praises French "freedom and equality" while the soldiers oppress the people, so it mocks the French claim to be liberators.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The print shows French soldiers seizing a cart, harassing women and humiliating a peasant — acting as oppressors. (1)
    2. So the plaque's praise of French freedom and equality mocks their claim to be liberators who opposed monarchy. (1)
    Common trap: taking the plaque at face value as German praise for the French. Read the plaque with the actions.

Common mistakes!

  • "Napoleon spread democracy across Europe."

    He destroyed democracy in France by returning to monarchy; his reforms were administrative.

    Write: rational and efficient administration, not democracy.

  • "The Civil Code gave equal votes to men and women."

    It gave equality before the law, not the vote; it reduced women to the status of a minor.

    Separate equality before the law from suffrage.

  • "Everyone in the conquered lands hated the French from the start."

    At first many welcomed them as harbingers of liberty; hostility came later.

    Write at first welcomed → later hostile, with the reasons.

  • Dating the Civil Code to 1815 or the Revolution to 1804.

    Revolution 1789; Civil Code 1804; Vienna 1815.

    Learn 1789 → 1804 → 1815.

Key points

  • 1789: sovereignty passed from the monarchy to the citizens — the first clear expression of nationalism.
  • Measures for a collective identity: la patrie and le citoyen; the tricolour; the National Assembly; hymns, oaths and martyrs; centralised administration and uniform laws; no internal customs, uniform weights and measures; Paris French as the common language.
  • The mission to liberate Europe's peoples; Jacobin clubs abroad; French armies in Holland, Belgium, Switzerland and much of Italy (1790s).
  • Napoleon destroyed democracy but made administration rational and efficient: Civil Code 1804 (no privileges by birth, equality before the law, right to property); feudalism and serfdom abolished, guild restrictions removed, transport improved.
  • Reactions: welcomed at first, then hostility — no political freedom, taxation, censorship, forced conscription (Figs 2, 4, 5).
