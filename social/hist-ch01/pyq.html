How this chapter is asked

In the 2026 board papers this chapter was the 5-mark History long answer in 12 of the 15 sets (the three 32/2 sets asked it for 3 marks). Its 1-mark questions are mostly arrange-in-order, assertion–reason, identify the person, place or picture, and statement-based items. The sample paper for 2026-27 gives it a 1-mark item and a 3-mark answer (4 marks); the 2025-26 paper gave it a 1-mark item and a 5-mark answer (6 marks).

About the marks

The sample-paper answers follow the official marking schemes. No marking scheme is published for the board papers, so their mark splits are our marking points, written to the CBSE word limits (2 marks — up to 40 words, 3 marks — up to 60, 5 marks — up to 120, case study — up to 100). Where an official question is printed with a slip or has no correct option, it is quoted exactly and a note explains the textbook answer.

CBSE Sample Question Paper 2026-27, Section A, Q11 mark

Question: Most conservative regimes set up in 1815 introduced strict censorship laws. What was the primary purpose of these laws?
a. To regulate information written about the rulers and their policies.
b. To control the growing demand for modernising the army.
c. To restrict the spread of ideas of liberty and freedom expressed through newspapers and other publications.
d. To prevent the circulation of views suggesting that modernisation could strengthen traditional institutions.
Options: A. Only a and b B. Only a and c C. Only b and d D. Only c and d

Asked: which two statements give the purpose of the censorship laws.

Concept used: How the conservative regimes ruled (Topic 4).

  1. 1

    a — true: the regimes did not tolerate criticism and dissent of their rule.

  2. 2

    c — true: censorship controlled what was said in newspapers, books, plays and songs that reflected the ideas of liberty and freedom of the French Revolution.

  3. 3

    b — false: the conservatives themselves wanted a modern army; they did not need to silence that demand.

  4. 4

    d — false: the idea that modernisation could strengthen traditional institutions was the conservatives' own view.

  5. Check: only one option pairs a with c, so B is the only fit.

    Common trap: choosing D because c is true. D also needs d, which describes the conservatives' own belief.
B. Only a and c
1 mark
CBSE Sample Question Paper 2026-27, Section A, Q6A3 marks

Question: 'The female figures become an allegory of the nation during the 19th century in Europe.' Elaborate on the statement with examples.

Asked: how female figures became allegories of the nation, with examples.

Concept used: Visualising the nation (Topic 9).

  1. 1

    Why a female figure: artists personified the nation as a woman who stood for no particular woman; she gave the abstract idea of the nation a concrete form.

  2. 2

    France: she was named Marianne, a popular Christian name for a people's nation, with the red cap, tricolour and cockade of Liberty and the Republic.

  3. 3

    Germany: Germania wears a crown of oak leaves, as the German oak stands for heroism.

  4. Check: the answer has the idea and two examples — three points for three marks, as the scheme lists.

    Common trap: saying the figures were real queens. The scheme stresses that the female form belonged to no particular woman.
  1. Artists personified the nation as a female figure, no real woman, giving the abstract idea of the nation a concrete form. (1)
  1. In France she was named Marianne; her red cap, tricolour and cockade came from Liberty and the Republic. (1)
  1. Germania became the allegory of the German nation, wearing a crown of oak leaves, as oak stands for heroism. (1)
3 marks

Note on the official texts: the question prints "The female figures become" (read: became). The marking scheme's point 1 says the female form "was an abstract idea, which gave the nation a concrete form"; your textbook says the female form gave the abstract idea of the nation a concrete form. The answer above follows the textbook.

Question: Assess the role of the Greek War of Independence in shaping nationalist consciousness among educated Europeans in the nineteenth century.

Asked: how the Greek war affected educated Europeans' national feeling.

Concept used: The Greek war of independence (Topic 5).

  1. 1

    The struggle: Greece, Ottoman since the fifteenth century, began its fight in 1821, sparked by revolutionary nationalism.

  2. 2

    Europe mobilised: exiled Greeks and West Europeans who admired ancient Greek culture helped; poets and artists praised Greece as the cradle of European civilisation and mobilised public opinion.

  3. 3

  4. Check: "assess the role" needs the effect on Europe (point 2), not only the Greek events.

    Common trap: dating independence to 1821. 1821 is the start; 1832 is recognition.
  1. Greece, Ottoman-ruled since the fifteenth century, began a struggle for independence in 1821, inspired by revolutionary nationalism. (1)
  1. Exiled Greeks and West Europeans who admired ancient Greek culture backed it; poets and artists hailed Greece as the cradle of European civilisation. (1)
  1. Byron raised funds and died in 1824; the Treaty of Constantinople (1832) recognised Greek independence. (1)

CBSE Sample Question Paper 2025-26, Section A, Q7A5 marks

Question: 'In Britain the formation of the nation-state was not the result of a sudden upheaval or revolution but was the result of a long-drawn-out process.' Analyze this statement with suitable reasons.

Asked: reasons and steps showing Britain's slow road.

Concept used: The strange case of Britain (Topic 8).

  1. 1

    Identities: before the eighteenth century people were English, Welsh, Scot or Irish.

  2. 2

    Scotland: the Act of Union (1707) let England impose its influence; Scottish culture and institutions were suppressed.

  3. 3

    Highlanders: forbidden Gaelic and national dress; many driven out.

  4. 4

    Ireland: English-backed Protestant dominance; Catholic revolts suppressed; forcibly incorporated in 1801.

  5. 5

    Symbols: Union Jack, anthem and English promoted; older nations subordinate partners.

  6. Check: the scheme's five points are all steps over time — which is what "long-drawn-out" means.

    Common trap: describing a British revolution. The statement says the opposite.
  1. There was no single upheaval: the primary identities in the British Isles were ethnic — English, Welsh, Scot or Irish. (1)
  1. The Act of Union (1707) formed the United Kingdom of Great Britain, letting England impose its influence on Scotland, whose culture and institutions were suppressed. (1)
  1. Scottish Highlanders were forbidden their Gaelic language and national dress, and many were driven out of their homeland. (1)
  1. England helped Protestants dominate Catholic Ireland, suppressed revolts, and forcibly incorporated Ireland in 1801. (1)
  1. The Union Jack, the national anthem and English were promoted; the older nations became subordinate partners. (1) — 5 marks (one mark per point, as the scheme lists five points)
CBSE Sample Question Paper 2025-26, Section A, Q7B5 marks

Question: 'The Treaty of Vienna was drawn up in 1815 with the object of undoing most of the changes that had come about in Europe during the Napoleonic wars.' Highlight the significant provisions of this treaty.

Asked: the provisions of the treaty.

Concept used: The Treaty of Vienna (Topic 4).

  1. 1

    France: Bourbons restored; annexed territories lost.

  2. 2

    Border states: the Netherlands (with Belgium) in the north; Genoa added to Piedmont in the south.

  3. 3

    Prussia and Austria: Prussia — western territories (and part of Saxony); Austria — northern Italy.

  4. 4

    Germany and Poland: the 39-state confederation untouched; Russia — part of Poland; Prussia — part of Saxony.

  5. 5

    Aim: restore the monarchies; a conservative order.

  6. Check: the five points match the five in the marking scheme.

    Common trap: writing that the treaty created a united Germany. It left the confederation of 39 states untouched.
  1. The Bourbon dynasty, deposed during the French Revolution, was restored, and France lost the territories annexed under Napoleon. (1)
  1. States were set up on France's borders: the Netherlands, including Belgium, in the north; Genoa added to Piedmont in the south. (1)
  1. Prussia gained important territories on its western frontiers; Austria gained control of northern Italy. (1)
  1. The 39-state German confederation was left untouched; Russia got part of Poland and Prussia a portion of Saxony. (1)
  1. Its main intention was to restore the monarchies overthrown by Napoleon and create a new conservative order. (1) — 5 marks (one mark per point, as the scheme lists five points)

Board 2026, 32/1/1 Q7 (B)5 marks

Question: "Ideas of national unity in the early nineteenth century Europe were closely allied to the ideology of liberalism." Justify the statement with suitable arguments.

Asked: how liberalism and national unity went together.

Concept used: Liberal nationalism (Topic 3).

  1. 1

    Who: the educated, liberal middle classes spread ideas of national unity after the abolition of aristocratic privileges.

  2. 2

    Political: government by consent, constitution, parliament — a nation of citizens.

  3. 3

    Economic: free markets; 39 states with own currencies and 11 customs barriers → the Zollverein (1834), economic nationalism.

  4. Check: the answer must show how liberal ideas led to unity, ending with the Zollverein link.

    Common trap: describing only what liberalism meant, without the link to national unity.
  1. The educated, liberal middle classes spread ideas of national unity following the abolition of aristocratic privileges. (1)
  1. Politically, liberals wanted government by consent, a constitution and representative government — a nation of equal citizens. (1)
  1. Economically, they wanted free markets, but the 39 German states had their own currencies, weights and measures. (1)
  1. A merchant from Hamburg to Nuremberg crossed 11 customs barriers, so the commercial classes demanded a unified economic territory. (1)
  1. The Zollverein (1834), led by Prussia, abolished tariffs and cut currencies to two; economic nationalism strengthened national feeling. (1)

Board 2026, 32/1/2 Q7 (A)5 marks

Question: "Culture played an important role in creating the idea of the nation." Examine the statement in the context of eighteenth century Europe.

Asked: the role of culture in creating the nation.

Concept used: The Romantic imagination (Topic 5); allegories (Topic 9).

  1. 1

    Romanticism: emotion and a shared cultural past; Herder (1744–1803) — das volk.

  2. 2

    Folk culture: the Grimms' tales; vernacular for illiterate audiences.

  3. 3

    Music and language: Poland — Kurpinski; Polish in church.

  4. 4

    Art: Delacroix's Massacre at Chios.

  5. Check: five cultural points, each with an example.

    Common trap: writing about wars. The question is about culture.
  1. Romantic artists and poets criticised the glorification of reason and science, stressing emotion and a shared cultural past as the basis of a nation. (1)
  1. Herder (1744–1803) said true German culture lay among the common people, das volk, in folk songs, poetry and dances. (1)
  1. The Grimm brothers collected folktales and compiled a German dictionary to oppose French domination and build a German identity. (1)
  1. In Poland, Kurpinski's music and Polish in church kept national feeling alive against Russian rule. (1)
  1. Delacroix's The Massacre at Chios won European sympathy for the Greeks by appealing to emotions. (1)

Question: "Giuseppe Mazzini played a major role in the unification of Italy." Examine the statement.

Asked: what Mazzini did, and how far he shaped unification ("examine").

Concept used: Mazzini (Topic 4); Italy unified (Topic 7).

    Ideas and societies: Carbonari, exile 1831, Young Italy (Marseilles), Young Europe (Berne); a unitary Italian republic.

  3. 3

    Limit: the revolutionary uprisings of 1831 and 1848 failed; Italy was made a kingdom by Cavour and Victor Emmanuel II.

  4. Check: "examine" needs both the role and its limit.

  1. Mazzini, born in Genoa in 1805, joined the Carbonari and was exiled in 1831 after attempting a revolution in Liguria. (1)
  1. He founded Young Italy in Marseilles and Young Europe in Berne to spread his goal of a unified Italian republic. (1)
  1. He taught that nations are the natural units of mankind; similar societies spread across Europe, and Metternich called him the most dangerous enemy of the social order. (1)
  1. His ideas inspired others: Garibaldi joined Young Italy in 1833. (1)
  1. But the revolutionary uprisings of 1831 and 1848 failed; Italy was finally unified as a kingdom by Cavour and Victor Emmanuel II. (1)
