Topic 4 of 10

Conservatism after 1815 and the revolutionaries

About 30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain conservatism and the aims and terms of the Treaty of Vienna (1815).
  • Describe how the conservative regimes worked (autocracy, censorship) and read Fig. 6.
  • Describe the secret societies and Mazzini's ideas and work.

In 1815 representatives of the powers that had defeated Napoleon met in Vienna to put Europe "back in order". They redrew borders, brought back old royal families, and agreed that ideas like liberty and the nation were dangerous. Newspapers were watched, plays were checked and songs were censored. So the young people who believed in those ideas went underground — into secret societies with passwords and oaths.

Quick reminder (atlas)

Places in this topic, from north to south: the Netherlands and Belgium, Prussia and Saxony (in today's Germany), Poland, Austria, Genoa and Piedmont (north-west Italy), Liguria (the coast around Genoa), Marseilles (south France), Berne (Switzerland).

  1. A new conservatism after 1815

After Napoleon's defeat in 1815, European governments were driven by conservatism.

Conservatives believed that established, traditional institutions of state and society — the monarchy, the Church, social hierarchies, property and the family — should be preserved.

But most of them did not want to go back to the society of pre-revolutionary days. From Napoleon's changes they had learnt that modernisation could strengthen traditional institutions like the monarchy: a modern army, an efficient bureaucracy, a dynamic economy, and the abolition of feudalism and serfdom could make the autocratic monarchies of Europe stronger.

New word. Conservatism — a political philosophy that stresses tradition, established institutions and customs, and prefers gradual development to quick change.

  1. The Treaty of Vienna, 1815

Napoleon had been beaten by four powers together — Britain, Russia, Prussia and Austria. In 1815 they met at Vienna to settle the future of Europe, at a Congress hosted by the Austrian Chancellor Duke Metternich. Their Treaty of Vienna set out to undo most of the changes of the Napoleonic wars.

Provision Detail
Old dynasty back The Bourbon dynasty, deposed during the French Revolution, was restored to power
France cut back France lost the territories it had annexed under Napoleon
A ring of states around France (to stop future French expansion) North: the kingdom of the Netherlands, which included Belgium. South: Genoa was added to Piedmont
Prussia Got important new territories on its western frontiers, and a portion of Saxony
Austria Got control of northern Italy
Russia Got part of Poland
Germany The German confederation of 39 states set up by Napoleon was left untouched

Main intention: to restore the monarchies overthrown by Napoleon and create a new conservative order in Europe.

  1. How the conservative regimes ruled

  • The regimes of 1815 were autocratic: they did not tolerate criticism and dissent and tried to curb activities that questioned their legitimacy.
  • Most imposed censorship laws to control what was said in newspapers, books, plays and songs that reflected the ideas of liberty and freedom associated with the French Revolution.
  • The memory of the French Revolution still inspired liberals. A major issue for the liberal-nationalists was freedom of the press.

Reading NCERT Fig. 6 — The Club of Thinkers (an anonymous caricature of about 1820). A plaque asks: "How long will thinking be allowed to us?" The club's rules say silence is its first commandment, and muzzles will be given to members as they enter so that no one is tempted to speak. What it shows: the artist mocks the censorship of the conservative regimes — thinkers are gagged and can meet only to stay silent.

  1. The revolutionaries and the secret societies

After 1815, liberal-nationalists feared repression, so many of them went underground. They formed secret societies in many European states, where they trained revolutionaries and spread their ideas.

To be a revolutionary then meant:

  1. a commitment to oppose the monarchical forms established after the Vienna Congress;
  2. a fight for liberty and freedom;
  3. for most of them, seeing the creation of nation-states as a necessary part of the struggle for freedom.

  1. Giuseppe Mazzini

Fact Detail
Birth Genoa, 1805
First society A member of the secret society of the Carbonari
Exile At 26, sent into exile in 1831 for attempting a revolution in Liguria
Societies he founded Young Italy in Marseilles, then Young Europe in Berne — members were like-minded young men from Poland, France, Italy and the German states
His belief God intended nations to be the natural units of mankind; Italy could not remain a patchwork of small states — it had to become a single unified republic within a wider alliance of nations; unification alone could be the basis of Italian liberty
His influence Following his model, secret societies were set up in Germany, France, Switzerland and Poland
How conservatives saw him His opposition to monarchy and his vision of democratic republics frightened them; Metternich called him "the most dangerous enemy of our social order"

Worked example 1

Reading the Club of Thinkers

Question. Look at NCERT Fig. 6, The Club of Thinkers. What does the muzzle rule tell us about the years after 1815? (2 marks)

Asked: what the caricature says about the period.

Concept used: How the conservative regimes ruled (this topic, section 3).

  1. 1

    What it shows: members of a "thinkers' club" are given muzzles so they cannot speak; the club asks how long thinking will be allowed.

  2. 2

    What it tells us: the conservative regimes of 1815 did not tolerate criticism and dissent and used censorship to silence the ideas of liberty and freedom.

  3. Check: the answer links the picture (muzzles) to a fact of the period (censorship). "The club wanted silence for study" reads the joke literally and misses the point.

  4. Answer It mocks censorship: after 1815, autocratic governments silenced people who spoke for liberty and freedom.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The caricature shows thinkers given muzzles and asking how long thinking will be allowed — free speech is gagged. (1)
    2. It mocks the autocratic regimes after 1815, which tolerated no dissent and censored ideas of liberty and freedom. (1)
    Common trap: treating a caricature as a real event. A caricature exaggerates to make a point — here, about censorship.

Worked example 2

Who got what at Vienna

Question. Match each power to what it received under the Treaty of Vienna: (i) Prussia; (ii) Austria; (iii) Russia. Then name the arrangement that was left untouched.

Asked: three gains and one unchanged arrangement.

Concept used: The Treaty of Vienna (this topic, section 2).

  1. 1

    (i) Prussia → new territories on its western frontiers and a portion of Saxony.

  2. 2

    (ii) Austria → control of northern Italy.

  3. 3

    (iii) Russia → part of Poland.

  4. 4

    Left untouched → the German confederation of 39 states.

  5. Check: each power appears once and each gain lies on its own side of Europe — Prussia in the west and centre, Austria in the south, Russia in the east; if "Russia → northern Italy" appeared, it would put Russia far from its own borders.

  6. Answer Prussia: western territories + part of Saxony; Austria: northern Italy; Russia: part of Poland; the 39-state German confederation was left untouched.

    Common trap: saying the treaty created the German confederation. Napoleon set it up; Vienna left it untouched.

Common mistakes!

  • "The Treaty of Vienna removed the Bourbons."

    It restored the Bourbon dynasty.

    Write: Bourbons restored.

  • "Vienna united the German states into one country."

    The 39-state confederation was left untouched; unification came in 1871.

    Write: confederation left untouched.

  • "Metternich was a liberal hero."

    He hosted the conservative Congress and called Mazzini "the most dangerous enemy of our social order".

    Metternich = conservative order.

  • "Mazzini founded Young Italy in Berne."

    Young Italy — Marseilles; Young Europe — Berne.

    Learn: Marseilles–Italy first, Berne–Europe second.

Key points

  • Conservatism after 1815: preserve monarchy, Church, social hierarchy, property and family — but use modernisation (army, bureaucracy, economy, end of serfdom) to make monarchies stronger.
  • Vienna, 1815 (Britain, Russia, Prussia, Austria; host Metternich): Bourbons restored; France loses annexations; Netherlands (with Belgium) and Piedmont (with Genoa) set up on France's borders; Prussia — western territories + part of Saxony; Austria — northern Italy; Russia — part of Poland; 39-state German confederation untouched. Aim: restore monarchies, a conservative order.
  • Regimes were autocratic, used censorship (Fig. 6); liberals demanded freedom of the press.
  • Secret societies trained revolutionaries. Mazzini (Genoa 1805; Carbonari; exiled 1831; Young Italy — Marseilles, Young Europe — Berne): nations are natural units; Italy must be one unified republic. Metternich: "the most dangerous enemy of our social order".
