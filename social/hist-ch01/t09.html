Topic 9 of 10

Visualising the nation

About 25 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain personification and allegory, and identify Marianne and Germania and their attributes.
  • Interpret Veit's Germania with Box 3 and read Figs 16–19.

You can paint a king — he has a face. But how do you paint a nation? A nation has millions of faces, or none at all. Nineteenth-century artists solved the problem by painting a woman who was nobody in particular, gave her a cap, a flag, a sword or a crown of leaves, and told the people: this is your country.

  1. Personification and allegory

  • It is easy to show a ruler through a portrait or a statue. To give a face to a nation, artists of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries personified it — represented a country as if it were a person.
  • The nation was shown as a female figure. She was not any real woman; she gave the abstract idea of the nation a concrete form. That is why the figure is called an allegory of the nation.

New word. Allegory — when an abstract idea (for instance greed, envy, freedom, liberty) is expressed through a person or a thing. An allegorical story has two meanings, one literal and one symbolic.

From the French Revolution: artists used the female allegory for ideas like Liberty, Justice and the Republic, each shown with objects or symbols:

  • Liberty — the red cap or the broken chain;
  • Justice — generally a blindfolded woman carrying a pair of weighing scales.

  1. Marianne and Germania

Marianne (France) Germania (Germany)
The name A popular Christian name — it stressed the idea of a people's nation The allegory of the German nation
Her attributes Drawn from Liberty and the Republic: the red cap, the tricolour, the cockade A crown of oak leaves — the German oak stands for heroism
Where she appeared Statues in public squares, to remind people of the national symbol of unity and persuade them to identify with it; on coins and stamps Paintings and banners (Figs 17–19)
In your textbook NCERT Fig. 16 — postage stamps of 1850 with Marianne representing the Republic of France NCERT Figs 17, 18, 19

  1. Reading Veit's Germania — NCERT Fig. 17 and Box 3

NCERT Fig. 17 — Germania, Philip Veit, 1848. Veit painted this Germania on a cotton banner, to hang from the ceiling of the Church of St Paul — the hall of the Frankfurt parliament (Topic 6).

Box 3 — Meanings of the symbols:

Attribute Significance
Broken chains Being freed
Breastplate with eagle Symbol of the German empire — strength
Crown of oak leaves Heroism
Sword Readiness to fight
Olive branch around the sword Willingness to make peace
Black, red and gold tricolour Flag of the liberal-nationalists in 1848, banned by the Dukes of the German states
Rays of the rising sun Beginning of a new era

Memory hook: "chains freed, eagle strong, oak brave, sword ready, olive peace, flag liberal, sun new".

The change since 1836. In an earlier allegorical painting of 1836, Veit had placed the Kaiser's crown where he now put the broken chain. What the change means: in 1848 the hope was no longer a German nation under an emperor but a nation freed from autocratic rule — a liberal, parliamentary Germany.

  1. Two more Germanias

  • NCERT Fig. 18 — The fallen Germania, Julius Hübner, 1850: Germania lies fallen on the ground. Painted two years after 1848, it can be read as a picture of the defeat of the liberal hopes of 1848 — the Frankfurt parliament had been forced to disband (Topic 6).
  • NCERT Fig. 19 — Germania guarding the Rhine, Lorenz Clasen, 1860 (a commissioned painting): Germania stands with a drawn sword; the inscription on the sword reads "The German sword protects the German Rhine." Here the nation appears as a guardian of its territory, ready to fight.

Note: the textbook's Activity under Fig. 18 says "Describe what you see in Fig. 17" but asks about Hübner — the painting meant is Fig. 18.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Decoding Clasen's Germania

Question. NCERT Fig. 19 shows Germania holding a sword inscribed "The German sword protects the German Rhine." Using Box 3, explain what the sword stands for and what message the painting gives. (3 marks)

Asked: the meaning of the sword; the painting's message.

Concept used: Box 3 and section 4.

  1. 1

    Who she is: Germania, the female allegory of the German nation, painted by Lorenz Clasen in 1860.

  2. 2

    The sword: in Box 3 a sword means readiness to fight.

  3. 3

    The message: with the inscription about the Rhine, the painting shows the nation as a guardian ready to defend German territory along the Rhine.

  4. Check: the meaning of the sword comes from Box 3, not from guesswork; "the sword means peace" would mix it up with the olive branch.

  5. Answer The sword means readiness to fight; the painting shows Germania as a guardian ready to defend the German Rhine.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. The figure is Germania, the female allegory of the German nation, painted by Lorenz Clasen in 1860. (1)
    2. In Box 3 a sword stands for readiness to fight. (1)
    3. With the inscription about the Rhine, the painting presents the nation as a guardian ready to defend German territory. (1)
    Common trap: calling her Marianne because she is a woman with a flag. Marianne's signs are the red cap, tricolour and cockade; Germania's is the crown of oak leaves.

Worked example 2

From the Kaiser's crown to broken chains

Question. In 1836 Veit painted the Kaiser's crown where, in his 1848 Germania, he placed a broken chain. What does this change show? (2 marks)

Asked: the meaning of the change.

Concept used: Section 3 and Box 3.

  1. 1

    The symbols: the Kaiser's crown stands for rule by an emperor; broken chains stand for being freed (Box 3).

  2. 2

    The change: by 1848 the German nation was imagined not under an emperor but freed from autocracy — the liberal hope of the Frankfurt parliament, for which the banner was made.

  3. Check: the answer uses Box 3's meaning ("being freed") and links it to 1848. Saying "Veit wanted a stronger emperor" reverses the change.

  4. Answer It shows a shift from loyalty to an emperor to the liberal hope of a free nation in 1848.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The Kaiser's crown stood for rule by an emperor; broken chains, in Box 3, mean being freed. (1)
    2. The change shows the 1848 hope of a German nation freed from autocracy, as the Frankfurt liberals wanted. (1)
    Common trap: describing only the objects. Say what the change means.

Common mistakes!

  • Marianne and Germania swapped.

    Marianne — France (red cap, tricolour, cockade); Germania — Germany (crown of oak leaves).

    Marianne — France; Germania — Germany.

  • "The olive branch means readiness to fight."

    The sword means readiness to fight; the olive branch means willingness to make peace.

    Learn Box 3 with the memory hook.

  • "Germania was a real German princess."

    The allegory did not stand for any particular woman.

    Write: an abstract idea given a concrete form.

  • "Britannia is the allegory of France."

    Britannia is the symbol of the British nation (Fig. 20, Topic 10).

    Three allegories: Marianne, Germania, Britannia.

Key points

  • Artists personified nations as female figures — allegories that gave the abstract idea of the nation a concrete form; no real woman was meant.
  • Revolutionary allegories: Liberty (red cap, broken chain), Justice (blindfold, scales).
  • Marianne (France): a popular Christian name → a people's nation; red cap, tricolour, cockade; statues, coins and stamps (Fig. 16, 1850).
  • Germania (Germany): crown of oak leaves = heroism. Veit, 1848 (Fig. 17), banner for the Frankfurt parliament; Box 3: broken chains — freed; eagle — strength; oak — heroism; sword — ready to fight; olive branch — peace; black, red, gold — liberal-nationalists of 1848; rising sun — new era.
  • Hübner, 1850 — The fallen Germania (Fig. 18); Clasen, 1860 — Germania guarding the Rhine (Fig. 19).
