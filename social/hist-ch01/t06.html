Topic 6 of 10

Hunger, hardship and 1848

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain the economic hardship of the 1830s and the 1848 revolt in Paris; read the Silesian weavers' account.
  • Describe the 1848 revolution of the liberals and the Frankfurt parliament, and why it failed.
  • Explain the debate over women's rights (Source C) and the concessions after 1848.

Two very different crowds filled the streets of Europe in 1848. One was hungry — weavers, workers and peasants with no bread and no jobs. The other was well dressed — lawyers, professors and businessmen marching in a festive procession to a church where they would write a constitution for a nation that did not yet exist. Both crowds wanted change. Neither got all it wanted.

  1. The hungry 1830s

The 1830s were years of great economic hardship in Europe:

Cause Effect
An enormous increase in population all over Europe in the first half of the nineteenth century More job-seekers than jobs
People from rural areas migrated to the cities They lived in overcrowded slums
Small producers in towns faced stiff competition from cheap machine-made goods from England, especially in textiles (made mostly in homes or small workshops, only partly mechanised) Small producers struggled to compete
Where the aristocracy still had power, peasants struggled under feudal dues and obligations Peasants struggled under the burden
A rise in food prices or a year of bad harvest Widespread pauperism (extreme poverty) in town and country

  1. 1848 in Paris: the revolt of the hungry

The year 1848 was one such year. Food shortages and widespread unemployment brought the people of Paris out on the roads:

  • Barricades were put up and Louis Philippe was forced to flee.
  • A National Assembly proclaimed a Republic, granted suffrage to all adult males above 21, and guaranteed the right to work.
  • National workshops were set up to provide employment.

In France the two revolutions met: the same February days that drove out Louis Philippe also brought the republic that liberals wanted (section 4).

  1. The Silesian weavers, 1845

Three years before, in 1845, the weavers of Silesia had risen against the contractors. The contractors gave the weavers raw material and orders for cloth, but kept cutting the payments sharply. The journalist Wilhelm Wolff described what happened in one Silesian village:

  • In these villages (18,000 inhabitants) cotton weaving was the main occupation and the misery of the workers was extreme; contractors used the desperate need for jobs to cut the prices of the goods they ordered.
  • On 4 June at 2 p.m. a large crowd of weavers marched in pairs to their contractor's mansion demanding higher wages; they were met with scorn and threats.
  • A group then forced its way into the house and smashed windowpanes, furniture and porcelain; another group plundered the storehouse of cloth.
  • The contractor fled to a neighbouring village, which refused to shelter him; he returned 24 hours later with the army. In the exchange that followed, eleven weavers were shot.

The journalist's viewpoint: Wolff writes about the extreme misery of the workers and how contractors took advantage of their need; his account is sympathetic to the weavers, while still reporting their violence.

  1. 1848: the revolution of the liberals

Parallel to the revolts of the poor, a revolution led by the educated middle classes was under way:

  • February 1848, France: the monarch abdicated and a republic based on universal male suffrage was proclaimed.
  • Where independent nation-states did not yet exist — Germany, Italy, Poland, the Austro-Hungarian Empire — men and women of the liberal middle classes combined demands for constitutionalism with national unification.
  • They used the growing popular unrest to push for a nation-state on parliamentary principles: a constitution, freedom of the press and freedom of association.

  1. The Frankfurt parliament

Step What happened
1 Political associations of middle-class professionals, businessmen and prosperous artisans met in Frankfurt and decided to vote for an all-German National Assembly
2 On 18 May 1848, 831 elected representatives marched in a festive procession to take their places in the Frankfurt parliament in the Church of St Paul
3 They drafted a constitution for a German nation headed by a monarchy subject to a parliament
4 The crown, on these terms, was offered to Friedrich Wilhelm IV, King of Prussia. He turned it down and joined the other monarchs against the elected assembly
5 The opposition of the aristocracy and military grew stronger; the parliament's social basis eroded — it was dominated by the middle classes, who resisted the demands of workers and artisans and so lost their support
6 In the end troops were called in and the assembly was forced to disband

Women and the vote. Women had formed their own political associations, founded newspapers and taken part in meetings and demonstrations — yet they were denied suffrage in the election of the Assembly. In the Church of St Paul they were admitted only as observers in the visitors' gallery. NCERT Fig. 10 (a contemporary print of the Frankfurt parliament) shows women in the upper left gallery.

A note on dates: the caption of NCERT Fig. 17 (Topic 9) says the parliament "was convened in March 1848"; the textbook's account gives 18 May 1848 for the day the elected representatives took their seats. Learn 18 May 1848.

  1. Reading Source C — three views on women's rights

Writer View
Carl Welcker, an elected member of the Frankfurt Parliament Nature made men and women for different functions: man, "the stronger", is protector and provider for public tasks; woman, "the weaker", belongs to the home; equality between the sexes would endanger harmony and the dignity of the family
Louise Otto-Peters (1819–95), founder of a women's journal and later a feminist political association — editorial of 21 April 1849 Men who would die for Liberty fight for only one half of humanity; "Liberty is indivisible!" — free men must not tolerate being surrounded by the unfree
An anonymous reader of her newspaper — letter of 25 June 1850 It is unreasonable to deny women political rights when they enjoy the right to property and carry responsibilities; even "the stupidest cattle-herder" can vote simply because he is a man

What it reveals about liberal ideology: liberals spoke of liberty and equality, but many — like Welcker — did not extend political rights to women; liberty was being defined for men of property, which is why women and their supporters demanded that liberty be indivisible.

New word. Feminist — awareness of women's rights and interests based on the belief in the social, economic and political equality of the genders.

  1. After 1848: concessions from above

Conservative forces suppressed the liberal movements of 1848 but could not restore the old order. Monarchs began to realise that the cycles of revolution and repression could be ended only by granting concessions. So in the years after 1848 the autocratic monarchies of Central and Eastern Europe introduced changes Western Europe had made before 1815:

  • serfdom and bonded labour were abolished in the Habsburg dominions and in Russia;
  • the Habsburg rulers granted more autonomy to the Hungarians in 1867.

New word. Ideology — a system of ideas reflecting a particular social and political vision.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Two crowds of 1848

Question. Distinguish between the revolt of the poor in Paris and the revolution of the liberals in 1848: who took part and what each wanted. (3 marks)

Asked: the people and the aims of each, and the difference.

Concept used: Sections 2 and 4 of this topic. Why it applies: the textbook calls the liberal revolution "parallel" to the revolts of the poor.

  1. 1

    Paris: driven by food shortages and unemployment, the people put up barricades; they won a republic with votes for all adult males over 21, a right to work and national workshops.

  2. 2

    Liberals: the educated middle classes of Germany, Italy, Poland and Austria-Hungary combined constitutionalism with national unification.

  3. 3

    Their aims: a nation-state on parliamentary principles — a constitution, freedom of the press and freedom of association.

  4. Check: the first point is about bread and jobs, the second and third about constitutions and nations — two different aims. If both points said "they wanted bread", the difference would vanish.

  5. Answer The Paris poor rose for food and work; the liberal middle classes demanded a constitution and a united nation-state.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. In Paris, food shortages and unemployment drove people to the barricades; they won votes for adult men over 21 and the right to work. (1)
    2. Elsewhere the educated middle classes combined demands for constitutionalism with national unification. (1)
    3. They wanted a nation-state on parliamentary principles: a constitution, freedom of the press and of association. (1)
    Common trap: saying the Frankfurt parliament was set up by hungry workers. It was formed by middle-class professionals, businessmen and prosperous artisans.

Worked example 2

Why Frankfurt failed (an answer plan)

Question. Plan a 5-mark answer: "Why did the Frankfurt parliament of 1848 fail to unite Germany?"

Asked: five reasons for failure.

Concept used: Section 5 of this topic.

  1. 1

    The king said no: Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia rejected the crown offered on the parliament's terms.

  2. 2

    Monarchs united against it: he joined other monarchs to oppose the elected assembly.

  3. 3

    Aristocracy and military: their opposition grew stronger.

  4. 4

    Lost the people: the parliament was dominated by the middle classes, who resisted the demands of workers and artisans and lost their support.

  5. 5

    Force: troops were called in and the assembly was forced to disband.

  6. Check: each point is a reason that leads to the end, and they run in order (rejection → opposition → lost support → troops). A point such as "it met in the Church of St Paul" is true but explains nothing.

  7. Answer Rejection by the King of Prussia, opposition of the monarchs, aristocracy and army, loss of workers' and artisans' support, and finally troops disbanded it.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    1. The parliament offered the crown of a German nation, under a parliament, to Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia; he rejected it. (1)
    2. He joined other monarchs to oppose the elected assembly. (1)
    3. The opposition of the aristocracy and the military grew stronger. (1)
    4. The middle-class members resisted the demands of workers and artisans, so the parliament's social basis eroded and it lost their support. (1)
    5. Finally troops were called in and the assembly was forced to disband. (1)
    Common trap: writing that women's demands broke up the parliament. Women were kept out, but the textbook gives the reasons above for its fall.

Common mistakes!

  • "Friedrich Wilhelm IV accepted the crown and became German emperor."

    He rejected it; the German Empire came in 1871 under William I.

    Write: crown rejected, 1848.

  • "Women voted for the Frankfurt Assembly."

    Women were denied suffrage and sat only in the visitors' gallery.

    Write: observers only.

  • "In 1848 Paris rose only because of hunger" or "only for a constitution."

    Your textbook gives both: food shortages and widespread unemployment brought the people of Paris out on the roads, and the February 1848 events in France (the king abdicated; a republic with universal male suffrage) are also part of the revolution of the liberals.

    Write both causes for France; for Germany, Italy and Poland, write the liberal demands (constitution + nation).

  • "The parliament failed because it was too radical for workers."

    It resisted workers' and artisans' demands and so lost their support.

    Write: middle-class parliament lost workers' support.

Key points

  • 1830s hardship: population rise, more job-seekers than jobs, city slums, cheap English machine-made textiles, feudal dues, food prices → pauperism.
  • 1848, Paris: food shortages and unemployment → barricades → Louis Philippe flees → Republic: votes for all men over 21, right to work, national workshops. 1845: Silesian weavers — eleven shot (Wolff's account).
  • Revolution of the liberals: constitution + national unification; free press, freedom of association.
  • Frankfurt parliament: 18 May 1848, 831 representatives, Church of St Paul; crown rejected by Friedrich Wilhelm IV; opposed by monarchs, aristocracy, military; lost workers' and artisans' support; disbanded by troops. Women only observers (Fig. 10).
  • Source C: Welcker (different functions) vs Otto-Peters ("Liberty is indivisible!") vs a reader (property but no vote).
  • After 1848: serfdom and bonded labour abolished in Habsburg lands and Russia; Hungarians get more autonomy, 1867.
