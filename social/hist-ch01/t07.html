Topic 7 of 10

The making of Germany and Italy

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe German unification under Prussia and Bismarck (1848 → 1871) and read Figs 11–13.
  • Describe Italian unification — Mazzini, Cavour, Garibaldi, Victor Emmanuel II (1859–1870) — and read Figs 14–15.
  • Explain why the unifications were led from above.

In 1848 the dream of a united Germany belonged to professors and lawyers making speeches in a church. Twenty-three years later Germany was united — not by speeches but by soldiers, in a palace hall in France, on a freezing January morning. In Italy, a peasant who had supported Garibaldi believed that "La Talia" was the king's wife. The nations of the dreamers were finally built — by kings, ministers and armies.

  1. After 1848: nationalism changes sides

After 1848, nationalism in Europe moved away from its association with democracy and revolution. Nationalist feelings were often mobilised by conservatives for promoting state power and achieving political domination over Europe. The unification of Germany and Italy shows this.

  1. Germany — can the army be the architect of a nation?

Step What happened
1. The liberal attempt In 1848 middle-class Germans tried to unite the regions of the German confederation into a nation-state governed by an elected parliament (Topic 6)
2. It is crushed The monarchy and the military joined forces to repress this liberal attempt, backed by Prussia's large landowners (Junkers)
3. Prussia takes the lead From then on Prussia led the movement for national unification
4. The architect Prussia's chief minister, Otto von Bismarck, was the architect of the process, carried out with the help of the Prussian army and bureaucracy
5. Three wars Three wars over seven years — with Austria, Denmark and France — ended in Prussian victory and completed unification
6. The proclamation 18 January 1871, a bitterly cold morning: in the unheated Hall of Mirrors of the Palace of Versailles, German princes, army representatives and leading Prussian ministers, Bismarck among them, watched the Prussian king William I being proclaimed German Emperor (Kaiser)
7. The new state It showed the dominance of Prussian state power; the new state stressed modernising the currency, banking, legal and judicial systems; Prussian measures and practices often became a model for the rest of Germany

Reading the figures.

  • NCERT Fig. 11 — The proclamation of the German empire in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, by Anton von Werner: the Kaiser and the chief commander of the Prussian army, General von Roon, at the centre, with Bismarck near them. The huge work (2.7 m × 2.7 m) was presented to Bismarck on his 70th birthday in 1885. What it tells us: the new empire was born among princes, generals and ministers, not elected deputies.
  • NCERT Fig. 12 — Unification of Germany (1866–71), a map whose key shows: Prussia before 1866; land conquered in the Austro-Prussian War, 1866; Austrian territories excluded from the German Confederation, 1867; states that joined Prussia to form the German Confederation, 1867; south German states joining to form the German Empire, 1871; land won in the Franco-Prussian War, 1871.
  • NCERT Fig. 13 — a caricature of Bismarck in the German Reichstag (parliament), from Figaro, Vienna, 5 March 1870. Bismarck stands cracking a whip, while the deputies duck low behind their benches. What it tells us: the artist shows Bismarck dominating the elected deputies — parliament is cowed, not in control.

  1. Italy unified

The starting point. Italy, like Germany, had long been split up — political fragmentation. By the mid-nineteenth century it was seven states. Only one, Sardinia-Piedmont, had an Italian princely house as its ruler. The Austrian Habsburgs held the north, the Pope ruled the centre, and the Bourbon kings of Spain ruled the south. There was not even one common form of the Italian language.

Step Who and what
1. The dreamer In the 1830s Giuseppe Mazzini worked out a programme for a unitary Italian Republic and formed Young Italy to spread it
2. Failure Revolutionary uprisings in 1831 and 1848 failed; the task fell to Sardinia-Piedmont under King Victor Emmanuel II, to unify the states through war. For the ruling elites there, a unified Italy promised economic development and political dominance
3. The diplomat Chief Minister Cavour led the movement. He was neither a revolutionary nor a democrat; like many of the Italian elite he spoke French better than Italian. Through a tactful diplomatic alliance with France, Sardinia-Piedmont defeated the Austrian forces in 1859
4. The soldier Besides regular troops, many armed volunteers under Giuseppe Garibaldi joined. In 1860 they marched into South Italy and the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies and won the support of the local peasants to drive out the Spanish rulers
5. The king In 1861 Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed king of united Italy
6. The limit Illiteracy was very high; much of the population was unaware of liberal-nationalist ideology. Peasants in the south who had supported Garibaldi had never heard of Italia and believed "La Talia" was Victor Emmanuel's wife

Box 2 — Giuseppe Garibaldi (1807–82).

  • From a family in coastal trade; a sailor in the merchant navy.
  • 1833: met Mazzini and joined Young Italy; 1834: took part in a republican uprising in Piedmont, which was suppressed; fled to South America, in exile till 1848.
  • 1854: supported Victor Emmanuel II in his efforts to unify the Italian states.
  • 1860: led the famous Expedition of the Thousand to South Italy; volunteers kept joining till they numbered about 30,000 — the Red Shirts.
  • 1867: led volunteers to Rome against the Papal States, where a French garrison was stationed; the Red Shirts were no match for the combined French and Papal troops.
  • 1870: during its war with Prussia, France withdrew its troops from Rome, and the Papal States were finally joined to Italy.

Memory hook — M-C-G: Mazzini dreamed a republic · Cavour used diplomacy and an alliance with France · Garibaldi led the volunteers in the south. The king, Victor Emmanuel II, wore the crown in 1861.

Reading the figures.

  • NCERT Fig. 14(a) — Italian states before unification, 1858; Fig. 14(b) — Italy after unification: the map marks 1858 for Sardinia-Piedmont, 1858–60 for the states of the north and centre, 1860 for the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, Venetia (1866) and Rome and the Papal State (1870). So the first region was Sardinia-Piedmont (1858); the last was the Papal State (Rome, 1870); and the largest number of states joined in 1858–60.

Note: the map marks 1858 and 1858–60, but the textbook's text says Sardinia-Piedmont defeated Austria in 1859, and its dates box gives 1859–1870 for the unification of Italy. Read the map as printed when a question asks about the map (as the NCERT Activity on p. 21 does); for dates, learn the text: 1859, 1860, 1861, 1866, 1870.

  • NCERT Fig. 15 — an English caricature of 1859: Garibaldi, kneeling, holds the base of a boot named "Italy" so that King Victor Emmanuel II of Sardinia-Piedmont can step into it from the top. What it tells us: Garibaldi does the work; the king gets to "wear" Italy — the map of Italy is shaped like a boot.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Reading Bismarck's whip

Question. Look at NCERT Fig. 13, the caricature of Bismarck in the Reichstag (1870). What does it suggest about how Bismarck dealt with the elected deputies? (2 marks)

Asked: what the picture shows and what it means.

Concept used: Reading the figures (section 2).

  1. 1

    What it shows: Bismarck stands cracking a whip while the deputies duck low behind their benches.

  2. 2

    What it means: the artist suggests the elected parliament was cowed and controlled by Bismarck — power lay with the Prussian chief minister, not with the people's representatives.

  3. Check: the answer reads both the whip and the ducking deputies; an answer that says "Bismarck respected parliament" contradicts the picture.

  4. Answer Bismarck dominated the deputies; the elected parliament had little real power.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The caricature shows Bismarck cracking a whip while the deputies of the Reichstag duck behind their benches. (1)
    2. It suggests he dominated the elected deputies, so real power lay with him, not with parliament. (1)
    Common trap: describing the picture without interpreting it. A 2-mark picture question needs what it shows + what it means.

Worked example 2

Who did what in Italy?

Question. Match each person with the role your textbook gives him: (i) Cavour; (ii) Garibaldi; (iii) Victor Emmanuel II; (iv) Mazzini — roles: (P) proclaimed king of united Italy in 1861; (Q) led armed volunteers into South Italy in 1860; (R) chief minister who made a diplomatic alliance with France; (S) put together a programme for a unitary Italian Republic in the 1830s.

Asked: the correct pairs.

Concept used: Italy unified (section 3) and the M-C-G hook.

  1. 1

    (i) Cavour → (R) — chief minister, alliance with France, Austria defeated 1859.

  2. 2

    (ii) Garibaldi → (Q) — volunteers (Red Shirts) into South Italy and the Two Sicilies, 1860.

  3. 3

    (iii) Victor Emmanuel II → (P) — king of united Italy, 1861.

  4. 4

    (iv) Mazzini → (S) — programme for a unitary republic; Young Italy.

  5. Check: only one person was a king (P), only one a chief minister (R); Mazzini wanted a republic, so he cannot be the one proclaimed king.

  6. Answer (i)–R, (ii)–Q, (iii)–P, (iv)–S.

    Common trap: giving Mazzini the credit for the kingdom of 1861. His dream was a republic; the kingdom was made by Cavour, Garibaldi and Victor Emmanuel II.

Worked example 3

"La Talia"

Question. What does the story of the southern peasants who thought "La Talia" was Victor Emmanuel's wife tell us about Italian unification? (2 marks)

Asked: the meaning of the story.

Concept used: The limit of Italian unification (section 3, step 6).

  1. 1

    The fact: illiteracy was very high and much of the population was unaware of liberal-nationalist ideology.

  2. 2

    The meaning: unification was achieved from above — by the king, his chief minister and armies — before ordinary people saw themselves as Italians.

  3. Check: the answer moves from the story to the conclusion. "Peasants did not support Garibaldi" is wrong — they did support him, without knowing the idea of Italy.

  4. Answer Many ordinary Italians did not yet feel Italian; the nation was made by elites before it was felt by the people.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. Illiteracy was high, and southern peasants who backed Garibaldi had never heard of Italia, thinking "La Talia" was the king's wife. (1)
    2. So unification came from above, before most ordinary people shared a sense of being Italian. (1)
    Common trap: treating the story as a joke only. It is evidence that national feeling had not reached the masses.

Common mistakes!

  • "Germany was unified by the Frankfurt parliament."

    It failed in 1848; Prussia, Bismarck, the army and bureaucracy unified Germany.

    Write: from above, by Prussia.

  • "William I was proclaimed emperor in Berlin."

    It was in the Hall of Mirrors, Versailles, 18 January 1871.

    Learn Versailles, January 1871.

  • "Cavour founded Young Italy" / "Mazzini led the Red Shirts".

    Mazzini — Young Italy; Garibaldi — Red Shirts; Cavour — chief minister and diplomat.

    Use M-C-G.

  • "Italy was fully united in 1861."

    1861 = king proclaimed; Venetia 1866; Rome 1870.

    Learn 1861 → 1866 → 1870.

  • A 5-mark "process of unification" answer that only lists names: "Mazzini, Cavour, Garibaldi, Victor Emmanuel II."

    Names alone show no steps; the examiner gives one mark per explained step.

    Write five numbered steps, each with who, what and when — like the step-by-step chain for Germany in this topic.

Key points

  • After 1848, nationalism was used by conservatives for state power.
  • Germany: 1848 liberal attempt crushed (monarchy, military, Junkers) → Prussia leads → Bismarck, the architect, with army and bureaucracy → three wars in seven years (Austria, Denmark, France) → 18 January 1871, Hall of Mirrors, Versailles: William I German Emperor → Prussian power dominates; currency, banking, legal and judicial systems modernised (Figs 11–13).
  • Italy: seven states, only Sardinia-Piedmont Italian-ruled → Mazzini (republic, Young Italy) → revolutionary uprisings of 1831 and 1848 fail → Victor Emmanuel II and Cavour (alliance with France; Austria defeated 1859) → Garibaldi (Red Shirts, South Italy 1860) → king of united Italy 1861 → Venetia 1866 → Rome 1870 (Box 2; Figs 14–15). "La Talia": the people lagged behind.
