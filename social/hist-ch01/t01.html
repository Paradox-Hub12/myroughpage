Sorrieu's dream and the idea of a nation

About 30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Read Sorrieu's print (Fig. 1) — the peoples, the statue of Liberty, the shattered symbols, the order of the nations — and explain why it is utopian.
  • Explain what a nation-state is and how it differs from a multi-national dynastic empire.
  • Summarise Renan's idea of a nation (Source A).

Imagine you could draw the future. In 1848 a French artist did exactly that. He drew men and women of every age and class walking in one long line, past a tall female statue holding a torch. On the ground lay broken crowns and sceptres. Each group in the line carried its own flag. The artist was dreaming of a world of free nations — at a time when most of Europe was still ruled by kings and emperors.

  1. Sorrieu's print — reading NCERT Fig. 1

In 1848, Frédéric Sorrieu, a French artist, prepared a series of four prints showing his dream of a world made up of "democratic and social Republics". The first print is NCERT Fig. 1, titled The Dream of Worldwide Democratic and Social Republics – The Pact Between Nations.

Read it in four parts:

Part of the print What you see What it means
The marchers The peoples of Europe and America — men and women of all ages and social classes — in a long train, offering homage to the statue as they pass Ordinary people, not kings, are the heroes
The statue of Liberty A female figure holding the torch of Enlightenment in one hand and the Charter of the Rights of Man in the other Liberty, as artists of the French Revolution showed it
The foreground The shattered remains of the symbols of absolutist institutions lying on the earth The old order of absolute kings is broken
The sky Christ, saints and angels gazing down Fraternity (brotherhood) among the nations

The order of the nations. The peoples are grouped as distinct nations, each known by its flag and national costume:

  • Leading the procession, already past the statue: the United States and Switzerland — by this time already nation-states.
  • France, with the revolutionary tricolour, has just reached the statue.
  • Then the peoples of Germany, with the black, red and gold flag. In 1848 the Germans were not yet a united nation: their flag was an expression of liberal hopes in 1848 to unify the many German-speaking principalities into a nation-state under a democratic constitution.
  • Then Austria, the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, Lombardy, Poland, England, Ireland, Hungary and Russia.

Why it is "utopian". A utopian vision is a picture of a society so ideal that it is unlikely to actually exist. In 1848 most of these peoples were not free nations; the print shows a world that did not yet exist — all peoples free, equal and at peace with one another.

New words.

  • Absolutist — a government with no restraints on its power; in history, a form of monarchy that was centralised, militarised and repressive.
  • Utopian — a vision of a society so ideal that it is unlikely to actually exist.

  1. What is a nation-state?

During the nineteenth century nationalism brought sweeping changes in the political and mental world of Europe. The end result: the nation-state replaced the multi-national dynastic empires of Europe.

A modern state A nation-state
A centralised power exercises sovereign control over a clearly defined territory — this had been developing in Europe for a long time The majority of its citizens, and not only its rulers, develop a sense of common identity and shared history or descent

People did not always have this common identity — not from time immemorial. It was made through struggles, by leaders and common people acting together. How it was made is the story of this chapter.

  1. Reading Source A — Ernst Renan, "What is a Nation?"

In a lecture at the University of Sorbonne in 1882, the French philosopher Ernst Renan (1823–92) explained what makes a nation.

What he rejected: the idea that a nation is formed by a common language, race, religion or territory.

What he said a nation is:

  1. The culmination of a long past of endeavours, sacrifice and devotion — a heroic past, great men and glory are the "social capital" of a national idea.
  2. Common glories in the past and a common will in the present; having done great deeds together and wishing to do more.
  3. A large-scale solidarity whose existence is "a daily plebiscite" — a nation exists because its people keep agreeing to live together.
  4. A nation never has a real interest in annexing or holding on to a country against its will — "a province is its inhabitants".

Why nations matter to Renan: they are "a good thing, a necessity even", because they protect liberty. In a world with only one law and only one master, liberty would be lost.

New word. Plebiscite — a direct vote by which all the people of a region are asked to accept or reject a proposal.

Worked example 1

The order of the marchers

Question. In NCERT Fig. 1, why does Sorrieu place the United States and Switzerland well ahead of the other peoples, and why is the German flag in the procession surprising? (2 marks)

Asked: the reason for the leading position; the surprise about Germany.

Concept used: The order of the nations (this topic, section 1). Why it applies: the position of each people in the line tells us how far it had come towards being a nation-state.

  1. 1

    Leaders: the USA and Switzerland had already become nation-states, so they have already passed the statue of Liberty.

  2. 2

    Germany: in 1848 the Germans were not yet a united nation; their black, red and gold flag only expressed the liberal hope of uniting the German-speaking principalities.

  3. Check: the answer must explain the position (how far along) and the flag (a hope, not a fact). Saying "Germany was already united" contradicts the date of the print — Germany was unified only in 1871 (Topic 7).

  4. Answer The USA and Switzerland lead because they were already nation-states; the German flag is surprising because Germany was not yet united — it showed only a hope.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    1. The USA and Switzerland lead the procession because they were already nation-states by 1848. (1)
    2. Germans carry a black, red and gold flag though they were not yet united; it expressed liberal hopes of a nation-state. (1)
    Common trap: writing that France leads the procession. France has just reached the statue; the USA and Switzerland are already past it.

Worked example 2

Renan against "one language, one religion"

Question. Many people in the nineteenth century believed that people who speak one language or follow one religion automatically form a nation. How would Ernst Renan have answered them? (3 marks)

Asked: Renan's reply, in three points.

Concept used: Source A (this topic, section 3). Why it applies: Renan's essay begins by rejecting exactly this idea.

  1. 1

    He disagreed: a nation is not formed by a common language, race, religion or territory.

  2. 2

    His alternative: a nation is a long past of sacrifice and devotion, shared glories in the past and a common will in the present.

  3. 3

    His test: a nation is a large-scale solidarity, a "daily plebiscite" — it lasts only as long as its people choose to live together.

  4. Check: each point is something Renan says; none adds a modern example. If a point said "Renan wanted one language for all Europe", it would contradict the first line of the source.

  5. Answer Renan would say that language or religion does not make a nation; shared memories, a common will and the daily consent of the people do.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    1. Renan rejected the idea that a common language, race, religion or territory forms a nation. (1)
    2. For him a nation grows from a long past of sacrifice and devotion, with shared glories and a common will in the present. (1)
    3. It is a large-scale solidarity, a "daily plebiscite", lasting only while its people choose to stay together. (1)
    Common trap: copying the whole quotation. Pick out the ideas — rejection, shared past and will, daily consent.

Common mistakes!

  • "A nation-state is any country ruled by a king."

    A nation-state needs the majority of citizens, not only rulers, to share an identity.

    Use the key phrase: majority of citizens, not only rulers.

  • Calling Sorrieu's print a picture of Europe in 1848 as it really was.

    It is utopian — a dream of free, democratic nations that did not yet exist.

    Write: a utopian vision of democratic and social republics.

  • "In Fig. 1 France leads the march."

    The USA and Switzerland lead; France has just reached the statue.

    Learn the order: USA, Switzerland → France → Germany → the rest.

  • "Renan said a common language makes a nation."

    He rejected language, race, religion and territory as the basis.

    Write: shared past + common will + daily consent.

Key points

  • In 1848 the French artist Frédéric Sorrieu drew a utopian dream of "democratic and social Republics" (NCERT Fig. 1): peoples of Europe and America march past the statue of Liberty (torch of Enlightenment, Charter of the Rights of Man); shattered symbols of absolutist institutions lie in front; Christ, saints and angels show fraternity.
  • The USA and Switzerland lead (already nation-states); France (tricolour) has just reached the statue; Germany (black, red, gold) was not yet united.
  • A nation-state is one in which the majority of citizens, not only rulers, share a common identity and history; this was forged through struggles.
  • Renan (1882): a nation is not language, race, religion or territory but a shared past, a common will and a "daily plebiscite"; nations guarantee liberty.
