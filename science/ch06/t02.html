Topic 2 of 5

Reflex action and the reflex arc

About 55 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Distinguish voluntary, involuntary and reflex actions, with examples of each.
  • Describe the reflex arc, explain why reflex arcs are formed in the spinal cord, and state the role of the brain in a reflex action.
  • Draw and label a reflex arc.

Think of the hot-pan moment as a relay race. Runner 1 grabs the news "HOT!" at your fingertip and sprints to the spinal cord. There, without waiting for anyone's permission, the baton is handed straight to runner 2, who races back to your arm muscle: "Pull!" Your hand is off the pan before your brain has even started to say "that hurts". Who decided — and why wasn't it the brain?

  1. What we mean by "reflex"

We say "I pulled my hand back reflexly" or "I jumped out of the bus's way reflexly". The common idea is that we acted without thinking about it, and without feeling in control. A reflex action is a sudden action in response to something in the surroundings, made without thinking.

  1. Why thinking would be too slow

Suppose you had to think before moving your hand: feel the heat, understand the danger, decide, then send orders to the muscles. Thinking happens in the brain, in the front of the skull. It needs messages to travel all the way up to the brain, a complicated web of neurons to work out the answer, and messages to travel all the way back. By then your skin could be burnt.

  1. The shortcut: the reflex arc

The body solves this by linking the nerves that detect heat directly to the nerves that work the muscles, by a far simpler route. This connection — from the input (detecting) to the output (action) — is called a reflex arc.

Where is the connection made? The best place is where the incoming and outgoing nerves first meet. Nerves from every part of the body come together as a bundle in the spinal cord before they reach the brain, so reflex arcs are formed in the spinal cord itself. The incoming information also continues to the brain, so a moment later the brain knows what happened — that is when you feel the pain.

  1. The parts of a reflex arc, in order

Use the labels of the reflex-arc diagram (NCERT Fig. 6.2):

  1. Receptors — heat/pain receptors in the skin detect the stimulus.
  2. Sensory neuron — carries the impulse from the receptor to the spinal cord.
  3. Relay neuron — inside the spinal cord (CNS); passes the message from the sensory neuron to the motor neuron (and the message also goes on to the brain).
  4. Motor neuron — carries the impulse from the spinal cord to the muscle.
  5. Effector — the muscle in the arm contracts and pulls the hand away.

The pathway card to memorise:

Stimulus → Receptor → Sensory neuron → Relay neuron (spinal cord) → Motor neuron → Effector (muscle or gland) → Response

  1. Why reflex arcs exist at all

Reflex arcs evolved because thinking in the brain is too slow for such moments. Many animals have little or no complex web of neurons for thinking at all, so for them reflex arcs do the job without true thought. Even in animals with large brains, reflex arcs remain more efficient for quick responses.

  1. Three kinds of action

The responses of the nervous system are of three kinds:

Point Reflex action Voluntary action Involuntary action
Thinking needed? no — sudden, automatic yes — we decide what to do no — we cannot easily control it even if we try
Controlled by a reflex arc in the spinal cord (brain informed afterwards) the brain (fore-brain, Topic 3) the mid-brain and hind-brain; many by the medulla
Examples pulling the hand back from a flame; lifting the foot off a pin writing, talking, moving a chair, clapping heartbeat, breathing, salivation, vomiting, blood pressure

Reflex vs involuntary. Neither needs thinking, so students mix them up. Tell them apart by what controls them. A reflex action is a sudden, quick response to a stimulus — usually protective, like pulling the hand from a flame — made through a reflex arc in the spinal cord. Involuntary actions are the body's own activities that we cannot control even if we try — heartbeat, breathing, digestion, salivation, vomiting, blood pressure — and are controlled by the mid-brain and hind-brain (blood pressure, salivation and vomiting by the medulla). Some involuntary actions, such as your mouth watering at the sight of food, are also set off by something you sense — so learn your textbook's list instead of asking "was it sudden?".

(Your textbook also calls the pupil getting smaller in bright light a reflex: light → receptors in the eye → nerve → the brain → muscles of the iris make the pupil smaller. Beyond the syllabus: for eye reflexes the connection is in the brain, not the spinal cord; for the hand and leg reflexes in this chapter, the arc is in the spinal cord.)

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Sort five actions

Question. Classify each action as reflex, voluntary or involuntary, with a reason: (a) sneezing when pepper dust enters the nose; (b) blinking at a sudden camera flash; (c) writing your name on an answer sheet; (d) your heart beating while you sleep; (e) pulling your finger back from a pin.

Given: five actions. Asked: the kind of each, with a reason.

Concept used: the three kinds of action (this topic). Why it applies: each kind differs in whether thinking is needed and in what controls it.

  1. 1

    (a) Sneezing — a sudden response to dust in the nose, without thinking → reflex.

  2. 2

    (b) Blinking at a flash — sudden, automatic, protects the eye → reflex.

  3. 3

    (c) Writing your name — you decide to do it → voluntary (brain).

  4. 4

    (d) Heartbeat — goes on all the time and cannot be controlled by thinking → involuntary (mid-brain and hind-brain).

  5. 5

    (e) Pulling back from a pin — sudden, protective, through a reflex arc in the spinal cord → reflex.

  6. Check: only (c) needed a decision; (d) is on your textbook's list of involuntary actions (mid- and hind-brain); (a), (b) and (e) are quick protective responses to a stimulus ✔.

  7. Answer (a) reflex (b) reflex (c) voluntary (d) involuntary (e) reflex

    Common trap: Calling the heartbeat a reflex because "we don't think about it". It is involuntary: continuous and controlled by the mid-brain and hind-brain, not a sudden response through a reflex arc.

Worked example 2

Why the spinal cord saves time

Question. Give two reasons why it helps that the reflex arc for pulling your foot off a hot tile is made in the spinal cord and not in the brain. Does the brain play any part?

Given: a reflex involving the leg. Asked: why the arc is in the spinal cord; the brain's part.

Concept used: the reflex arc and its position (this topic). Why it applies: the time taken depends on where the input and output nerves are joined.

  1. 1

    Shorter route → the nerves from the foot first meet the other nerves in the spinal cord, before the brain; joining there means the message does not travel the extra distance up to the brain and back.

  2. 2

    No thinking step → in the spinal cord the sensory neuron is linked to the motor neuron in a simple way; the brain's thinking needs many neurons working together, which takes longer.

  3. 3

    Result → the foot is lifted before the skin is badly burnt.

  4. 4

    The brain's part → the incoming message also travels on to the brain, so the brain is informed — you feel the pain and remember to avoid the hot tile — but it does not make the quick movement.

  5. Check: if the arc were in the brain, the message would have to travel the whole length of the spinal cord twice, and the brain would need time to think — exactly the delay the textbook warns could get us burnt ✔.

  6. Answer (i) The input and output nerves first meet in the spinal cord, so the route is shortest. (ii) The link there is simple, with no thinking step. The brain is informed afterwards (you feel pain) but does not control the reflex.

    Common trap: "The brain decides to pull the foot away." The spinal cord makes the reflex connection; the brain only learns of it.

Worked example 3

A broken link in the arc

Question. In a reflex arc for the hand, suppose the motor neuron going to the arm muscle is damaged, but everything else works. The person touches a hot iron. What happens to (a) the reflex, and (b) the feeling of heat? Explain.

Given: motor neuron damaged; receptors, sensory neuron and spinal cord normal. Asked: effect on the reflex and on the feeling.

Concept used: the order of parts in the reflex arc; the message to the brain (this topic). Why it applies: each part passes the message to the next.

  1. 1

    Receptors in the skin detect the heat → the sensory neuron carries the impulse to the spinal cord. This still works.

  2. 2

    In the spinal cord, the relay neuron passes the message on — but the motor neuron cannot carry it to the arm muscle.

  3. 3

    (a) So the effector (muscle) gets no message, and the hand is not pulled away by reflex.

  4. 4

    (b) The message also goes to the brain along the spinal cord, so the person still feels the heat and pain.

  5. Check: the break is after the branch to the brain on the pathway card, so only the action is lost, not the feeling ✔.

  6. Answer (a) The reflex fails — the arm muscle receives no impulse. (b) The heat is still felt, because the sensory message still reaches the spinal cord and goes on to the brain.

    Common trap: "Nothing will be felt." The feeling depends on the sensory side and the message to the brain, which are undamaged.

Common mistakes!

  • "The brain decides a reflex action."

    Thinking is too slow; the link is made in the spinal cord.

    The reflex arc is in the spinal cord; the brain is informed afterwards.

  • "Every involuntary action is a reflex."

    Heartbeat, breathing and salivation are controlled by the mid- and hind-brain, not by reflex arcs.

    Reflex = sudden response through a reflex arc (spinal cord); involuntary = heartbeat, breathing, salivation, vomiting, blood pressure — controlled by the mid- and hind-brain (medulla).

  • Sensory and motor neurons swapped

    The sensory neuron carries inwards.

    Receptor → sensory → spinal cord → motor → muscle.

  • Arrows drawn from the muscle towards the skin

    The impulse starts at the receptor.

    Arrows: skin → spinal cord → muscle (plus one arrow up to the brain).

  • Labels without leader lines, or "Effector" left out

    Marks go for every labelled part.

    Label all seven parts of the textbook's figure with ruler lines.

  • "Walking is a reflex because we don't think about each step."

    We decide to walk; the cerebellum keeps it precise and balanced.

    Walking is voluntary.

Key points

  • A reflex action is a sudden action in response to a stimulus, made without thinking.
  • Reflex arc: receptor → sensory neuron → relay neuron in the spinal cord → motor neuron → effector (muscle or gland).
  • Reflex arcs are formed in the spinal cord, where nerves from the body first meet; the message also goes to the brain, which is informed but does not make the quick response.
  • Reflex arcs evolved because the brain's thinking is not fast enough; they remain more efficient for quick responses.
  • Voluntary actions (writing, talking) are decided by the brain; involuntary actions (heartbeat, breathing, salivation, vomiting, blood pressure) are controlled by the mid-brain and hind-brain, many by the medulla.
