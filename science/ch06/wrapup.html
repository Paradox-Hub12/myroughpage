Wrap-up

Two ways to send a message

About 40 minutes · 10 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe how a message travels in a neuron and across a synapse.
  • Draw and label a neuron.
  • Distinguish voluntary, involuntary and reflex actions.
  • Draw and label a reflex arc.
  • Describe the parts of the brain and their functions.
  • Label the human brain.
  • Compare the two kinds of plant movement.
  • Explain the role of auxin.
  • Compare nervous and hormonal coordination.
  • Label the endocrine glands.

Every topic in this chapter is about sending a message from where a change is detected to where something must be done. Animals use two kinds of message — electrical impulses in nerves and hormones in the blood; plants use chemicals (and, in the sensitive plant, electrical-chemical signals from cell to cell). Many questions on this chapter ask you to compare — for example "Compare and contrast nervous and hormonal mechanisms for control and coordination in animals" (NCERT Exercise Q11). This page puts the chapter on three cards and then works through five mixed questions.

Card 1 — Nervous and hormonal control in animals

Point Nervous control Hormonal control
Made up of neurons (brain, spinal cord, nerves) endocrine glands and their hormones
Form of the message electrical impulse along a neuron (chemicals only across the synapse) chemical — a hormone
How it travels along nerve fibres in the blood
Reaches only cells connected by nervous tissue all cells of the body that respond to it (target organs)
Speed fast slower
How long it lasts short; a neuron needs time to reset before sending the next impulse steady and persistent — longer-lasting
Example pulling the hand away from a flame (reflex) adrenaline in an emergency; insulin and blood sugar

How they work together: in an emergency, nerves give the instant reaction, and adrenaline keeps the whole body ready for as long as needed. Hormone levels are kept right by feedback, and the brain links the two systems: the hypothalamus releases hormones that act on the pituitary gland.

Card 2 — Control in plants and animals

Point Plants Animals
Nervous system and muscles none nervous tissue and muscle tissue
How information is passed electrical-chemical means from cell to cell (sensitive plant); hormones that diffuse (growth movements) electrical impulses in neurons; hormones in the blood
How cells change shape to move by changing their water content (swelling or shrinking) special proteins in muscle cells change shape and arrangement
Movement due to growth tropisms — directional growth towards or away from light, gravity, water, chemicals, touch growth is controlled (e.g. growth hormone), but not directional towards a stimulus
Hormones auxin, gibberellins, cytokinins, abscisic acid growth hormone, thyroxin, insulin, adrenaline, testosterone, oestrogen, releasing hormones

The one-line rule: plants respond without nerves or muscles — quickly by water movement, or slowly by hormone-controlled growth.

Card 3 — Draw it in the exam

Rules for every biology diagram

  1. Use a sharp pencil; draw neat outlines, no shading, about a third to half a page in size.
  2. Draw every label line with a ruler; lines must touch the part and must not cross.
  3. Write labels horizontally, lined up on one or both sides, in neat print.
  4. Write a title under the diagram (e.g. "Structure of a neuron").
  5. Add arrows wherever a message or impulse travels — in the direction it travels.
  6. Learn the label set — your textbook's figure labels are listed below exactly as printed.

Five mixed worked examples

Mixed example 1

One minute in the exam hall

Question. During an exam, Kabir (a) reads a question and writes the answer; (b) his heart beats steadily throughout; (c) a dropped compass pricks his foot and he jerks it away at once; (d) when the invigilator announces "ten minutes left", his heart races and he breathes faster for several minutes. For each, name the kind of control and the part of the body (or gland) in charge.

Given: four responses. Asked: the kind of control and what is in charge.

Concept used: voluntary, involuntary and reflex actions (Topics 2–3); adrenaline (Topic 5). Why it applies: each response uses a different control route.

  1. 1

    (a) Writing an answer is decided → voluntary action, controlled by the fore-brain (thinking and motor areas).

  2. 2

    (b) The steady heartbeat goes on without thinking → involuntary action, controlled by the mid-brain and hind-brain.

  3. 3

    (c) Jerking the foot away → reflex action, through a reflex arc in the spinal cord.

  4. 4

    (d) A racing heart and faster breathing that last for minutes → hormonal control: adrenaline from the adrenal glands, carried in the blood.

  5. Check: the only response that lasted minutes after the stimulus is (d) — the mark of a hormone, which acts steadily and persistently ✔.

  6. Answer (a) voluntary — fore-brain (b) involuntary — mid- and hind-brain (c) reflex — spinal cord (d) hormonal — adrenaline (adrenal glands)

    Common trap: Calling (d) a reflex. A reflex is a single quick response; the long-lasting racing heart is due to adrenaline.

Mixed example 2

Where do chemicals carry the message?

Question. In which of these is a message carried by a chemical, and how does the chemical reach its target? (a) from one neuron to the next; (b) from the adrenal glands to the heart; (c) from a shoot tip to the cells on the shady side; (d) along the axon of a neuron.

Given: four message routes. Asked: which use chemicals; how the chemical travels.

Concept used: synapse (Topic 1); hormones in animals (Topic 5); plant hormones (Topic 4). Why it applies: chemical messages appear in all three systems.

  1. 1

    (a) Yes — chemicals released at the nerve ending cross the synapse to the next neuron's dendrite.

  2. 2

    (b) Yes — adrenaline, a hormone, is carried in the blood.

  3. 3

    (c) Yes — auxin diffuses from the shoot tip to the shady side.

  4. 4

    (d) No — along the axon the message is an electrical impulse.

  5. Check: the one electrical route, (d), is the only one inside a single cell ✔.

  6. Answer (a) chemicals across the synapse (b) adrenaline in the blood (c) auxin by diffusion. (d) is electrical.

    Common trap: Thinking "nerves = electrical only". The synapse is a chemical step.

Mixed example 3

Spot the errors in a reflex-arc drawing

Question. A student's drawing of a reflex arc for touching a hot pan shows: arrows running from the arm muscle up to the spinal cord and down to the skin; the neuron from the skin labelled "motor neuron"; the connection between the two neurons drawn inside the brain; and no label on the arm muscle. List the errors and correct each.

Given: a faulty diagram. Asked: the errors and corrections.

Concept used: the reflex arc and its labels (Topic 2). Why it applies: each error breaks the order or the labels of the textbook's figure.

  1. 1

    Arrows reversed → the impulse starts at the receptors in the skin: arrows go skin → spinal cord → muscle.

  2. 2

    Neuron from the skin mislabelled → it is the sensory neuron; the one to the muscle is the motor neuron.

  3. 3

    Connection in the brain → the reflex arc is made in the spinal cord (CNS), by a relay neuron; only a "message to brain" goes up.

  4. 4

    Unlabelled muscle → label it "Effector = Muscle in arm".

  5. Check: after the corrections, reading the arrows gives the pathway card exactly: receptor → sensory neuron → relay neuron (spinal cord) → motor neuron → effector ✔.

  6. Answer Reverse the arrows (skin → cord → muscle); swap to "sensory neuron" from the skin; move the connection to the spinal cord (relay neuron) with a separate "message to brain"; label the effector.

    Common trap: Correcting the arrows but leaving the connection in the brain — the arc is in the spinal cord.

Mixed example 4

A child who is not growing well

Question. A doctor checks a ten-year-old who is much shorter than her classmates. From your textbook, name two hormones whose shortage could slow a child's growth, with the gland for each, and say what each does.

Given: poor growth in a child. Asked: two hormones, their glands and functions.

Concept used: hormones and growth (Topic 5). Why it applies: two hormones in Table 6.1 are linked to body growth.

  1. 1

    Growth hormone — from the pituitary gland; regulates growth and development; a deficiency in childhood leads to dwarfism.

  2. 2

    Thyroxin — from the thyroid gland; regulates carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism to give the best balance for growth; it needs iodine.

  3. Check: both appear in Table 6.1 with a growth function: "stimulates growth in all organs" and "regulates metabolism for body growth" ✔.

  4. Answer Growth hormone (pituitary) — growth and development; thyroxin (thyroid, needs iodine) — metabolism for body growth.

    Common trap: Naming insulin. Insulin regulates blood sugar; Table 6.1 does not list it as a growth hormone.

Mixed example 5

Fast or slow, nerve or hormone?

Question. Say whether each response is brought about mainly by nerve impulses or by hormones, and give a reason: (a) blinking when an insect flies at your eye; (b) the changes in a boy's body at puberty; (c) a sunflower seedling's shoot bending towards light over two days; (d) the blood sugar level falling back to normal after lunch.

Given: four responses. Asked: nerve or hormone; reason.

Concept used: Card 1 (this page); plant hormones (Topic 4). Why it applies: speed and duration point to the kind of message.

  1. 1

    (a) Nerve impulses — an instant, short response (a reflex).

  2. 2

    (b) Hormones — testosterone from the testes; slow changes over years.

  3. 3

    (c) Hormones — auxin in the plant; slow growth over days (plants have no nerves).

  4. 4

    (d) Hormones — insulin from the pancreas, controlled by feedback.

  5. Check: the only fast, short-lived response, (a), is the only one carried by nerves ✔.

  6. Answer (a) nerves (b) hormones — testosterone (c) hormones — auxin (d) hormones — insulin

    Common trap: (c) "nerve impulses in the stem". Plants have no nervous system.
