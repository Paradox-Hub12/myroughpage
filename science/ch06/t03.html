Topic 3 of 5

The human brain and protection of the nervous system

About 60 minutes · 5 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Name the parts of the central and peripheral nervous systems.
  • Describe the three regions of the brain — fore-brain, mid-brain and hind-brain — their parts (cerebrum, hypothalamus, pons, medulla, cerebellum) and their functions.
  • Explain how the brain and spinal cord are protected.
  • Explain how nervous tissue causes action (how muscle cells shorten), and which signals are disrupted by a spinal-cord injury.
  • Draw and label the human brain.

In one minute of a cricket match a fielder sees the ball leave the bat, judges its path, runs, keeps his balance, dives, closes his fingers round the ball — and his heart keeps beating and his lungs keep breathing without a single thought from him. One organ, about the size of two fists, is running all of this at once. How does it divide the work?

Quick reminder (Topic 2)

Reflex actions are handled by reflex arcs in the spinal cord. Voluntary actions need thinking; involuntary actions go on without our thinking control.

  1. Central and peripheral nervous systems

The spinal cord does more than reflexes: it is made of nerves that carry information to the brain for thinking. Thinking needs much more complex connections between neurons, and these are concentrated in the brain, the main coordinating centre of the body.

  • Central nervous system (CNS): the brain and the spinal cord. They collect information from the whole body and integrate it (put it together).
  • Peripheral nervous system (PNS): the nerves that connect the CNS with the other parts of the body — cranial nerves, which arise from the brain, and spinal nerves, which arise from the spinal cord.

When we decide to write or move a chair (voluntary actions), the brain itself has to send orders to the muscles — a second route to the muscles, besides reflex arcs.

  1. Three regions of the brain

The brain has three major regions: the fore-brain, the mid-brain and the hind-brain. Each handles different inputs and outputs.

Fore-brain — the main thinking part. Its largest part, the folded cerebrum, fills most of the skull.

  • It has areas that receive sensory impulses from receptors — different areas handle hearing, smell and sight.
  • Association areas make sense of this input by combining it with input from other receptors and with what is already stored in the brain (memory).
  • A decision is made, and motor areas send it on to control voluntary muscles, such as the muscles of the leg.
  • The feeling of being full after eating comes from a centre associated with hunger, in another part of the fore-brain. (Other books name this part the hypothalamus.)
  • The hypothalamus, at the base of the fore-brain, also controls the release of hormones from the pituitary gland just below it (Topic 5).

Mid-brain and hind-brain — actions without thinking. Many involuntary actions are controlled by the mid-brain and hind-brain. The hind-brain has three parts:

  • Medulla — controls involuntary actions such as blood pressure, salivation and vomiting. The spinal cord continues down from it.
  • Cerebellum — responsible for the precision of voluntary actions and for keeping the body's posture and balance — walking along a straight line, balancing on a bicycle, picking up a pencil neatly.
  • Pons — also part of the hind-brain; your textbook names it on the brain diagram but gives it no separate job to learn.
Region Parts on the diagram Functions (as in your textbook)
Fore-brain cerebrum; hypothalamus thinking; sensory areas (hearing, smell, sight); association areas; motor areas for voluntary muscles; hunger centre

Memory hook (just a memory aid): Cerebellum = balance, the little brain at the back; Medulla = "must-happen" jobs you cannot stop by thinking.

  1. How the brain and spinal cord are protected

Such a delicate and important organ needs careful protection:

  • The brain is enclosed in a bony box — the cranium (skull).
  • Inside the box, the brain is held in a fluid-filled balloon, which absorbs shocks.
  • The spinal cord is protected by the vertebral column (backbone) — the hard, bumpy ridge you can feel down the middle of your back.

  1. How nervous tissue causes action

The nervous system collects information, carries it, decides, and passes the decision to the muscles — but the muscle tissue does the final job.

  1. When a nerve impulse reaches a muscle, the muscle cells change their shape so that they shorten.
  2. Muscle cells contain special proteins. When nerve impulses arrive, these proteins alter their shape and rearrange themselves inside the cell.
  3. The new arrangement of these proteins gives the muscle cells a shorter form — the muscle contracts and the part moves.

Muscles are of two kinds (Class 9): voluntary muscles, such as those of the arms and legs, work under our thinking control through the fore-brain's motor areas; involuntary muscles, such as those of the heart and gut, work without our thinking control.

  1. If the spinal cord is injured

Nerves from the body run up the spinal cord to the brain, and orders from the brain run down it to the muscles. An injury to the spinal cord disrupts both kinds of signal for the parts of the body whose nerves join the cord below the injury:

  • signals from receptors to the brain (the person may not feel touch or pain there), and
  • signals from the brain to the muscles (the person may not be able to move those parts at will).

Reflex arcs that pass through the damaged part of the cord are also affected.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Which part is at work?

Question. Name the part of the brain mainly responsible in each case: (a) holding a yoga pose on one leg without wobbling; (b) feeling that you have eaten enough at lunch; (c) solving a sum in your head; (d) vomiting after eating stale food; (e) threading a needle precisely.

Given: five activities. Asked: the part of the brain for each.

Concept used: functions of the parts of the brain (this topic). Why it applies: each activity matches one function in the table.

  1. 1

    (a) Posture and balance → cerebellum (hind-brain).

  2. 2

    (b) Feeling full → the hunger centre in a separate part of the fore-brain.

  3. 3

    (c) Thinking → fore-brain (cerebrum), the main thinking part.

  4. 4

    (d) Vomiting, an involuntary action → medulla (hind-brain).

  5. 5

    (e) Precision of a voluntary action → cerebellum.

  6. Check: the two hind-brain answers split cleanly — medulla for the involuntary action (d), cerebellum for balance and precision (a, e) ✔.

  7. Answer (a) cerebellum (b) fore-brain (hunger centre) (c) fore-brain (cerebrum) (d) medulla (e) cerebellum

    Common trap: Giving "cerebrum" for balance because it is the "big" brain. Balance and posture belong to the cerebellum.

Worked example 2

Two injuries, two results

Question. Two people have spinal-cord injuries: Mr A in the neck, Mr B in the lower back. Which person is likely to lose movement and feeling in more of the body? Which signals are disrupted? Keep to your textbook's ideas.

Given: injuries at two heights of the spinal cord. Asked: who is affected more; which signals.

Concept used: the spinal cord carries signals between the body and the brain; nerves join it along its length (this topic). Why it applies: only nerves joining below the injury lose their connection with the brain.

  1. 1

    Nerves from the body join the spinal cord along its whole length and carry signals up to the brain; orders from the brain travel down the cord to the muscles.

  2. 2

    An injury cuts the path at that point, so signals from parts whose nerves join below it cannot reach the brain, and the brain's orders cannot reach their muscles.

  3. 3

    Mr A (neck): the injury is high up, so nerves from the arms, trunk and legs all join below it → more of the body is affected.

  4. 4

    Mr B (lower back): only nerves joining below the lower back — mainly from the legs — are cut off.

  5. 5

    Signals disrupted: sensory signals from receptors to the brain, and motor signals from the brain to the muscles.

  6. Check: a higher injury has more of the cord below it, so it can only affect the same parts or more ✔.

  7. Answer Mr A, with the neck injury, is likely to be affected in more of the body. In both, messages from receptors to the brain and from the brain to the muscles are disrupted for the parts below the injury.

    Common trap: Saying "only the brain's thinking is affected". The brain is unharmed; it is the path for signals that is cut.

Worked example 3

A helmet and three layers of safety

Question. A cyclist's helmet takes a hard knock in a fall, but her brain is not injured. Besides the helmet, name the protection her body itself gives the brain, and the protection it gives the spinal cord.

Given: a knock to the head. Asked: the body's own protection for the brain and spinal cord.

Concept used: protection of the CNS (this topic). Why it applies: the body has its own "helmet" and "shock absorber".

  1. 1

    Bony box → the cranium (skull) surrounds the brain, like a hard helmet.

  2. 2

    Fluid-filled balloon → inside the skull, the brain is held in fluid, which absorbs shocks so the brain does not strike the bone.

  3. 3

    Spinal cord → protected by the vertebral column (backbone).

  4. Check: the helmet adds a fourth layer outside the skull; the textbook's three protections are all inside the body ✔.

  5. Answer Brain: the cranium (bony box) and the fluid-filled balloon (shock absorber). Spinal cord: the vertebral column.

    Common trap: Writing "the skull protects the spinal cord". The skull protects the brain; the backbone protects the spinal cord.

Quick check · 3 of 4Short answer2 marks

A muscle receives a nerve impulse. What happens inside its cells that makes the muscle shorten?

Answer: Muscle cells contain special proteins. In response to the nervous electrical impulse, these proteins alter their shape and rearrange themselves in the cell; the new arrangement gives the muscle cells a shorter form, so the muscle contracts.

Three points: special proteins · change shape and arrangement · cells become shorter.

Common mistakes!

  • "The cerebrum controls balance and posture."

    That is the cerebellum.

    Cerebrum — thinking; cerebellum — posture, balance, precision.

  • "The cerebellum controls blood pressure and vomiting."

    These are the medulla's jobs.

    Medulla — blood pressure, salivation, vomiting.

  • "The medulla and the pons are the same thing" or "the pons controls vomiting"

    They are two separate parts of the hind-brain.

    Medulla leads into the spinal cord; your textbook gives the pons no separate job.

  • "The hypothalamus is part of the hind-brain."

    It is at the base of the fore-brain.

    Hind-brain = pons, medulla, cerebellum only.

  • Cerebellum drawn at the front, or medulla at the top

    Wrong positions cost marks.

    Face on the left: cerebellum at the lower back; medulla runs into the spinal cord.

  • "The skull protects the spinal cord."

    The skull protects the brain.

    Vertebral column protects the spinal cord.

Key points

  • CNS = brain + spinal cord (receive and integrate information). PNS = cranial nerves (from the brain) + spinal nerves (from the spinal cord).
  • Fore-brain (cerebrum): main thinking part — sensory areas (hearing, smell, sight), association areas, motor areas for voluntary muscles; hunger centre in a separate part.
  • Mid-brain and hind-brain: many involuntary actions. Medulla — blood pressure, salivation, vomiting. Cerebellum — posture, balance, precision of voluntary actions. Pons — part of the hind-brain.
  • Protection: cranium (bony box) + fluid-filled balloon for the brain; vertebral column for the spinal cord.
  • Muscle cells shorten when special proteins change their shape and arrangement in response to nerve impulses.
  • A spinal-cord injury cuts signals from receptors to the brain and from the brain to muscles, for parts below the injury.
