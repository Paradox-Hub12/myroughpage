Why control and coordination? Receptors and neurons

About 55 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why living organisms need systems for control and coordination.
  • Describe receptors (gustatory, olfactory and others), where they are found, and what goes wrong if they do not work.
  • Describe the structure of a neuron and how a message travels — as an electrical impulse along the neuron and as chemicals across the synapse — including the neuromuscular junction.
  • Draw and label the structure of a neuron.

You are walking barefoot on the terrace in May and step onto a sun-baked tile. Before you have even said "ouch", your foot is already off it. In that fraction of a second, your skin noticed the heat, a message raced along your nerves, and the right leg muscles pulled your foot up — not your arm, not your eyelids. How does a body pick exactly the right response, every time, so fast?

Quick reminder (Class 9)

Nervous tissue is made of nerve cells (neurons) that carry messages; muscle tissue contracts to move parts of the body. Diffusion (Chapter 5) is the spreading of a substance from where there is more of it to where there is less — that is how chemicals cross the tiny gap between two neurons.

  1. Why living things need control and coordination

We usually take movement as a sign of life. Some movements come from growth — a seedling pushing up through the soil. Others have nothing to do with growth — a cat chasing a mouse, a buffalo chewing cud, children on a swing.

Most of these movements are responses to a change in the surroundings, and each change gets its own, suitable response. In class you whisper to a friend instead of shouting; you pull your hand away from a hot vessel but keep holding a cool one. So an organism must:

  1. detect what has changed (the stimulus),
  2. choose the correct response, and
  3. carry it out with the right body part, at the right time.

Doing this needs control (deciding the right response) and coordination (making different organs work together for it). Multicellular organisms use specialised tissues for the job. In animals these are nervous tissue and muscular tissue; in plants, which have neither, chemicals (and, for quick movements such as the sensitive plant's, electrical-chemical signals from cell to cell) do the job (Topic 4).

  1. Receptors — where information enters

Every piece of information about the surroundings is picked up by receptors. A receptor is the specialised tip of certain nerve cells. Receptors usually sit in the sense organs — tongue, nose, eyes, skin and inner ear.

Receptor Detects Found in
Gustatory receptors taste tongue
Olfactory receptors smell nose
Heat and pain receptors heat, pain skin

If receptors do not work, that information does not enter the nervous system, so the body cannot respond. A person who cannot feel heat in the fingers may hold a hot vessel until the skin is burnt. Food tastes flat when olfactory receptors are blocked by a cold, because much of a food's flavour comes from its smell (try NCERT Activity 6.1: eat a little sugar, then eat it again with your nose pinched shut).

  1. The neuron — the messenger cell

Nervous tissue is an organised network of neurons, specialised to carry information around the body as electrical impulses. A neuron has four main parts:

Part What it looks like Job
Dendrite short, branched threads around the cell body picks up information (at its tip)
Cell body the widest part, with the nucleus the impulse passes through it
Axon one long fibre carries the impulse away from the cell body
Nerve ending small branches at the end of the axon releases chemicals that pass the message on

  1. How the message travels — electrical, then chemical

Follow the message in order:

  1. Information is picked up at the tip of a dendrite. There it starts a chemical reaction, which produces an electrical impulse.
  2. The impulse travels dendrite → cell body → axon → nerve ending — this is the direction of travel inside a neuron.
  3. When the impulse reaches the axon's far end, it makes the nerve ending release some chemicals.
  4. The chemicals cross a tiny gap, the synapse, and set off a fresh electrical impulse in the next neuron's dendrite.

So the message is electrical inside a neuron and chemical across the synapse. The chemicals are released only at the axon's end, so the message can cross a synapse in one direction only — from the axon end of one neuron to the dendrite of the next.

Answer to the textbook's question on the neuron diagram: information is picked up at the dendrite (its tips); it travels as an electrical impulse through the cell body and along the axon; it is converted into a chemical signal at the nerve ending (end of the axon).

  1. The last link — the neuromuscular junction

The chain of neurons must finally reach the cells that act — muscle cells or glands. The last neuron's axon ends on a muscle fibre at a neuromuscular junction, which works like a synapse: chemicals from the nerve ending start the response in the muscle cell. Muscles and glands that respond are called effectors (Topic 2).

Worked example 1

Laddoo with a cold

Question. Ananya has a heavy cold and a blocked nose. At a family function she says the besan laddoo "tastes of nothing — just sweet and dry". Her cousin, with no cold, enjoys its rich flavour. Explain the difference using receptors.

Given: Ananya's nose is blocked; her cousin's is not. Asked: why the laddoo seems flavourless to Ananya.

Concept used: gustatory receptors (tongue) detect taste; olfactory receptors (nose) detect smell; the flavour of food uses both (this topic). Why it applies: only one of the two sets of receptors is working for Ananya.

  1. 1

    Her gustatory receptors still work → the tongue still detects sweetness, which is why the laddoo still tastes "just sweet".

  2. 2

    Her olfactory receptors cannot detect smells → the blocked nose stops the smell of roasted besan and ghee from reaching them.

  3. 3

    Much of what we call flavour comes from smell → without that information, the brain receives only the basic taste.

  4. 4

    Her cousin gets information from both sets of receptors, so the laddoo tastes rich.

  5. Check: this matches NCERT Activity 6.1 — sugar tastes different when the nose is pinched shut ✔.

  6. Answer The blocked nose stops Ananya's olfactory receptors from detecting the laddoo's smell; her gustatory receptors on the tongue still detect sweetness. Flavour needs both, so the laddoo seems flat to her.

    Common trap: Saying "her tongue stops working during a cold". The tongue's receptors still work; it is the smell information that is missing.

Worked example 2

Which receptor is at work?

Question. Name the receptor (or the sense organ holding it) that first detects each stimulus: (a) the smell of mitti after the first rain; (b) the bitterness of karela; (c) the heat of a tawa touched by mistake; (d) a whistle blown in the playground.

Given: four stimuli. Asked: the receptor or sense organ for each.

Concept used: receptors are specialised tips of nerve cells located in sense organs (this topic). Why it applies: each kind of stimulus is detected by its own receptors.

  1. 1

    (a) A smell → olfactory receptors in the nose.

  2. 2

    (b) A taste → gustatory receptors on the tongue.

  3. 3

    (c) Heat → heat (and pain) receptors in the skin of the hand.

  4. 4

    (d) A sound → receptors in the inner ear.

  5. Check: each answer pairs one kind of stimulus with one sense organ — no receptor has been given two jobs ✔.

  6. Answer (a) olfactory receptors (nose) (b) gustatory receptors (tongue) (c) heat/pain receptors (skin) (d) receptors in the inner ear

    Common trap: Swapping the two long names. Gustatory — gulp (taste); olfactory — odour (smell). (Just a memory aid.)

Worked example 3

One message, two neurons

Question. A message has to pass from neuron A to neuron B. Starting at the tip of A's dendrite, name every part the message passes through until it is moving again inside B, and say whether it is electrical or chemical at each stage.

Given: two neurons, A and B, linked by a synapse. Asked: the parts in order, and the form of the message at each.

Concept used: the route through a neuron and across a synapse (this topic). Why it applies: the message changes form at the synapse.

  1. 1

    Dendrite tip of A → information picked up; a chemical reaction creates an electrical impulse.

  2. 2

    Dendrite → cell body → axon of A → the impulse travels as an electrical impulse.

  3. 3

    Nerve ending of A → the electrical impulse causes chemicals to be released.

  4. 4

    Synapse → the chemicals cross the gap.

  5. 5

    Dendrite of B → the chemicals start a new electrical impulse, which travels on through B's cell body and axon.

  6. Check: the order in A is dendrite → cell body → axon → nerve ending, and the message enters B at a dendrite, as the synapse diagram shows ✔.

  7. Answer Dendrite of A (electrical) → cell body → axon (electrical) → nerve ending (chemicals released) → synapse (chemical) → dendrite of B (electrical again).

    Common trap: Writing "the electrical impulse jumps across the synapse". It is chemicals that cross the gap.

Common mistakes!

  • "The impulse travels axon → cell body → dendrite."

    That is the reverse.

    Inside a neuron: dendrite → cell body → axon → nerve ending.

  • "An electrical impulse crosses the synapse."

    The gap is crossed by chemicals.

    Electrical along the neuron; chemical across the synapse.

  • "Receptors are the sense organs."

    Receptors are the specialised tips of nerve cells.

    Receptors are located in sense organs (tongue, nose, inner ear, skin).

  • Dendrite and axon swapped on a neuron diagram

    The two look different.

    Many short, branched dendrites; one long axon.

  • Neuron drawn without a nucleus, or labels without lines

    Marks are lost for missing or unclear labels.

    Draw the nucleus in the cell body; ruler line from every label to its part.

  • "Gustatory = smell."

    The two names are easily swapped.

    Gustatory — taste; olfactory — smell (odour).

Key points

  • Organisms detect a change, choose a suitable response and act; this needs control and coordination. In animals it is done by nervous and muscular tissue.
  • Receptors are specialised tips of nerve cells, located in sense organs: gustatory (taste, tongue), olfactory (smell, nose), heat and pain (skin), and others in the inner ear and eye.
  • A neuron has dendrites, a cell body (with nucleus), an axon and nerve endings.
  • Inside a neuron the message is an electrical impulse: dendrite → cell body → axon → nerve ending.
  • At the nerve ending, chemicals are released; they cross the synapse and start a new impulse in the next neuron's dendrite — one way only.
  • Neurons hand the message to muscles or glands at junctions such as the neuromuscular junction.
