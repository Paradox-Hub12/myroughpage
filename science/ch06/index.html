Chapter 6 · Unit II

Control and Coordination

5 topicsAbout 6–7 hoursBoard exam
Why this chapter matters

A cricket ball is hit hard towards you. In under a second your eyes track it, your legs move, your hands close round the ball — and all the while your heart keeps beating and your lungs keep breathing without a single thought from you. A neem seedling on a dark verandah, meanwhile, slowly bends its shoot towards the light, with no brain or nerves at all. How do living things pick the right response to every change, and get every part to work together?

This chapter answers with two ways of sending messages: electrical impulses along nerves, and chemicals (hormones) that travel round the body. You will follow a message from a receptor to a muscle, find out why a reflex does not wait for the brain, learn which part of the brain does what, see how plants respond without nerves or muscles, and meet the hormones that prepare you for an emergency or keep your blood sugar in balance. It belongs to Unit II, World of Living. In the two CBSE sample papers it supplies questions on the parts of the brain and the actions they control (both papers), a draw-and-explain question on nerve cells and an MCQ on insulin (2025-26), and questions on the sensitive plant and on how muscles move (2026-27).

What you'll learn

  • Explain why organisms need control and coordination; describe receptors; describe a neuron and how a message travels — electrical along the neuron, chemical across the synapse; draw and label a neuron.
  • Tell voluntary, involuntary and reflex actions apart; describe the reflex arc and why it is made in the spinal cord; draw and label a reflex arc.
  • Name the central and peripheral nervous systems; describe the fore-, mid- and hind-brain and their parts' jobs; explain how the brain and spinal cord are protected and how muscles move; label the brain.
  • Compare the sensitive plant's quick movement with growth movements; describe the tropisms; explain how auxin makes a shoot bend; describe the four plant hormones; design tropism experiments.
  • Explain chemical coordination; match each hormone to its gland and job; explain the adrenaline response and the feedback control of insulin; label the endocrine glands.
  • Compare nervous and hormonal control, and plants and animals, on one card; use the "draw it in the exam" checklist and the label-the-diagram trainer.

Most of the marks in this chapter come from three skills, and every topic practises them:

  1. Trace a pathway in order — stimulus → receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord → motor neuron → effector; or blood sugar → pancreas → insulin.
  2. Draw and label — the neuron, reflex arc, brain and endocrine glands each have a "Draw it in the exam" checklist and a plain line version to copy.
  3. Compare — tables with matched rows, the way marking schemes award marks.
