Topic 4 of 5

Coordination in plants

About 75 minutes · 5 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Distinguish immediate movement in response to a stimulus (the sensitive plant — no growth) from movement due to growth (tropic movements).
  • Describe phototropism, geotropism, hydrotropism and chemotropism, and the response of tendrils to touch, with examples.
  • Explain the role of auxin in phototropism and in the coiling of tendrils.
  • Describe plant hormones and the roles of auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins and abscisic acid.
  • Design and explain experiments on tropic movements (phototropism; hydrotropism).

Keep a tulsi pot on a window-sill for a week and its stems all lean towards the glass. Turn the pot round, and a few days later the new growth is leaning towards the light again. Brush a chhui-mui leaf with your finger and its leaflets fold up within seconds. A plant has no brain, no nerves and no muscles. So how does it "know" where the light is — and how does it move?

Quick reminder (Class 9)

Plants grow in length at the tips of shoots and roots, where cells divide actively (meristematic tissue). Cells behind the tip then grow longer.

  1. Two kinds of plant movement

Plants have no nervous system and no muscles, yet they respond to stimuli. They show two different kinds of movement:

Point Immediate response — sensitive plant Movement due to growth — shoot bending to light
Growth involved? no yes — it is directional growth
Speed very quick (seconds) slow (days)
Depends on the direction of the stimulus? no — the leaves fold wherever they are touched yes — towards or away from the stimulus
How the cells change cells swell or shrink by changing their water content cells on one side grow longer than on the other (hormones)
If growth is stopped still happens does not happen

  1. Immediate response: the sensitive plant

When the leaves of chhui-mui (the sensitive or touch-me-not plant, Mimosa family) are touched, they fold up and droop. No growth is involved, so the plant must actually move its leaves.

  • The leaves move somewhere other than the spot that was touched, so the news of the touch must be passed on.
  • Plant cells pass it from cell to cell by electrical-chemical means — but, unlike animals, plants have no specialised tissue for conducting information.
  • To move, some cells must change shape. Instead of the special proteins of animal muscle cells, plant cells alter their shape by gaining or losing water — they swell or shrink.

  1. Movement due to growth: tropisms

More often, plants respond slowly, by growing in one particular direction; since the growth has a direction, the plant seems to move. Such directional growth movements are called tropic movements (tropisms). A plant part may bend towards or away from the stimulus.

Tropism Stimulus Response (example)
Phototropism light shoot grows towards light; root grows away (seedling bending to a window)
Geotropism gravity root grows down (positively geotropic); shoot grows up (negatively geotropic) (pot laid on its side)
Hydrotropism water root grows towards water (roots bending to moist soil)
Chemotropism chemicals pollen tube grows towards the ovule

Tendrils of a pea plant are sensitive to touch. The side of the tendril touching a support grows more slowly than the side away from it, so the tendril circles round the support and clings to it. (Other books call growth in response to touch thigmotropism; your textbook does not use this word.)

  1. Slow and fast — why chemicals are used

The chhui-mui responds in seconds; a sunflower's response to day and night is quite slow; growth movements are slower still. Fast responses need fast messages, and electrical impulses are fast. But they have limits:

  • they reach only cells connected by nervous tissue, not every cell;
  • after sending one impulse, a cell needs time to reset before it can send another, so cells cannot keep sending impulses continuously.

So most multicellular organisms also use chemical communication. A chemical released by a stimulated cell diffuses all around; cells with special molecules on their surface detect it. This is slower, but it can reach all cells, whether or not they are joined by nerves, and it can work steadily and persistently. These chemicals are hormones.

  1. Plant hormones

Plant hormones coordinate a plant's growth, its development and its responses to the environment. Each is made in one part of the plant and diffuses to the part where it acts.

Hormone What it does Growth effect Where your textbook places it
Auxin helps cells grow longer promotes made at the shoot tip
Gibberellins help the stem grow (as auxins do) promotes —
Cytokinins promote cell division promotes high in areas of rapid cell division — fruits and seeds
Abscisic acid inhibits growth; for example, it causes wilting of leaves inhibits —

Plants need signals to stop growing, too — that is the job of abscisic acid.

  1. How auxin makes a shoot bend towards light

  1. Auxin is made at the shoot tip and helps the cells below it grow longer.
  2. When light comes from one side, auxin spreads to the shaded side of the shoot.
  3. The extra auxin makes the cells on the shady side (away from the light) grow longer than those on the lit side.
  4. A shoot that grows more on one side bends towards the other — so the shoot bends towards the light.

The light does not "pull" the shoot; the shoot bends because its shady side grows faster.

Tendrils: the same idea of unequal growth explains coiling — the cells on the side away from the support grow faster, and auxin (which makes cells grow longer) brings about this extra growth, so the tendril wraps round the support.

Virtual lab — Activity 6.2: shoots and roots respond to light

What you do:

  1. Fill a conical flask with water and cover its neck with a wire mesh.
  2. Place two or three bean seeds that have just germinated on the mesh, with their young roots hanging into the water.
  3. Put the flask in a cardboard box open on one side, with the open side facing light from a window.
  4. After two or three days, note the direction of the shoots and roots. Then rotate the flask half a turn, so the shoots now point away from the light and the roots towards it; leave it for a few more days.

What you observe:

  1. After the first days, the shoots bend towards the light and the roots bend away from it.
  2. After the flask is rotated, the parts that grew earlier keep their old direction.
  3. The new growth bends again: new shoot growth towards the light, new root growth away from it.

What it shows:

  1. Shoots and roots respond to the direction of light in opposite ways — phototropism.
  2. The response is a growth movement: only the growing parts change direction; parts that have finished growing stay as they were.
  3. The bending is caused by unequal growth of cells on the two sides (auxin, section 6).

Virtual lab — Designing an experiment to show hydrotropism

What you do:

  1. Fill a deep box or plastic tub (at least 15 cm deep) with soil or sawdust that is only slightly damp — just enough for seeds to germinate.
  2. Sow a few soaked seeds (for example, gram) in a line across the middle of the tray.
  3. At one end of the tray, bury a small porous clay pot (unglazed) filled with water. Water seeps slowly out of it, so the soil near the pot becomes much wetter than the rest.
  4. Set up a control tray in exactly the same way but without the clay pot, so the soil is equally damp everywhere.
  5. After about a week, gently wash the soil away and look at the roots.

What you observe: In the test tray, the roots bend sideways towards the clay pot. In the control tray, the roots grow straight down.

What it shows: Roots grow towards water — hydrotropism. Because the roots in the test tray bend sideways, not just downwards, the bending is caused by water and not by gravity; the control tray shows how roots grow when the soil is equally damp everywhere.

Safety

handle the clay pot gently; wash your hands after handling soil.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

The pot that was turned round

Question. A potted tulsi plant has grown leaning towards a window. Meena turns the pot through half a turn so that the plant now leans away from the window. Describe what she will see after a week, and explain it.

Given: a shoot already bent towards light; pot turned round. Asked: the change after a week, with the reason.

Concept used: phototropism is a growth movement caused by auxin (this topic). Why it applies: only growing parts can change direction.

  1. 1

    The old, bent part of the stem stays bent away from the window — it has finished growing, so it cannot change direction.

  2. 2

    The growing tip detects light coming from the new side.

  3. 3

    Auxin made at the tip diffuses to the shady side (now facing the room).

  4. 4

    Cells on that shady side grow longer, so the new growth curves back towards the window.

  5. 5

    The stem ends up with a gentle S-shape: old part one way, new part towards the light.

  6. Check: this is exactly what Activity 6.2 shows when the flask is turned — old parts unchanged, new growth bends towards light ✔.

  7. Answer The old part stays as it was; the new growth bends towards the window, because auxin collects on the shady side and makes those cells grow longer.

    Common trap: "The whole plant will turn back." Only the growing region can bend.

Worked example 2

Roots in the drain pipe

Question. A plumber finds tree roots growing into a cracked, slowly leaking drain pipe more than a metre away from the tree, although the roots had to grow sideways to reach it. Name the response and explain why it is a tropic movement.

Given: roots grew sideways to a source of water. Asked: the response; why it counts as tropic.

Concept used: tropisms are directional growth movements towards or away from a stimulus (this topic). Why it applies: the roots grew in the direction of the water.

  1. 1

    The stimulus is water leaking from the pipe, which moistens the soil on that side.

  2. 2

    The roots grew towards the water → hydrotropism ("hydro" = water).

  3. 3

    It is a tropic movement because the root's growth is directional — it depends on the direction of the stimulus.

  4. 4

    Growing sideways shows the water was the cause: gravity alone would have sent the roots downwards.

  5. Check: the same reasoning as the hydrotropism virtual lab — roots bending sideways towards a porous pot ✔.

  6. Answer Hydrotropism — the roots grew towards the water, a directional growth response, so it is a tropic movement.

    Common trap: Calling it geotropism because "roots grow into the ground". Geotropism is a response to gravity (downwards); sideways growth to a pipe follows the water.

Worked example 3

How a pea tendril climbs

Question. A pea plant is grown next to a thin bamboo stick. One of its tendrils touches the stick and, a day later, has wound round it twice. Explain how this happens.

Given: a tendril in contact with a stick. Asked: the mechanism of coiling.

Concept used: tendrils are sensitive to touch; unequal growth; auxin helps cells grow longer (this topic). Why it applies: coiling is caused by one side growing more than the other.

  1. 1

    Tendrils are sensitive to touch; the touch of the stick is the stimulus.

  2. 2

    The side of the tendril touching the stick grows more slowly.

  3. 3

    The part away from the stick grows faster — auxin, which makes cells grow longer, brings about this extra growth.

  4. 4

    With its outer side longer than its inner side, the tendril curves round the stick and clings to it.

  5. Check: the outer side of any coil is longer than the inner side, which fits "the side away from the support grows more" ✔.

  6. Answer The touched side grows slowly while the side away from the stick grows faster (auxin promotes this growth), so the tendril coils round the support.

    Common trap: "The side touching the stick grows faster and pushes the tendril round." It is the other side that grows faster.

Worked example 4

A seedling laid on its side

Question. A pot with a young seedling is laid on its side in a dark cupboard for three days. Predict the direction of growth of the shoot and the root, name the response and explain why light cannot be the cause.

Given: pot horizontal; no light. Asked: predicted directions; the response; why not light.

Concept used: geotropism — shoots negatively geotropic, roots positively geotropic (this topic). Why it applies: gravity acts even in the dark.

  1. 1

    The only directional stimulus in the dark cupboard is gravity.

  2. 2

    The shoot curves upwards, away from the earth → negatively geotropic.

  3. 3

    The root curves downwards → positively geotropic.

  4. 4

    Light cannot be the cause, because there is no light in the cupboard.

  5. Check: the result matches the textbook's figure of a pot on its side — shoot labelled "Negatively geotropic", roots "Positively geotropic" ✔.

  6. Answer Shoot bends upwards, root bends downwards — geotropism; light is ruled out because the cupboard is dark.

    Common trap: Calling the shoot's upward bend phototropism. In the dark, only gravity can explain it.

Common mistakes!

  • "Auxin pulls the shoot towards light."

    Auxin moves away from light, to the shady side.

    Shady-side cells grow longer → shoot bends towards light.

  • "The chhui-mui folds because it grows" or "…because it has muscles."

    No growth and no muscles are involved.

    Cells swell or shrink by changing their water content.

  • "Abscisic acid promotes growth."

    It inhibits growth.

    Auxin, gibberellins, cytokinins promote; abscisic acid inhibits (wilting of leaves).

  • "Cytokinins make cells longer; auxin makes cells divide."

    The two jobs are swapped.

    Auxin — cells grow longer; cytokinins — cell division.

  • "Roots grow towards light."

    Roots grow away from light.

    Shoot: towards light, away from gravity. Root: away from light, towards gravity and water.

  • "The pollen tube growing to the ovule is hydrotropism."

    The stimulus is a chemical.

    Pollen tube → chemotropism.

  • "The side of the tendril touching the support grows faster."

    It grows less.

    The side away from the support grows more, so the tendril coils.

Key points

  • Plants have no nervous system and no muscles, but they respond to stimuli in two ways: immediate (no growth) and growth movements.
  • Sensitive plant: a touch is passed cell to cell by electrical-chemical means (no special conducting tissue); cells change shape by changing their water content.
  • Tropisms are directional growth movements: phototropism (shoot towards light, root away), geotropism (root down — positively; shoot up — negatively), hydrotropism (towards water), chemotropism (pollen tube to ovule). Tendrils respond to touch.
  • Plant hormones are made away from where they act and diffuse there. Auxin (shoot tip) — cells grow longer; gibberellins — stem growth; cytokinins — cell division (fruits, seeds); abscisic acid — inhibits growth (wilting).
  • Phototropism: auxin diffuses to the shady side → those cells grow longer → shoot bends towards light.
  • Electrical impulses are fast but reach only nerve-connected cells and cannot be sent continuously; chemical (hormonal) messages are slower but reach all cells and act steadily.
