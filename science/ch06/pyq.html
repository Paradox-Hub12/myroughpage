CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section A, Q11 option A2 marks

Question: Which parts of the brain perform certain actions for which we do not have any thinking control? What are such actions called, give two examples.

Given: actions without thinking control. Asked: the brain parts; the name of such actions; two examples.

Concept used: involuntary actions and the mid-brain and hind-brain (Topics 2 and 3). Why it applies: the textbook links actions without thinking control to these two regions.

  1. 1

    Parts of the brain → the mid-brain and the hind-brain control many actions over which we have no thinking control.

  2. 2

    Name → such actions are called involuntary actions.

  3. 3

    Examples → blood pressure, salivation and vomiting (controlled by the medulla in the hind-brain); the heartbeat is another.

  4. Check: these are not reflex actions — they are not sudden responses through a reflex arc in the spinal cord ✔.

    Common trap: Answering "spinal cord — reflex actions". The question asks about parts of the brain, and reflexes are made in the spinal cord.
The actions over which we do not have any thinking control are controlled by the mid-brain and hind-brain (½). These are called involuntary actions (½). Examples (any two): blood pressure, salivation, vomiting (½ + ½). — 2 marks (split inferred; the scheme gives only the total)
CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section A, Q11 option B2 marks

Question: How is the information of touch in the ‘sensitive plant' controlled and coordinated?

Given: a sensitive plant (chhui-mui) is touched. Asked: how the information is passed and how the movement happens.

Concept used: immediate response to a stimulus in plants (Topic 4). Why it applies: the plant has no nerves or muscles, yet moves its leaves.

  1. 1

    Passing the information → the leaves move away from the spot that was touched, so the news of the touch is passed from cell to cell by electrical-chemical means (plants have no specialised conducting tissue).

  2. 2

    Changing shape → plant cells alter their shape by gaining or losing water — they swell or shrink.

  3. 3

    Movement → this change of shape makes the leaves fold and droop.

  4. Check: no growth is involved, which is why the response is so quick ✔.

    Common trap: "The sensitive plant has nerves in its leaves" or "it moves by growing". It has no nerves, and the movement involves no growth.
The information of touch is communicated by electrical-chemical means from cell to cell (1). Plant cells change shape by changing the amount of water in them, resulting in swelling or shrinking, thus changing shapes and causing movement (1). — 2 marks (split inferred)
CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section A, Q133 marks

Question: How do muscles move at the cellular level and respond when processed messages are received from the brain?

Given: a message from the brain reaches a muscle. Asked: how muscle cells move.

Concept used: how nervous tissue causes action (Topic 3). Why it applies: the muscle does the final job of any action.

  1. 1

    Muscle cells shorten → when an impulse arrives at a muscle, its cells change shape and become shorter.

  2. 2

    Special proteins → muscle cells contain special proteins; the electrical impulses make these proteins alter their shape and rearrange themselves.

  3. 3

    Shorter form → the new arrangement of these proteins gives the muscle cells a shorter form, so the muscle contracts and the body part moves.

  4. Check: compare the sensitive plant, whose cells change shape by changing their water content — muscle cells use proteins instead ✔.

    Common trap: "The muscle cells grow shorter." Nothing grows; the proteins rearrange.
  • When a nerve impulse reaches the muscle, the muscle cells move by changing their shape so that they shorten. (1)
  • Muscle cells have special proteins that change both their shape and their arrangement in the cell in response to nervous electrical impulses. (1)
  • When this happens, new arrangements of these proteins give the muscle cells a shorter form. (1) — 3 marks (split inferred: one mark per point)
CBSE SQP 2025-26, Section A, Q31 mark

Question: Which of the following is a correct combination of function and part of the brain?
A. Posture and balance: Cerebrum
B. Salivation: Medulla in midbrain
C. Hunger: Pons in hindbrain
D. Blood pressure: Medulla in hindbrain

Given: four function–part pairs. Asked: the correct one.

Concept used: parts of the brain and their functions (Topic 3). Why it applies: each option names a function, a part and a region — all three must be right.

  1. 1

    A ✘ → posture and balance belong to the cerebellum, not the cerebrum.

  2. 2

    B ✘ → salivation is controlled by the medulla, but the medulla is in the hind-brain, not the mid-brain.

  3. 3

    C ✘ → the hunger centre is in the fore-brain, not the pons.

  4. 4

    D ✔ → blood pressure is controlled by the medulla, which is in the hind-brain.

  5. Check: only D has the right function, the right part and the right region ✔.

    Common trap: Choosing B because "salivation — medulla" is right. Read the region too: the medulla is in the hind-brain.
D. Blood pressure: Medulla in hindbrain
1 mark
CBSE SQP 2025-26, Section A, Q41 mark

Question: The blood glucose level in a patient was very high. It may be due to inadequate secretion of:
A. growth hormone from pituitary gland
B. oestrogen from ovary
C. insulin from pituitary gland
D. insulin from pancreas

Given: very high blood glucose. Asked: which hormone shortage, from which gland, could cause it.

Concept used: insulin and blood sugar (Topic 5). Why it applies: insulin regulates blood sugar.

  1. 1

    Insulin regulates the blood sugar level; if too little is released, blood sugar rises.

  2. 2

    Insulin is produced by the pancreas → D.

  3. 3

    C names the right hormone but the wrong gland (pituitary). A and B are hormones that do not regulate blood sugar.

  4. Check: D is the only option with both the right hormone and the right gland ✔.

    Common trap: Choosing C because it says "insulin". Check the gland as well as the hormone.
D. insulin from pancreas
1 mark
CBSE SQP 2025-26, Section A, Q133 marks

Question: Draw and explain how the nerve cells help in transmission of impulses?

Given: nerve cells (neurons). Asked: a diagram and an explanation of how impulses are transmitted.

Concept used: structure of a neuron; how a message travels and crosses a synapse (Topic 1). Why it applies: this is the textbook's general scheme of how nerve impulses travel.

  1. 1

    Draw a neuron: cell body with nucleus, branched dendrites, a long axon and nerve endings; label them (see the exam line version of the neuron diagram in Topic 1).

  2. 2

    Picking up information → receptors — the specialised tips of some nerve cells — detect information; at the tip of a dendrite this starts a chemical reaction, which produces an electrical impulse.

  3. 3

    Along the neuron → the impulse passes from the dendrite through the cell body and along the axon; at the axon's end it makes the nerve ending release chemicals.

  4. 4

    Across the synapse → the chemicals cross the gap (synapse) and set off a new electrical impulse in the next neuron's dendrite. A similar junction passes impulses from neurons to muscle cells (neuromuscular junction).

  5. Check: the three explanation points follow the message in order — dendrite → cell body → axon → synapse → next dendrite ✔.

    Common trap: Drawing the neuron but explaining only its parts. The question asks how impulses are transmitted — include the synapse step.
  • All information from our environment is detected by the specialised tips of some nerve cells. The information acquired at the end of the dendritic tip of a nerve cell sets off a chemical reaction that creates an electrical impulse. (1)
  • This impulse travels from the dendrite to the cell body, and then along the axon to its end. At the end of the axon, the electrical impulse sets off the release of some chemicals. (1)
  • These chemicals cross the gap, or synapse, and start a similar electrical impulse in a dendrite of the next neuron. (1)
  • Diagram: labelled structure of a neuron (and the neuromuscular junction), as in the scheme. — 3 marks (split inferred: the scheme gives three points and the figures with a total of 3; draw the labelled neuron as part of your answer)
