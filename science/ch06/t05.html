Topic 5 of 5

Hormones in animals

About 65 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain how chemical coordination takes place in animals and why it is needed alongside the nervous system.
  • Name the endocrine glands, their hormones and the functions of these hormones (NCERT Table 6.1 and Fig. 6.7), including thyroxin and iodine, growth hormone, insulin, adrenaline, testosterone, oestrogen and releasing hormones.
  • Explain the body's response to adrenaline and the feedback control of insulin.
  • Label the endocrine glands in the human body.

A street dog suddenly barks and runs at you. In seconds your heart is pounding, you are breathing fast, your mouth feels dry and your legs are ready to sprint. No single nerve could have told your heart, lungs, gut, skin and leg muscles all at once to get ready. Something else spread the alarm through your whole body — in your blood.

Quick reminder (Chapter 5)

The pancreas pours pancreatic juice into the small intestine. Blood carries dissolved substances to every part of the body. Glucose from digested food is absorbed into the blood.

  1. Why animals need hormones as well as nerves

Think of a squirrel in a frightening situation. Its body must prepare either to fight or to run away — two very different activities, but both need a lot of energy and many tissues working together. Some common preparations can be made in advance.

If the body used only electrical impulses through nerves, only the tissues connected by nerves would be told to prepare. A chemical signal sent in the blood can reach all cells and bring about wide-ranging changes. These chemical messengers are hormones.

Endocrine glands release hormones directly into the blood, which carries them to the target organs or tissues on which they act. Working alongside the nervous system, the endocrine system gives the body a second system of control and coordination.

  1. Adrenaline — ready for an emergency

Adrenaline is secreted by the adrenal glands (one above each kidney) straight into the blood, which takes it all round the body.

  • Its target organs include the heart: the heart beats faster, supplying more oxygen to the muscles.
  • Muscles around the small arteries of the digestive system and skin contract, reducing the blood going there; this diverts blood to the skeletal muscles.
  • The breathing rate increases because of contractions of the diaphragm and rib muscles.

Together, these changes make the body ready to deal with the situation.

  1. Hormones control growth, too

Animals do not bend towards light like plants, but their growth is still carefully controlled: nobody grows a finger on the forehead. Hormones help keep the body's design during growth.

Thyroxin and iodine. The thyroid gland, in the neck, makes thyroxin. Iodine is essential for making thyroxin. Thyroxin controls how the body uses carbohydrates, proteins and fats (metabolism), keeping the right balance for growth. If iodine is lacking in the diet, a person may suffer from goitre, in which the neck swells (where the thyroid gland lies). This is why iodised salt is advised.

Growth hormone. The pituitary gland secretes growth hormone, which controls the body's growth and development. A deficiency in childhood leads to dwarfism. (Beyond the syllabus: too much growth hormone in childhood is linked to giants. Your textbook asks you to know only that a deficiency causes dwarfism.)

Puberty. The big changes in the body at about 10–12 years of age are caused by testosterone (from the testes) in males and oestrogen (from the ovaries) in females.

Insulin. The pancreas produces insulin, which helps regulate the blood sugar level. If the pancreas does not release enough insulin, the blood sugar level climbs too high and harms the body — this is diabetes. Some patients are treated with injections of insulin.

  1. Feedback — the right amount at the right time

Hormones must be released in precise amounts. The timing and amount are regulated by feedback mechanisms. For insulin:

  1. After a meal, the blood sugar level rises.
  2. Cells of the pancreas detect the rise and respond by producing more insulin.
  3. Insulin brings the blood sugar level down.
  4. As the blood sugar level drops, the pancreas releases less insulin.

It works like the thermostat of an air-conditioner: as the room gets hotter the machine works harder, and as the room cools it eases off. In the same way, the result of the hormone controls its own release.

Hormone Endocrine gland Function

Other glands on the diagram. The figure of endocrine glands (NCERT Fig. 6.7) also labels the pineal gland, the parathyroid glands and the thymus. Learn where they are, so you can label them; your textbook does not ask you to learn their hormones.

NCERT information box

The hypothalamus has a big say in when many hormones are released. For instance, if growth hormone runs low, the hypothalamus releases a growth hormone releasing factor, which stimulates the pituitary gland to release growth hormone.

Worked example 1

The barking dog

Question. Rohan is chased by a barking dog and runs home. Name the hormone that prepares his body, the gland that secretes it and how it reaches its targets. List the changes it brings about, in order, and say how each helps him run.

Given: a frightening situation. Asked: hormone, gland, route, changes and their use.

Concept used: adrenaline and its effects (this topic). Why it applies: the body prepares for fighting or running away.

  1. 1

    Hormone and gland → adrenaline, from the adrenal glands.

  2. 2

    Route → secreted directly into the blood, which carries it to the target organs.

  3. 3

    Heart beats faster → more oxygen reaches his leg muscles.

  4. 4

    Muscles round the small arteries of the gut and skin contract → less blood goes there, so more blood is diverted to the skeletal muscles.

  5. 5

    Breathing rate increases (diaphragm and rib muscles contract more) → more oxygen is taken in.

  6. 6

    Together → his muscles get the extra oxygen and blood they need to run.

  7. Check: every change sends more blood or oxygen to the skeletal muscles — what both fighting and running need ✔.

  8. Answer Adrenaline from the adrenal glands, carried in the blood: faster heartbeat, blood diverted from gut and skin to skeletal muscles, faster breathing — more oxygen for running.

    Common trap: "Adrenaline is carried by nerves to the heart." Hormones travel in the blood.

Worked example 2

A swollen neck in a hill village

Question. A health worker finds many people with swollen necks in a hill village where the food grown locally is poor in iodine. Name the condition, the gland and hormone involved, and suggest a simple remedy.

Given: swollen necks; diet poor in iodine. Asked: condition, gland, hormone, remedy.

Concept used: thyroxin needs iodine; lack of iodine can cause goitre (this topic). Why it applies: the symptom and its position match the thyroid gland.

  1. 1

    The swollen neck is a symptom of goitre.

  2. 2

    The swelling is in the neck, where the thyroid gland lies.

  3. 3

    The thyroid makes thyroxin, and iodine is essential to make it.

  4. 4

    With too little iodine in the diet, thyroxin cannot be made properly → goitre.

  5. 5

    Remedy: use iodised salt in cooking, so the thyroid gets the iodine it needs.

  6. Check: the swelling's position (neck) matches the thyroid's position on the endocrine-gland diagram ✔.

  7. Answer Goitre — thyroid gland, thyroxin; caused by too little iodine. Remedy: iodised salt.

    Common trap: "Iodine is a hormone." Iodine is a mineral needed to make the hormone thyroxin.

Worked example 3

After the jalebis

Question. Priya, who is healthy, eats three jalebis. Describe what happens to her blood sugar and insulin over the next hour or two. Why does her grandmother, who has diabetes, need insulin injections?

Given: a sugary snack; a healthy girl; a grandmother with diabetes. Asked: the feedback sequence; reason for injections.

Concept used: feedback control of insulin (this topic). Why it applies: sugar from the snack raises the blood sugar level.

  1. 1

    Sugar from the jalebis is absorbed → Priya's blood sugar rises.

  2. 2

    Cells of her pancreas detect the rise and produce more insulin.

  3. 3

    Insulin helps bring the blood sugar down towards normal.

  4. 4

    As the level falls, her pancreas reduces insulin secretion — feedback.

  5. 5

    In her grandmother, insulin is not secreted in proper amounts, so her blood sugar stays high, causing harmful effects. Injections supply the insulin her pancreas does not provide, helping to regulate her blood sugar.

  6. Check: in Priya the loop switches itself off as sugar falls; in her grandmother the loop is broken at step 2, so the injection replaces that step ✔.

  7. Answer Blood sugar rises → pancreas releases more insulin → sugar falls → less insulin released. The grandmother's pancreas does not secrete enough insulin, so injected insulin is needed to regulate her blood sugar.

    Common trap: "Insulin is made in the pituitary gland." It is made in the pancreas.

Common mistakes!

  • "Insulin is made by the pituitary."

    Insulin is a pancreas hormone.

    Insulin — pancreas; growth hormone — pituitary.

  • "Adrenaline comes from the thyroid."

    It comes from the adrenal glands on the kidneys.

    Adrenaline — adrenal; thyroxin — thyroid.

  • "Thyroxin can be made without iodine" or "iron is needed for thyroxin."

    Iodine is essential.

    Thyroid + iodine → thyroxin; lack → goitre.

  • "Lack of growth hormone causes giants."

    A lack in childhood causes dwarfism.

    Too little growth hormone in childhood → dwarfism.

  • "Hormones work as fast as nerves" or "travel along nerves."

    Hormones travel in the blood — slower but widespread and lasting.

    Nerves: fast, electrical, to connected cells. Hormones: slower, in blood, to all target cells.

  • "Releasing hormones come from the pituitary."

    They act on the pituitary.

    Hypothalamus → releasing hormones → stimulate the pituitary.

  • Glands placed wrongly on the diagram (adrenals in the neck, thyroid in the head)

    Positions are marked.

    Head: hypothalamus, pineal, pituitary; neck: thyroid, parathyroids; chest: thymus; abdomen: adrenals (on kidneys), pancreas; testes / ovaries.

Key points

  • Endocrine glands release hormones directly into the blood, which carries them to target organs; this is the second way of control and coordination.
  • Adrenaline (adrenal glands): heart beats faster; blood diverted from gut and skin to skeletal muscles; faster breathing — body ready for an emergency.
  • Thyroxin (thyroid) regulates carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism; needs iodine — lack → goitre (use iodised salt).
  • Growth hormone (pituitary) — growth and development; deficiency in childhood → dwarfism. Testosterone (testes) and oestrogen (ovaries) — changes at puberty.
  • Insulin (pancreas) regulates blood sugar; too little → blood sugar rises (diabetes). Releasing hormones (hypothalamus) stimulate the pituitary.
  • Feedback: blood sugar up → pancreas makes more insulin → sugar down → less insulin.
