Domestic electric circuits

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the household supply (mains; live — red, neutral — black, 220 V; earth — green), the meter, the main fuse, the main switch and the two separate circuits (15 A and 5 A), and draw the schematic diagram (Fig 12.15).
  • Explain why appliances are connected in parallel with separate switches, and the function of the earth wire and earthing.
  • Explain short-circuiting and overloading (causes and effects), the role of the fuse, and the precautions; use I = P/V to decide whether a circuit or fuse rating is exceeded.

Open a three-pin plug and you find three wires in three colours. Switch on a heater, an iron and a kettle together and the lights may suddenly go out. Why three wires, why does a fuse "blow", and why does a metal toaster need a wire that normally carries no current at all?

Quick reminder (Chapter 11)

Appliances joined in parallel each get the full supply p.d. and work independently. A fuse is a short wire of suitable melting point placed in series; when the current is too large it melts and breaks the circuit. The current an appliance draws is I=PVI = \dfrac{P}{V}I=VP​.

  1. The supply that comes into a house

Electric power reaches our homes through the mains, carried on overhead poles or underground cables. The supply has two wires:

  • the live wire (or positive), usually with red insulation;
  • the neutral wire (or negative), with black insulation.

In India the potential difference between the live and the neutral wire is 220 V (and the supply is AC at 50 Hz — Topic 5). (The names "positive" and "negative" are your textbook's; with AC the live wire does not stay positive — the names only tell the two wires apart.)

Inside the house (Fig 12.15):

  1. At the meter-board, the wires pass through the main fuse (the electricity board's fuse) into the electricity meter, which records the energy used.
  2. From the meter they go through the main switch and on to the line wires that run through the house.
  3. The line wires feed separate circuits, each with its own fuse in the distribution box. Often there are two: a 15 A circuit for appliances with high power ratings such as geysers and air coolers, and a 5 A circuit for bulbs, fans, etc.

  1. Appliances are joined in parallel

In each circuit, the appliances are connected across the live and neutral wires — that is, in parallel with each other — and each has its own switch to turn it on or off. In parallel, every appliance gets the same potential difference (the full 220 V), and switching one off does not affect the others.

  1. The earth wire — a safety wire

The third wire, the earth wire, has green insulation. It is usually connected to a metal plate buried deep in the earth near the house.

  • It is a safety measure, used especially for appliances with a metallic body — an electric press (iron), toaster, table fan, refrigerator.
  • The metal body is connected to the earth wire, which gives a low-resistance conducting path for the current.
  • If a fault makes current leak to the metal body, the earth wire keeps the body at the same potential as the earth, so the user does not get a severe electric shock.

Normally the earth wire carries no current — the appliance works through the live and neutral wires. The earth wire matters only when something goes wrong.

  1. Short-circuiting and overloading

Overloading means a current larger than the circuit is meant to carry. Your textbook names its causes:

  1. Short-circuiting — the live wire and the neutral wire come into direct contact. This happens when the insulation of the wires is damaged or there is a fault in an appliance. The current in the circuit then increases abruptly.
  2. An accidental hike in the supply voltage.
  3. Connecting too many appliances to a single socket.

  1. The fuse protects the circuit

The electric fuse is an important part of every domestic circuit. In an overload, the Joule heating in the fuse wire melts it and breaks the circuit, stopping the unduly high current before it damages the appliances or the wiring. So the fuse is the most important safety device against short-circuiting and overloading.

  • The fuse goes in series with the circuit it protects, in the live wire — then, when it melts, the appliances are cut off from the live side of the supply. (Your textbook shows the fuses at the meter and in the distribution box; placing them in the live wire is the usual practice.)
  • A blown fuse must be replaced by one of the same rating. A higher-rated fuse (or a piece of thick wire) would not melt in time, and the wiring or appliance could be damaged.

Precautions against overloading: do not connect too many appliances to one socket; run high-power appliances (geyser, heater, air cooler) on the 15 A circuit; use fuses of the correct rating; keep wires and appliances in good repair so that the insulation is not damaged.

Two safety measures commonly used in electric circuits and appliances are therefore the electric fuse and earthing.

Worked example 3

When a live wire touches the metal body

Question. Inside a metal-bodied toaster, the insulation of the live wire wears through and the bare wire touches the metal case. (a) What happens if the toaster is properly earthed? (b) What could happen if the earth wire were missing?

Given: live wire touching the metal body. Asked: the effect with and without earthing.

Concept used: the earth wire gives a low-resistance path to the earth and keeps the body at the earth's potential (this topic).

  1. 1

    (a) With earthing → the leaked current flows through the earth wire (a low-resistance path) to the earth. The metal body stays at the potential of the earth, so a person touching it does not get a severe shock. (Beyond your textbook: the large current in this low-resistance path can also melt the fuse and cut off the toaster.)

  2. 2

    (b) Without earthing → the metal body is at the potential of the live wire. A person who touches it becomes the path to the earth and can get a severe electric shock.

  3. Check: this is exactly the purpose your textbook gives for the earth wire ✔.

  4. Answer (a) The current leaks safely to the earth through the earth wire; the body stays at earth potential, so the user is not severely shocked. (b) The body would be "live", and touching it could give a severe shock.

    Common trap: "the earth wire makes the toaster work". The toaster works through the live and neutral wires; the earth wire is only for safety.

Worked example 4

Short circuit or overload?

Question. For each event, say whether it is a short circuit, and whether it overloads the circuit: (a) the cord of a table lamp is crushed under a cupboard and the live and neutral wires touch; (b) a heater, an iron and a kettle are all plugged into one socket through an adapter; (c) the supply voltage suddenly rises for a few seconds.

Given: three events. Asked: short circuit? overload?

Concept used: short-circuiting = live and neutral in direct contact; overloading and its three causes (this topic).

  1. 1

    (a) Live touches neutral → a short circuit; the current rises abruptly → overload.

  2. 2

    (b) Too many appliances on one socket → not a short circuit, but an overload.

  3. 3

    (c) Hike in the supply voltage → not a short circuit, but it can cause an overload.

  4. Check: all three are the causes of overloading listed in your textbook; only (a) is a short circuit ✔.

  5. Answer (a) Short circuit → overload. (b) Overload (not a short circuit). (c) Overload (not a short circuit). In each case the fuse protects the circuit by melting.

    Common trap: "in a short circuit the current falls". The resistance of the path becomes very small, so the current rises abruptly.

Common mistakes!

  • "Green is the live wire."

    Live is red, neutral black, earth green.

    Learn the three as a set: red–live, black–neutral, green–earth.

  • "The earth wire carries the current that runs the appliance."

    The appliance works through live and neutral; the earth wire is a safety path.

    Earth wire → metal body to a buried plate; carries current only when there is a fault.

  • "In a short circuit the current drops."

    The live and neutral touch, so the resistance becomes very small.

    Short circuit → current increases abruptly → overload → fuse melts.

  • Treating overloading and short-circuiting as unrelated.

    Short-circuiting is one cause of overloading.

    Causes of overloading: short circuit, voltage hike, too many appliances on one socket.

  • Putting the fuse in parallel, or replacing it with a higher rating or a thick wire.

    In parallel it would not carry the current; a higher rating would not melt in time.

    Fuse in series, in the live wire; replace with the same rating.

  • "Each appliance gets only part of the 220 V."

    In parallel each gets the full p.d.

    Parallel → same p.d. (220 V) across every appliance; the currents add up.

Key points

  • The mains bring a live wire (red; also called positive) and a neutral wire (black; also called negative) with 220 V between them; the earth wire is green.
  • Order at the meter-board: main fuse → electricity meter → main switch → line wires → separate circuits (15 A for high-power appliances such as geysers and air coolers; 5 A for bulbs and fans).
  • Appliances are connected in parallel across live and neutral, each with its own switch, so each gets the same p.d.
  • Earthing: the metal body is joined to the earth wire, which goes to a metal plate deep in the earth; it gives a low-resistance path for leaked current and keeps the body at earth potential, so the user gets no severe shock.
  • Short-circuiting: live and neutral wires in direct contact (damaged insulation, faulty appliance) → current increases abruptly. Overloading is caused by a short circuit, a hike in supply voltage, or too many appliances on one socket.
  • The fuse (in series) melts by Joule heating and breaks the circuit — the most important safety device. Fuse and earthing are the two common safety measures.
