Topic 1 of 6

Magnets, magnetic field and field lines

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe a compass needle as a small magnet with north-seeking and south-seeking poles, recall that like poles repel and unlike poles attract, and define a magnetic field.
  • Describe how field lines are obtained (iron filings, Activity 12.2; compass, Activity 12.3) and draw the field lines of a bar magnet with correct arrows (Figs 12.2–12.4).
  • State the properties of field lines (direction, closed curves, crowding shows strength, never cross) and draw a uniform field.

Hold a compass near a fridge magnet and the needle swings round, even though nothing touches it. Sprinkle iron dust on paper over the magnet and the dust forms neat curved lines. Something invisible fills the space around a magnet — this topic shows you how to see it and draw it.

Quick reminder (Class 6)

Every magnet has two poles, a north pole (N) and a south pole (S). Like poles repel each other; unlike poles attract each other. A freely suspended magnet comes to rest pointing roughly north–south.

  1. A compass needle is a small magnet

A compass needle is a tiny bar magnet that can turn freely. Its ends point approximately north and south:

  • the end that points towards north is called the north-seeking or north pole of the needle;
  • the end that points towards south is its south-seeking or south pole.

Bring a bar magnet near a compass and the needle turns. Its poles are pushed and pulled by the poles of the magnet — the N pole of the needle is pushed away from the magnet's N pole and pulled towards the magnet's S pole.

  1. The magnetic field

A magnet affects things around it without touching them. The region surrounding a magnet, in which the force of the magnet can be detected, is said to have a magnetic field.

A magnetic field has both direction and magnitude (strength). Its direction at any point is taken to be the direction in which the north pole of a compass needle moves (points) when the compass is placed there.

Fix a white sheet on a drawing board, place a bar magnet in the middle, sprinkle iron filings evenly round it (a salt-sprinkler helps) and tap the board gently.

the filings arrange themselves in a pattern of curved lines running from one end of the magnet round to the other (Fig 12.2).
Shows
each filing feels a force from the magnet and lines up along the field. The lines along which the filings line up represent magnetic field lines.

  1. Drawing field lines with a compass (Activity 12.3)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Fix the magnet on a sheet and mark its outline. Place a small compass near its north pole. Mark the positions of both ends of the needle. Move the compass so that its south end sits where its north end was, and mark again. Repeat, step by step, until you reach the magnet's south pole. Join the dots with a smooth curve. Repeat from other starting points.
See
near the magnet's N pole, the needle's S end points towards the magnet and its N end points away. Each curve runs from the N pole round to the S pole. The needle swings more strongly as you move it closer to the poles.
Shows
each smooth curve is a field line (Fig 12.3), and together they make the field-line pattern of the magnet (Fig 12.4). The bigger swing near the poles shows that the field is strongest near the poles.

  1. Properties of magnetic field lines

Your textbook also defines a field line directly: a field line is the path along which a hypothetical free north pole would tend to move. ("Hypothetical" means imagined.)

  1. Direction: at any point, the field line points the way the north pole of a compass would point there. So, by convention, field lines come out of the north pole and go into the south pole outside the magnet.
  2. Closed curves: inside the magnet the lines run from the S pole to the N pole. So every field line is a closed curve — it has no start and no end.
  3. Strength: the field is stronger where the lines are closer together (crowded), as near the poles, and weaker where they are far apart.
  4. No crossing: two field lines never cross each other. If they did, a compass placed at the crossing point would have to point in two directions at once, which is impossible — the field at a point has only one direction.

A uniform field is a field that has the same strength and the same direction everywhere in a region. It is drawn as parallel straight lines, equally spaced, with all the arrows pointing the same way.

Worked example 1

Reading a field-line diagram

Question. A bar magnet lies on a table with its N pole at the right end. A small compass is placed at three points: P, just beyond the N end, on the magnet's axis; Q, a little above the middle of the magnet; R, just beyond the S end, on the axis. (a) In which direction does the compass needle's north pole point at P, Q and R? (b) At which of these points is the field strongest?

Given: magnet with N at the right; points P (beyond N), Q (above the middle), R (beyond S). Asked: needle direction at each point; where the field is strongest.

Concept used: field lines leave N and enter S outside the magnet; a compass's north pole points along the field; crowded lines mean a strong field (this topic). Why it applies: the needle simply follows the field line through each point.

  1. 1

    (a) At P → the lines are coming out of the N end, so they point away from the magnet: the needle's N pole points right.

  2. 2

    At Q → the lines above the middle run from the N end (right) over to the S end (left): the needle's N pole points left.

  3. 3

    At R → the lines are going into the S end, which is to the right of R: the needle's N pole points right.

  4. 4

    (b) Strongest field → the lines are crowded near the poles and spread out above the middle, so the field is strongest at P and R (near the poles) and weakest at Q.

  5. Check: follow one line with your finger — out of N at the right, over the top towards the left, down into S. At Q your finger moves left; at R it moves right into S ✔.

  6. Answer (a) P: right; Q: left; R: right. (b) The field is stronger at P and R (near the poles) than at Q.

    Common trap: saying that above the middle the needle points right "because N is on the right". Outside the magnet the field runs from N towards S.

Worked example 2

Can these lines be right?

Question. Anika draws the field lines of a magnet. In her drawing, two of the lines cross each other at a point X. Her friend says the drawing must be wrong. Who is right, and why?

Given: two field lines crossing at X. Asked: is this possible?

Concept used: the direction of the field at a point is the direction a compass needle points there (this topic). Why it applies: at a crossing, the two lines give two different directions at one point.

  1. 1

    At X → each line has its own direction (its tangent), and the two directions are different.

  2. 2

    A compass at X → it would have to point along both lines at the same time.

  3. 3

    Impossible → a needle points in only one direction, because the field at a point has only one direction.

  4. Check: look at any real iron-filing pattern — the curves bend away from each other but never meet ✔.

  5. Answer The friend is right. Two field lines can never cross: at the crossing point a compass needle would have to point in two directions at once, which is not possible.

    Common trap: "they may cross where the field is very strong". Lines get closer where the field is strong, but they still never cross.

Worked example 3

Which end of the needle?

Question. A compass is placed close to the north pole of a bar magnet, on the magnet's axis. (a) Which end of the needle points towards the magnet? (b) Why?

Given: compass near the magnet's N pole. Asked: the end of the needle facing the magnet, and the reason.

Concept used: like poles repel, unlike poles attract; the needle's N pole points along the field (this topic).

  1. 1

    (a) Field direction there → the field lines come out of the magnet's N pole, so the field points away from the magnet.

  2. 2

    Needle → its N pole points along the field, away from the magnet; so its south pole points towards the magnet.

  3. 3

    (b) Reason in terms of poles → the magnet's N pole repels the needle's N pole and attracts its S pole.

  4. Check: this is exactly what your textbook reports in Activity 12.3 ✔.

  5. Answer (a) The needle's south pole points towards the magnet's north pole. (b) Unlike poles attract (N attracts the needle's S) and like poles repel (N pushes the needle's N away), so the needle lies along the field, which points away from N.

    Common trap: "the needle's north pole points to the magnet's north pole". Like poles repel.

Common mistakes!

  • Arrows from S to N outside the magnet, or N to S inside it.

    The convention is the way a compass's north pole points: outside it points away from N.

    Outside: out of N, into S. Inside: S to N. Every line is closed.

  • Drawing lines that cross or touch; drawing a uniform field with uneven gaps.

    At a crossing the field would have two directions; uneven gaps mean a changing strength.

    Never let lines meet. Uniform field: straight, parallel, equally spaced, arrows the same way.

  • "Lines far apart show a stronger field."

    Strength is shown by how crowded the lines are.

    Close lines → strong field; spread lines → weak field.

  • "The north pole of a compass points to the north pole of a magnet."

    Like poles repel.

    Near a magnet's N pole the needle's S end faces the magnet.

Key points

  • A compass needle is a small magnet; the end pointing north is its north (north-seeking) pole. Like poles repel, unlike poles attract.
  • A magnetic field is the region surrounding a magnet in which its force can be detected. It has direction and magnitude.
  • A field line is the path along which a hypothetical free north pole would tend to move; iron filings line up along field lines (Activity 12.2), and a compass can trace them (Activity 12.3).
  • The field direction at a point is the direction in which a compass's north pole points there: outside a magnet, lines go from N to S; inside, from S to N — they are closed curves.
  • Lines are closer where the field is stronger, and two field lines never cross. A uniform field is drawn as equally spaced parallel straight lines.
