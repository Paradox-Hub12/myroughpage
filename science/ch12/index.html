Chapter 12 · Unit IV

Magnetic Effects of Electric Current

6 topicsAbout 7–8 hoursBoard exam
Why this chapter matters

A phone-charger cable can turn a compass needle. A switch at a guard's desk can unlock a heavy school gate. A crane can pick up a car's worth of scrap and drop it at the press of a button. All of these work because an electric current makes a magnetic field — and a magnetic field pushes on a current. The same ideas decide why your house has three wires of three colours, and why a fuse sometimes "blows".

In this chapter you will learn what a magnetic field is and how to draw field lines, the field made by a current in a straight wire, a loop and a solenoid, how an electromagnet works, the force on a current in a magnetic field, the difference between direct and alternating current, and how domestic electric circuits are wired and kept safe. It belongs to Unit IV, Effects of Current. In the CBSE sample papers it supplies a 3-mark question on the right-hand thumb rule and a 4-mark case on a solenoid door lock (2026-27), and a 3-mark field-line diagram (2025-26).

What you'll learn

  • Define a magnetic field, draw the field lines of a bar magnet, and state their properties.
  • Describe the circular field round a straight wire, how it depends on current and distance, and find its direction with the right-hand thumb rule.
  • Draw the fields of a loop and a solenoid, find a solenoid's N and S ends, and explain electromagnets.
  • Describe the force on a current in a field, and find its direction with Fleming's left-hand rule — also for beams of charged particles.
  • Tell DC from AC, use the 50 Hz frequency, and give the advantage of AC.
  • Describe the live, neutral and earth wires, earthing, the fuse, short-circuiting and overloading, and check a circuit's current.
  • Decide which hand rule to use, and draw every field pattern correctly.

Most of the marks in this chapter come from four skills, and every topic practises them:

  1. Draw field lines correctly — arrows on every line, out of N and into S, never crossing, crowded where the field is strong.
  2. Pick the right hand rule — field round a current: right-hand thumb rule; force on a current: Fleming's left-hand rule.
  3. Turn electron beams round — the current is opposite to the motion of electrons.
  4. Explain home safety — the three wires, earthing, the fuse, short-circuiting and overloading.
