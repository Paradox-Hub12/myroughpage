Topic 3 of 6

Field of a circular loop and a solenoid; electromagnets

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe and draw the field of a current-carrying circular loop (Fig 12.8) and explain why a coil of n turns gives n times the field (Activity 12.6).
  • Define a solenoid, describe and draw its field (Fig 12.10), compare it with a bar magnet, find its N and S ends, and explain the uniform field inside.
  • Explain an electromagnet (soft iron core, Fig 12.11), how to make its field stronger, and how a permanent magnet can be made with a steel rod.

A scrapyard crane lifts a heap of iron, swings it across and drops it the moment the operator presses a switch. A school door unlocks with a click when the guard presses a button. Both use the same trick: wind the wire into a coil, and the weak field of a straight wire becomes a strong magnet you can switch on and off.

Quick reminder (Topic 2)

Round a straight wire the field lines are circles; the field is stronger close to the wire and stronger for a larger current. The right-hand thumb rule gives its direction.

  1. Bend the wire into a loop

Now bend the straight wire into a circular loop and pass a current through it (Fig 12.8).

  • Near the wire, every small piece of the loop still has its own small circles of field round it.
  • Towards the centre, these circles get bigger and bigger. By the time they reach the centre, the arcs of these big circles look like straight lines.
  • Apply the right-hand thumb rule to any piece of the loop: every piece makes the field inside the loop point the same way. So inside the loop the fields of all the pieces add up.

More turns, more field. The field at a point depends directly on the current producing it. In a circular coil of nnn turns, the current in every turn flows the same way round, so the fields of the turns simply add: the field is nnn times as large as that of a single turn.

Activity 12.6 — the pattern of a coil

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Push a circular coil of many turns through two holes in a rectangular cardboard, so that the coil stands upright through the card. Connect the coil in series with a battery, a key and a rheostat. Sprinkle iron filings evenly on the card, insert the key and tap the card gently.
See
close to each place where the wire passes through the card, the filings form small circles; between the two places, near the centre of the coil, they lie in nearly straight lines (Fig 12.9).
Shows
the field of a loop is circular close to the wire and almost straight and strong at the centre, as described above.

Which way through the loop? Use the thumb rule on the part of the loop nearest you and look where your fingers point inside the loop. The field lines come out of one face of the loop and go into the other. The face they come out of behaves like a north pole, and the other face like a south pole — just like the two ends of a very short bar magnet.

  1. The solenoid

A coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire wrapped closely in the shape of a cylinder is called a solenoid.

Pass a current through a solenoid and its field (Fig 12.10) looks very like the field of a bar magnet (compare with Topic 1):

  • Outside, the lines leave one end, curve round and enter the other end. One end of the solenoid behaves as a north pole and the other as a south pole.
  • Inside, the field lines are parallel straight lines, equally spaced. This shows that the field is the same at all points inside the solenoid — the field inside is uniform.
  • Field lines spread out near the ends: the field is weaker there and beyond.

Finding the N end.

  1. Look at one turn and use the right-hand thumb rule on the part of the turn nearest you to find which way the field points inside the coil.
  2. Inside a magnet, field lines run from S to N; so the end towards which the field inside points — the end where the lines come out — is the north pole.

A shortcut used in the CBSE sample-paper marking scheme (2026-27): curl the fingers of your right hand round the solenoid in the direction of the current in the turns; your thumb then points towards the north pole. (The marking scheme calls this the right-hand thumb rule too.) It always agrees with steps 1–2.

Reverse the current and the field inside points the other way, so the N and S ends change places.

  1. Electromagnets

The strong field inside a solenoid can magnetise a piece of magnetic material such as soft iron placed inside the coil (Fig 12.11). A magnet made in this way is called an electromagnet. An electromagnet consists of a core of soft iron wrapped round with a coil of insulated copper wire.

  • It is a magnet only while the current flows — switch the current off and the strong field disappears (this is how the electric door lock and the scrapyard crane work).
  • To make an electromagnet stronger: pass a larger current; use more turns of wire on the coil; and use a soft iron core inside it.
  • A permanent magnet can be made the same way by placing a steel rod inside the solenoid and passing a direct current: your textbook's Fig 12.11 shows a steel rod being magnetised, and steel keeps its magnetism after the current stops.

Worked example 1

A loop on the page

Question. A circular loop of wire lies flat on this page. The current flows round it anticlockwise as you look at the page. (a) What is the direction of the magnetic field at the centre of the loop? (b) Just outside the loop? (c) Which face of the loop — the one facing you or the one facing away — behaves like a north pole?

Given: loop in the plane of the page; current anticlockwise as seen. Asked: field at the centre; just outside; which face is N.

Concept used: right-hand thumb rule on a piece of the loop; field lines come out of the N face (this topic).

  1. 1

    Pick the top part of the loop → going anticlockwise, the current there flows to the left.

  2. 2

    Thumb → right thumb pointing left along that part; fingers curl round it.

  3. 3

    Below that part (towards the centre) → the fingers come out of the page.

  4. 4

    (a) Centre → every part of the loop gives the same result, so the field at the centre is out of the page (⊙\odot⊙).

  5. 5

    (b) Just outside (above the top part) → the fingers go into the page (⊗\otimes⊗).

  6. 6

    (c) N face → the field lines come out of the loop on your side, so the face towards you acts as a north pole.

  7. Check: test your right hand on your textbook's Fig 12.7 first (thumb down along the current → seen from above, the fingers go round clockwise). Then try the bottom part, where the current flows to the right: above it (at the centre) the fingers again point out of the page ✔.

  8. Answer (a) Out of the page (⊙\odot⊙). (b) Into the page (⊗\otimes⊗). (c) The face towards you behaves like a north pole.

    Common trap: using the thumb rule on the whole loop at once and pointing the thumb "round" the loop. The thumb always points along a straight piece of current.

Worked example 2

Which end is the north pole?

Question. A solenoid lies along the page from left to right. On the side of the coil facing you, the current in every turn flows down the page. (a) Which way does the field point inside the solenoid? (b) Which end is the north pole? (c) What happens if the battery connections are reversed?

Given: current down the page on the near side of each turn. Asked: field inside; the N end; the effect of reversing.

Concept used: right-hand thumb rule; the end where the field lines come out is N (this topic).

  1. 1

    Near side of a turn → right thumb pointing down the page along it.

  2. 2

    Behind that wire (inside the coil, away from you) → your fingers point to the right.

  3. 3

    (a) Field inside → points to the right, along the axis.

  4. 4

    (b) N end → the field lines come out at the right end, so the right end is the north pole (the left end is S).

  5. 5

    (c) Reversed → the current and the field inside reverse; now the left end is N and the right end S.

  6. Check: look at the right end face-on: the current goes round anticlockwise there — like the N face of the loop in Worked example 1 ✔. The marking-scheme shortcut agrees: curl the right-hand fingers with the current, and the thumb points right ✔.

  7. Answer (a) To the right. (b) The right end is the north pole. (c) The poles change places — the left end becomes N.

    Common trap: thinking a solenoid has a fixed N end. The poles depend on the direction of the current.

Worked example 3

A stronger electromagnet

Question. A student's electromagnet, made with 100 turns of insulated copper wire on an iron nail, can just lift 6 paper pins. (a) Give three changes that would let it lift more pins. (b) If she wound a coil of the same size with 300 turns instead of 100 and passed the same current, how would the field at its centre compare? (c) What would she use instead of the nail to make a permanent magnet?

Given: a coil of 100 turns on an iron core. Asked: three ways to strengthen it; the effect of 300 turns; a permanent magnet.

Concept used: the field depends on the current and the number of turns; a soft iron core strengthens it; steel keeps its magnetism (this topic).

  1. 1

    (a) Larger current → the field of every turn is stronger.

  2. 2

    More turns → the fields of more turns add up.

  3. 3

    Soft iron core → a core of soft iron becomes strongly magnetised inside the coil and adds to the field.

  4. 4

    (b) Turns → 300 is 3 times 100; for a circular coil carrying the same current, the field at the centre is nnn times that of one turn, so it is 3 times as large.

  5. 5

    (c) Permanent magnet → a steel rod in the coil, with a direct current; steel stays magnetised when the current stops.

  6. Check: each change in (a) matches one of the rules in this topic: field ∝ current, field ∝ number of turns, iron core ✔.

  7. Answer (a) Increase the current; increase the number of turns; use a soft iron core. (b) 3 times as strong at the centre of the coil. (c) A steel rod (with direct current).

    Common trap: "use thicker insulation" or "a longer wire with the same turns". Neither makes the field stronger.

Common mistakes!

  • "The field inside a solenoid is zero" or "strongest at the ends".

    Inside, the lines are parallel and equally spaced — the field is uniform; it weakens near the ends, where the lines spread out.

    Draw straight, equally spaced lines inside; spread them at the ends.

  • "One end of a solenoid is always N."

    The poles come from the direction of the current.

    Reverse the current → the poles swap. Find N each time with the rule.

  • "A solenoid's field is quite unlike a magnet's."

    Outside, its pattern matches a bar magnet's.

    Solenoid ↔ bar magnet: lines out of N, into S, S to N inside.

  • Pointing the thumb round the loop.

    The thumb rule is for a straight piece of current.

    Take one short, nearly straight part of the loop; thumb along it.

  • Using a steel core for an electromagnet that must switch off.

    Steel keeps its magnetism.

    Soft iron for an electromagnet; steel for a permanent magnet.

Key points

  • The field of a circular loop is circular near the wire and nearly straight at the centre; all parts of the loop make the field inside point the same way.
  • A coil of nnn turns gives nnn times the field of one turn (the turns' fields add).
  • A solenoid is a coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire wrapped closely in the shape of a cylinder. Its field is like a bar magnet's: one end N, the other S; the field inside is uniform (parallel straight lines).
  • N end: find the field inside with the right-hand thumb rule — the end where the field lines come out is N. Reversing the current swaps the poles.
  • An electromagnet is a soft iron core inside a current-carrying coil; it is a magnet only while the current flows. Stronger with more current, more turns and a soft iron core. A steel rod becomes a permanent magnet.
