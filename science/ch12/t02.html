Topic 2 of 6

Magnetic field of a current in a straight conductor

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe Oersted's observation (Activity 12.1) and Activity 12.4, and explain that a current produces a magnetic field whose direction reverses with the current.
  • Describe the field pattern round a straight wire (concentric circles, Activity 12.5, Fig 12.6) and how its strength depends on the current and the distance.
  • State and use the right-hand thumb rule (Fig 12.7) to find the field direction at a point, including with the into-the-page and out-of-the-page symbols and compass directions.

Lay a phone-charger cable over a compass and switch the charger on: the needle twitches away from north. There is no magnet in the cable — only a current. A current makes its own magnetic field, and one simple hand rule tells you which way it points.

Quick reminder (Chapter 11)

The current in a circuit diagram is the conventional current: it flows from the + terminal of the cell, round the outside circuit, to the − terminal — opposite to the flow of electrons.

  1. A current deflects a compass (Activity 12.1)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Connect a thick straight copper wire XY in a circuit with a cell, a resistor and a plug key, with XY held perpendicular to the paper. Place a small compass flat on the paper near the wire. Insert the key.
See
the compass needle is deflected when the current flows, and settles back when the key is taken out.
Shows
the current through the wire has produced a magnetic effect. Electricity and magnetism are linked.
Did you know?Not in board exam

In 1820 the Danish scientist Hans Christian Oersted noticed, by accident, that a compass needle moved when a current flowed in a wire near it. His discovery showed that electricity and magnetism are related. A unit of magnetic field strength, the oersted, is named after him.

  1. Reversing the current reverses the field (Activity 12.4)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Stretch a long straight copper wire over a compass, parallel to its needle (so the wire runs north–south). Connect it to two or three 1.5 V cells and a plug key, and insert the key. Then swap the cell connections so that the current flows the other way.
See
with the current flowing from north to south in the wire, the needle's north pole moves towards the east. With the current reversed (south to north), it moves towards the west.
Shows
the current produces a magnetic field, and reversing the current reverses the direction of the field.

  1. The pattern round a straight wire (Activity 12.5)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Push a long straight thick copper wire vertically through the centre of a fixed rectangular cardboard. Connect it in series with a 12 V battery, a rheostat, an ammeter (0–5 A) and a plug key. Sprinkle iron filings evenly on the card, pass a current, and tap the card gently. Then place a compass at a point P on the card.
See
the filings form concentric circles with the wire at their centre (Fig 12.6). The compass needle lies along the circle through P. If the current is reversed, the needle turns round.
Shows
the magnetic field lines round a straight current-carrying wire are concentric circles centred on the wire, in a plane perpendicular to it. The north pole of the compass gives the direction of the field at P, and this direction reverses when the current reverses.

How strong is the field? The same activity, with the compass kept at a fixed point:

  • Increase the current (with the rheostat) → the needle is deflected more. So the field at a given point increases as the current increases.
  • Move the compass farther away (from P to a point Q farther out), with the same current → the deflection decreases. So the field decreases as the distance from the wire increases. That is why the circles drawn for the field get larger and farther apart as you move away from the wire.

  1. Directions on a flat page: ⊗\otimes⊗ and ⊙\odot⊙

A wire often runs straight into or out of the page. We draw it like the feathers or the tip of an arrow:

  • ⊗\otimes⊗ (a cross in a circle) — the current goes into the page, away from you (you see the tail of the arrow);
  • ⊙\odot⊙ (a dot in a circle) — the current comes out of the page, towards you (you see the tip of the arrow).

The same two symbols are used for a field pointing into or out of the page. Every answer should also be written in words — "into the page", "out of the page".

  1. The right-hand thumb rule

To find the direction of the field round a straight wire, use the right-hand thumb rule:

Imagine that you are holding the current-carrying straight conductor in your right hand with your thumb pointing in the direction of the current. Then your fingers wrap round the conductor in the direction of the magnetic field lines (Fig 12.7).

Two quick results follow from the rule:

  • current towards you (⊙\odot⊙) → the field lines go round anticlockwise as you see them;
  • current away from you (⊗\otimes⊗) → the field lines go round clockwise as you see them.

Your textbook mentions another name for it, Maxwell's corkscrew rule: picture a corkscrew being screwed in along the current — the way the handle turns is the way the field goes round. It is the same rule with a different picture.

Using the rule step by step: (1) point the thumb of your right hand along the current (conventional current); (2) curl your fingers round the wire; (3) at the point you want, the field points the way your fingertips move past that point; (4) write the answer in words.

Worked example 1

A wire through a card

Question. A long straight wire passes vertically through a horizontal sheet of cardboard. The current in the wire flows upward. Points P, Q, R and S on the card are close to the wire, to its east, north, west and south respectively. (a) What is the direction of the magnetic field at each point? (b) Seen from above, do the field lines go clockwise or anticlockwise?

Given: vertical wire, current upward; points to the east, north, west and south of it. Asked: field direction at each point; sense seen from above.

Concept used: right-hand thumb rule (this topic). Why it applies: we need the direction of the field round a straight current.

  1. 1

    Thumb → point your right thumb up, along the current, and curl your fingers round the wire.

  2. 2

    Seen from above → the current comes towards you, so your fingers curl anticlockwise.

  3. 3

    At P (east) → moving anticlockwise past the east side, the fingers point north.

  4. 4

    At Q (north) → they point west.

  5. 5

    At R (west) → they point south.

  6. 6

    At S (south) → they point east.

  7. Check: test your right hand on your textbook's Fig 12.7 first (thumb down along the current → seen from above, the fingers go round clockwise). Our current flows up — the opposite way — so the circles must be anticlockwise, and east → north → west → south is anticlockwise on a map with north at the top ✔.

  8. Answer (a) P: north; Q: west; R: south; S: east. (b) Anticlockwise, seen from above.

    Common trap: saying the field points away from the wire (east at P). The field goes round the wire, never straight out from it.

Worked example 2

A compass above a wire

Question. A straight wire runs north–south along a table. A compass is placed on top of the wire, so that its needle (pointing north) lies just above the wire. A current is passed from south to north in the wire. Which way does the north pole of the needle turn — towards the east or towards the west?

Given: wire along north–south; current towards the north; compass above the wire. Asked: the direction in which the needle's north pole turns.

Concept used: right-hand thumb rule (this topic). Why it applies: the needle turns towards the field that the current makes at its position.

  1. 1

    Thumb → right thumb pointing north, along the current.

  2. 2

    Fingers → they curl over the top of the wire and down the other side.

  3. 3

    Above the wire → your fingers pass over the top moving towards the east.

  4. 4

    Needle → its north pole turns towards the field direction, so it swings towards the east.

  5. Check: in your textbook's Activity 12.4 the compass is below the wire, and the same south-to-north current turns the needle towards the west. Above the wire the field points the opposite way, so east fits ✔.

  6. Answer The north pole of the needle turns towards the east.

    Common trap: copying the textbook's answer (west) without noticing that here the compass is above the wire, not below it.

Worked example 3

Current into the page

Question. A long straight wire carries a current into the page (⊗\otimes⊗). Find the direction of its magnetic field at four points close to it: (a) just above it (towards the top of the page), (b) just to its right, (c) just below it, (d) just to its left.

Given: current into the page. Asked: field directions at four points.

Concept used: right-hand thumb rule; ⊗\otimes⊗ means away from you (this topic).

  1. 1

    Sense → current away from you → field lines go round clockwise as you see the page.

  2. 2

    (a) Above the wire → moving clockwise across the top, the field points right.

  3. 3

    (b) To the right → it points down the page.

  4. 4

    (c) Below → it points left.

  5. 5

    (d) To the left → it points up the page.

  6. Check: test your right hand on your textbook's Fig 12.7 first (thumb down along the current → seen from above, the fingers go round clockwise). Current into the page is also current going away from you, so the circles must be clockwise — and right → down → left → up is clockwise ✔.

  7. Answer (a) right (b) down the page (c) left (d) up the page — clockwise circles.

    Common trap: using the left hand, which gives every direction the wrong way round.

Worked example 4

What changes the deflection?

Question. A compass is placed on a card 4 cm from a vertical current-carrying wire, and its needle is deflected. What happens to the deflection if (a) the current is made larger, (b) the compass is moved to 8 cm from the wire with the same current, (c) the current is reversed?

Given: compass at 4 cm; three changes. Asked: the effect on the deflection.

Concept used: the field of a straight wire increases with the current and decreases with distance; it reverses with the current (this topic).

  1. 1

    (a) Larger current → stronger field at the compass → larger deflection.

  2. 2

    (b) Farther away → weaker field → smaller deflection.

  3. 3

    (c) Reversed current → the field at the compass points the opposite way → the needle is deflected the opposite way.

  4. Check: these are the three results your textbook reports for Activity 12.5 and Activity 12.4 ✔.

  5. Answer (a) Increases. (b) Decreases. (c) The needle is deflected in the opposite direction.

    Common trap: (b) "it is the same field everywhere on the circle, so no change". The field is the same all round one circle, but weaker on a bigger circle.

A compass is kept at a fixed point near a straight wire. When the current in the wire is increased, the magnetic field at that point:

The field at a given point increases as the current increases.

The field lines go round the wire, not along it.

A straight wire carries a current out of the page (⊙\odot⊙). What is the direction of the magnetic field at a point just to the right of the wire?

Answer: Up the page — current towards you gives anticlockwise circles, and moving anticlockwise past the right-hand side means moving up.

If you said "down the page", you used clockwise circles — that is for current into the page.

Common mistakes!

  • Drawing the field of a straight wire as straight lines or as lines spreading out from it.

    The filings form concentric circles round the wire.

    Draw circles centred on the wire, in a plane perpendicular to it.

  • "The field is stronger farther from the wire."

    The deflection falls as the compass moves away.

    Field ↑ with current; field ↓ with distance; circles drawn farther apart outwards.

  • Using the left hand for the thumb rule.

    It gives every direction reversed.

    Right hand; thumb along the current; fingers give the field.

  • Pointing the thumb along the electron flow.

    The rule uses the conventional current.

    Electrons one way → current the opposite way → thumb along the current.

  • "The field does not depend on which way the current flows."

    Activity 12.4 shows the needle swinging the other way.

    Reverse the current → reverse the field.

Key points

  • A current in a wire produces a magnetic field — it deflects a nearby compass (Oersted; Activity 12.1). Reversing the current reverses the field (Activity 12.4).
  • Round a straight current-carrying wire the field lines are concentric circles centred on the wire (Activity 12.5).
  • The field at a point is stronger for a larger current and weaker farther from the wire.
  • Right-hand thumb rule: hold the wire in your right hand with the thumb along the current; the curled fingers give the direction of the field lines.
  • ⊗\otimes⊗ = into the page (away from you); ⊙\odot⊙ = out of the page (towards you). Current into the page → clockwise field lines; out of the page → anticlockwise.
