Topic 4 of 6

Force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe Activity 12.7 (Fig 12.12) — the force on a current in a field, its reversal with the current or the field, and the condition for the largest force (current perpendicular to the field).
  • State and use Fleming's left-hand rule (Fig 12.13) to find the force, the current or the field, including for beams of charged particles (current opposite to electron motion).
  • Name devices that use current-carrying conductors and magnetic fields, and explain which properties of a moving charge a magnetic field can change.

A current pushes a compass needle. Push and pull always come in pairs — so does the magnet push back on the wire? Hang a light rod between the poles of a strong magnet, switch on a current, and the rod jumps sideways. This push is what turns every electric fan and pump.

Quick reminder (Topic 2 and Chapter 11)

A current produces a magnetic field round it, which exerts a force on a magnet nearby. The current in every rule is the conventional current — opposite to the direction in which electrons move.

  1. The magnet pushes back (Activity 12.7)

The French scientist André-Marie Ampère reasoned that forces come in pairs: if a current pushes on a magnet, the magnet should push back on the wire with an equal and opposite force.

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Hang a small aluminium rod AB (about 5 cm long) horizontally from a stand by two connecting wires. Place a strong horse-shoe magnet so that the rod lies between its poles, with the north pole vertically below the rod and the south pole vertically above it — so the field is directed upwards. Connect the rod in series with a battery, a key and a rheostat, and pass a current from end B to end A. Then reverse the current. Then turn the magnet over (field downwards).
See
with the current from B to A, the rod is displaced (to the left in Fig 12.12). When the current is reversed, it is displaced the other way (to the right). With the poles interchanged (field downwards), the displacement reverses again.
Shows
a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experiences a force. Its direction depends on both the direction of the current and the direction of the field — reversing either one reverses the force.

When is the force largest? Experiments show that the displacement of the rod — and so the force — is largest when the current is at right angles to the magnetic field. In that case the force is perpendicular to both the current and the field.

  1. Fleming's left-hand rule

When the current and the field are perpendicular to each other, the direction of the force is given by Fleming's left-hand rule:

Stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of your left hand so that they are mutually perpendicular (Fig 12.13). If the first finger (forefinger) points in the direction of the magnetic field and the second finger (middle finger) in the direction of the current, then the thumb points in the direction of motion or the force acting on the conductor.

Memory hook (ours, not your textbook's): forefinger → field; second finger → current; thumb → motion.

Using the rule step by step:

  1. Hold up your left hand with the three fingers at right angles to each other.
  2. Turn your hand until the forefinger points along the field (from N to S between the poles of a magnet).
  3. Keeping that, turn your wrist until the middle finger points along the current.
  4. The thumb now gives the force. Say the answer in words (left, right, up the page, into the page …).

The rule also works the other way round: if you know the force and the current, find the hand position that fits them and read the field from the forefinger (or the current from the middle finger).

  1. Moving charges

A beam of charged particles moving through space is also a current:

  • positive particles (protons, alpha particles) → the current is in the direction they move;
  • electrons (negative) → the current is opposite to the direction they move.

So a magnetic field also pushes on a beam of charged particles moving across it. Always turn an electron beam's direction round before you use the rule.

The force on a moving charge is at right angles to its motion. So the field changes the direction in which the particle moves (and therefore its velocity and momentum), but it does not make it go faster or slower; its speed (and of course its mass) stay the same.

  1. Where is this used?

Current-carrying conductors and magnetic fields work together in the electric motor, the electric generator, loudspeakers, microphones and electrical measuring instruments. (How the motor and the generator work — and electromagnetic induction — are in the syllabus for school assessment only, not for the board exam; they are not taught in this chapter.)

Did you know?Not in board exam

Every current makes a magnetic field — even the tiny flows of ions in our nerves, whose fields are roughly a billion times weaker than the earth's. The heart and the brain make the most noticeable fields in the body, and magnetic fields are used in Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) to take pictures of the inside of the body that help doctors.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Which way is the push?

Question. A straight wire lies across the page and carries a current from right to left. A uniform magnetic field points out of the page (⊙\odot⊙). In which direction is the force on the wire?

Given: current towards the left; field out of the page; they are perpendicular. Asked: direction of the force.

Concept used: Fleming's left-hand rule (this topic). Why it applies: a current-carrying conductor lies across a magnetic field.

  1. 1

    Left hand, three fingers at right angles.

  2. 2

    Forefinger (field) → pointing out of the page, towards you.

  3. 3

    Middle finger (current) → turn your wrist until it points to the left.

  4. 4

    Thumb (force) → it now points up the page.

  5. Check: test your left hand on your textbook's Fig 12.13 first (forefinger up for the field, middle finger towards you for the current → the thumb must point to the left). Then reverse only the current (to the right): forefinger out, middle finger right, thumb down — the opposite of our answer, as reversing the current must give ✔.

  6. Answer The force on the wire is up the page (towards the top of the page).

    Common trap: using the right hand — it gives "down the page", exactly the wrong way.

Worked example 2

Find the field

Question. A horizontal wire runs west–east and carries a current towards the east. It is pushed vertically upwards by a magnetic field that is horizontal and at right angles to the wire. What is the direction of the field — north or south?

Given: current towards the east; force upwards; field horizontal and perpendicular to the wire. Asked: direction of the field.

Concept used: Fleming's left-hand rule, used to find the field (this topic).

  1. 1

    Middle finger (current) → point it to the east.

  2. 2

    Thumb (force) → keep the middle finger east and turn your hand until the thumb points up.

  3. 3

    Forefinger (field) → it now points to the north.

  4. Check: test your left hand on your textbook's Fig 12.13 first (forefinger up for the field, middle finger towards you for the current → the thumb must point to the left). Then try the other choice: with the forefinger to the south and the middle finger east, the thumb points down — not what happened ✔.

  5. Answer The magnetic field points towards the north.

    Common trap: taking the field along the force ("upwards"). The field, the current and the force are all at right angles to one another.

Worked example 3

A proton beam and an electron beam

Question. A beam of protons moves up the page into a region where the magnetic field points into the page (⊗\otimes⊗). (a) In which direction is the force on the protons? (b) A beam of electrons moving up the page enters the same field. Which way is it pushed?

Given: protons and then electrons moving up the page; field into the page. Asked: the force on each beam.

Concept used: a beam of charges is a current; current is opposite to electron motion; Fleming's left-hand rule (this topic).

  1. 1

    (a) Current for protons → positive charges, so the current is up the page.

  2. 2

    Forefinger into the page, middle finger up the page → the thumb points left.

  3. 3

    (b) Current for electrons → negative charges moving up, so the current is down the page.

  4. 4

    Forefinger into the page, middle finger down the page → the thumb points right.

  5. Check: test your left hand on your textbook's Fig 12.13 first (forefinger up for the field, middle finger towards you for the current → the thumb must point to the left). Then: the two beams carry opposite charges, so they must be pushed in opposite directions — left and right ✔.

  6. Answer (a) The protons are pushed to the left. (b) The electrons are pushed to the right.

    Common trap: (b) putting the middle finger along the electrons' motion. For electrons, the current is the opposite way.

Worked example 4

Change one thing at a time

Question. In Activity 12.7 the rod is pushed to the left. What happens to the push if (a) only the current is reversed, (b) both the current and the field are reversed together, (c) the current is made larger, (d) the magnet is turned so that the field runs along the length of the rod instead of across it?

Given: rod pushed left in the original set-up. Asked: the effect of four changes.

Concept used: the direction of the force depends on the current and the field; the force is largest when they are perpendicular (this topic).

  1. 1

    (a) One reversal → the force reverses: the rod is pushed to the right.

  2. 2

    (b) Two reversals → each reversal turns the force round, so two of them bring it back: still pushed to the left.

  3. 3

    (c) Larger current → a larger push in the same direction (the rod is displaced more).

  4. 4

    (d) Field along the rod → the current is no longer at right angles to the field, so the push is much smaller than before.

  5. Check: with your left hand: reverse the middle finger alone and the thumb reverses; reverse both forefinger and middle finger and the thumb comes back ✔.

  6. Answer (a) To the right. (b) Still to the left. (c) A larger displacement to the left. (d) The push becomes much smaller — it is largest only when the current is perpendicular to the field. (Beyond your textbook: when the wire lies exactly along the field there is no force at all.)

    Common trap: (b) "both reversed, so it reverses twice as much". Two reversals cancel each other.

Common mistakes!

  • Using the right hand for the force, or Fleming's rule for the field round a wire.

    Each rule has its own hand and its own job.

    Field round a current → right-hand thumb rule. Force on a current in a field → Fleming's left-hand rule.

  • Swapping the fingers (forefinger for the current).

    The rule fixes which finger stands for what.

    Forefinger → field, second finger → current, thumb → motion.

  • Putting the middle finger along an electron beam.

    The current is opposite to the electrons' motion.

    Turn the electron direction round first; protons and alpha particles: no change.

  • "The force is along the field" or "along the current".

    The force is at right angles to both.

    Check your answer is perpendicular to the current and the field.

  • "The force is largest when the wire is parallel to the field."

    Activity 12.7: largest when perpendicular.

    Perpendicular → largest; turning the wire towards the field → smaller.

Key points

  • A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force (Activity 12.7). Reversing the current or the field reverses the force.
  • The force is largest when the current is at right angles to the field, and then it is perpendicular to both.
  • Fleming's left-hand rule: thumb, forefinger and middle finger of the left hand mutually perpendicular — forefinger = field, middle finger = current, thumb = motion (force).
  • A beam of charges is a current: for positive charges the current is along the motion; for electrons it is opposite. The force changes the particle's direction (velocity, momentum), not its speed.
  • Uses: motors, generators, loudspeakers, microphones, measuring instruments. (Motor, generator and electromagnetic induction are for school assessment only — not in the board exam.)
