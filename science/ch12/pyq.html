CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section C, Q363 marks

Question:
A. State the Right-Hand Thumb Rule and describe how it helps determine the direction of magnetic field around a current-carrying conductor.
B. Explain how the magnetic field changes with distance from the conductor.

Given: a straight current-carrying conductor. Asked: the rule and its use; how the field changes with distance.

Concept used: right-hand thumb rule; the field of a straight wire (Topic 2). Why it applies: both parts are about the field round a straight current.

  1. 1

    A — the rule → imagine holding the current-carrying straight conductor in your right hand with the thumb pointing in the direction of the current; your fingers wrap round the conductor in the direction of the magnetic field lines.

  2. 2

    A — how it helps → point the thumb along the (conventional) current; the way the curled fingers go round tells you the direction of the circular field lines, and so the field direction at any point near the wire.

  3. 3

    B — distance → the field is strongest close to the wire and decreases as the distance from the wire increases (the field depends inversely on the distance). That is why the circles drawn for the field get farther apart outwards.

  4. Check: Activity 12.5 — the compass deflection is smaller at Q (farther) than at P ✔.

    Common trap: in A, stating only the rule. The question also asks how it gives the direction — say that the curled fingers show the direction of the field lines round the conductor.
A. Right-hand thumb rule: imagine holding the current-carrying straight conductor in the right hand with the thumb pointing in the direction of the current; the fingers then wrap round the conductor in the direction of the magnetic field lines. (1) So, with the thumb along the conventional current, the curled fingers give the direction of the field lines round the conductor. (1)
B. The magnetic field decreases as the distance from the conductor increases — it varies inversely with the distance (B∝IrB \propto \dfrac{I}{r}B∝rI​ in the marking scheme). (1)
3 marks

The marking scheme writes B∝IrB \propto \dfrac{I}{r}B∝rI​. This is a short way of writing two statements in your textbook: the field increases with the current and depends inversely on the distance. You will not be asked to calculate a field.

CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section C, Q38 — case-based4 marks

Question:
A school has installed an automatic electromagnetic door-locking system at the main entrance. The locking mechanism uses a solenoid controlled by a switch at the security desk. When electricity flows through the solenoid, it creates a magnetic field strong enough to pull an iron bolt, unlocking the door.
As soon as the switch is turned off, the magnetic field disappears, and a spring pushes the bolt back, locking the door.
[Figure described: a coil wound on a soft iron core inside a frame; a plunger above the coil with an arrow pointing up; leads from the coil to a switch; labels "Plunger", "Switch", "Electric current energizes a coil", "Soft iron core".]
A. Why is an iron core kept inside the solenoid in this locking mechanism?
B. How can we determine the magnetic poles in this solenoid?
Attempt either subpart (C) or (D).
C. If the current in the solenoid is increased from 1 A to 2 A, explain how the magnetic effect changes and its effect on unlocking the door.
D. If the source of the current is fixed, how can we increase the magnetic field in the solenoid?

Given: a solenoid with a soft iron core pulls an iron bolt when the switch is on. Asked: the job of the core; how to find the poles; the effect of doubling the current (or how to strengthen the field with a fixed source).

Concept used: electromagnet; poles of a solenoid; field and current; number of turns (Topic 3). Why it applies: the lock is an electromagnet.

  1. 1

    A → the soft iron core becomes magnetised by the field of the solenoid and makes the magnet much stronger (the scheme: it "concentrates and amplifies" the field), so the pull on the bolt is stronger. When the switch is off, the soft iron loses this magnetism and the spring can push the bolt back.

  2. 2

    B — by the rule → find the field inside with the right-hand thumb rule on one turn; the end where the field lines come out is the N pole. (The marking scheme gives the shortcut: curl the fingers of the right hand in the direction of the current; the thumb points to the north pole.)

  3. 3

    B — by testing → bring the known N pole of a bar magnet near one end of the solenoid: repulsion shows that end is N (like poles repel).

  4. 4

    C → the field of the solenoid depends directly on the current, so increasing it from 1 A to 2 A makes the field stronger (twice as strong). The pull on the bolt is stronger, so the door unlocks more effectively (more reliably).

  5. 5

    D → with a fixed source (fixed current): increase the number of turns of the coil, and use a soft iron core. (The marking scheme also accepts reducing the length of the coil — winding the same turns more closely; your textbook does not discuss this.)

  6. Check: each answer uses one fact from Topic 3: the core is magnetised; N is where the field lines come out; field ∝ current; field ∝ number of turns ✔.

    Common trap: in B, "the N pole is the end nearer the battery's + terminal". The poles depend on which way the current goes round the turns, not on which end the wire comes from.
A. The (soft) iron core inside the solenoid is magnetised and concentrates/strengthens the magnetic field, producing a stronger pull on the bolt. (1)
B. Using the right-hand thumb rule (curl the fingers of the right hand along the current; the thumb points to the north pole), or bring the known N pole of a bar magnet near one end — repulsion shows that end is the north pole. (1)
C. The magnetic field of the solenoid is directly proportional to the current; increasing the current from 1 A to 2 A increases (doubles) the field, so the pull on the bolt is stronger and the door unlocks more effectively. (2)
OR D. Increase the number of turns of the coil; insert a soft iron core (the scheme also accepts reducing the length of the coil) — any two. (2)
4 marks
CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section C, Q38 — for visually impaired students4 marks

Question:
A student places a small magnetic compass on a wooden table and aligns it so that the compass needle points naturally toward North–South. She then stretches a straight charging cable above the compass so that the wire is parallel to the compass needle. When the phone charger is switched on and current begins to flow through the wire, the compass needle deflects away from its North–South position.
A. What causes the compass needle to deflect when current flows through the wire?
B. What happens to the compass needle when the current in the wire is switched off?
C. Explain how the amount of current in the wire affects the magnitude of deflection of the compass needle?
OR
D. When the direction of current is reversed, the compass deflects in the opposite direction. Explain how the Right-Hand Thumb Rule accounts for this change.

Given: a north–south cable above a compass; the needle deflects when current flows. Asked: the cause; what happens when the current stops; the effect of more current (or why reversing the current reverses the deflection).

Concept used: a current produces a magnetic field; the field increases with the current; right-hand thumb rule (Topic 2). Why it applies: this is Activity 12.4 with a charging cable.

  1. 1

    A → the current in the wire produces a magnetic field round it; this field acts on the compass needle (a small magnet) and deflects it.

  2. 2

    B → no current, no field from the wire: the needle swings back to point north–south, along the earth's magnetic field.

  3. 3

    C → the field at a given point increases as the current increases, so a larger current gives a larger deflection.

  4. 4

    D → by the right-hand thumb rule, the curled fingers give the field direction. Reverse the current (thumb the other way) and the fingers curl the other way, so the field below the wire reverses and the needle is deflected the opposite way.

  5. Check: with the cable carrying current from south to north above the compass, the thumb rule gives a field towards the west below the wire; with the current north to south it gives east — the two deflections are opposite, as the question says ✔.

    Common trap: in B, "the needle stays where it was". The needle is a magnet in the earth's field; once the wire's field disappears it returns to north–south.
A. The current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field around it, which deflects the compass needle (a small magnet). (1)
B. The needle returns to align itself with the earth's magnetic field in the north–south direction. (1)
C. The deflection increases with the current, since the magnetic field of a current-carrying conductor is directly proportional to the current. (2)
OR D. When the current reverses, the thumb points the other way, so by the right-hand thumb rule the fingers (field) curl in the opposite direction; the needle aligns with the reversed field and deflects the opposite way. (2)
4 marks
CBSE SQP 2025-26, Section C, Q373 marks

Question:
Magnetic field lines are shown in the given diagram. A student makes a statement that the magnetic field at X is stronger than at Y.
[Figure described: a horizontal bar magnet, N at the left end and S at the right end; three field lines drawn above it only, each rising from near the N end, arching over the magnet and coming down near the S end, without arrows; a straight horizontal line runs along the magnet's axis, through it and out beyond both ends; point X at the left end where the lines leave the magnet close together; point Y at the top of the outermost arch, where the lines are farther apart.]
(i) Explain with reason if the student’s claim is correct.
(ii) Also redraw the diagram and mark the direction of magnetic field lines.

Given: field lines over a bar magnet; X near the N pole, Y at the top of the arch. Asked: whether the field at X is stronger than at Y; the diagram with arrows.

Concept used: closeness of field lines shows strength; field lines go from N to S outside the magnet (Topic 1).

  1. 1

    (i) Near X → X is at the pole, where the lines are crowded together.

  2. 2

    At Y → far from the magnet, the lines are spread out.

  3. 3

    So → the field at X is stronger than at Y: the student's claim is correct.

  4. 4

    (ii) Arrows → outside the magnet the lines go from N to S: on each arch the arrows point away from the N end, over the top, and down into the S end (left to right over the top).

  5. Check: put a compass at X in your drawing — its north pole points away from N, the same way as your arrow ✔.

    Common trap: (i) "Y is stronger because it is higher up / on the biggest line". Strength depends on how close the lines are, not on how big a line is.
(i) Yes, the claim is correct. The closeness of magnetic field lines shows the strength of the field (1); at X (near the pole) the lines are crowded, so the field at X is stronger than at Y (1)
(ii) Diagram redrawn with arrows on the field lines from the North pole to the South pole. (1) — 3 marks (split inferred)

(b) Determine whether the 10 A fuse is appropriate for this circuit or if it will blow.

("1500 Wand" is printed in the paper for "1500 W and".)

Given: heater 1500 W and fan 500 W in parallel on 220 V; fuse 10 A. Asked: why the fuse is in series and the appliances in parallel; total current; whether the fuse blows.

  2. 2

    Appliances in parallel → each is connected across live and neutral, so each gets the same p.d. (220 V), draws only the current it needs, and can be switched on or off independently with its own switch.
