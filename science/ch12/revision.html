The whole chapter on one page

Magnets and field lines — a compass needle is a small magnet; its north-seeking end is its N pole. Like poles repel, unlike poles attract. A magnetic field is the region round a magnet in which its force can be detected; it has direction and magnitude. Its direction at a point is the way a compass's north pole points there. Field lines: out of N and into S outside, S to N inside — closed curves; closer where the field is stronger; never cross (a compass cannot point two ways). A uniform field: equally spaced parallel lines.

Field of a straight current — a current produces a magnetic field (Oersted; Activity 12.1); reversing the current reverses the field (Activity 12.4). Round a straight wire the field lines are concentric circles (Activity 12.5). Field ↑ with current, ↓ with distance. Right-hand thumb rule: thumb along the current, fingers give the field. ⊗\otimes⊗ = into the page (clockwise circles), ⊙\odot⊙ = out of the page (anticlockwise).

Loop, solenoid, electromagnet — in a circular loop the lines are circles near the wire and nearly straight at the centre; all parts add the same way; nnn turns → nnn times the field. A solenoid behaves like a bar magnet (one end N, one end S); the field inside is uniform; reversing the current swaps the poles. N end = where the field lines come out. Electromagnet = soft iron core in a current-carrying coil; stronger with more current, more turns, a soft iron core. Steel + DC → permanent magnet.

Force on a current — a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field feels a force (Activity 12.7); reversing the current or the field reverses it; largest when the current ⟂ field, and then perpendicular to both. Fleming's left-hand rule: forefinger = field, middle finger = current, thumb = force (motion). Charged beams: positive → current along the motion; electrons → current opposite. The force changes the direction (velocity, momentum), not the speed. Uses: motors, generators, loudspeakers, microphones, measuring instruments.

DC and AC — DC flows in one direction (cells); AC reverses periodically. Indian supply: AC, 220 V, 50 Hz → 100 changes of direction per second (once every 1100\dfrac{1}{100}1001​ s). Advantage of AC: power can be transmitted over long distances without much loss of energy.

Domestic circuits — live (red; also called positive) and neutral (black; negative), 220 V between them; earth (green) to a metal plate deep in the earth. Main fuse → meter → main switch → line wires → 15 A circuit (geysers, air coolers) and 5 A circuit (bulbs, fans). Appliances in parallel, each with its own switch. Earthing: low-resistance path for leaked current; body stays at earth potential; no severe shock. Short circuit: live and neutral touch → current rises abruptly. Overloading: short circuit, voltage hike, too many appliances on one socket. Fuse (in series) melts and breaks the circuit; replace with the same rating. Safety measures: fuse and earthing.

Direction rules

  • Right-hand thumb rule (field round a current): right thumb along the current → curled fingers give the field lines.
  • Fleming's left-hand rule (force on a current in a field): forefinger → field, middle finger → current, thumb → force (motion), all mutually perpendicular.
  • Electron beam: current is opposite to the electrons' motion.
  • N end of a loop or solenoid: the end where the field lines inside come out.

Key definitions

(as in your textbook — learn the words in bold)

  • Magnetic field: the region surrounding a magnet, in which the force of the magnet can be detected.
  • Direction of the magnetic field: the direction in which a north pole of a compass needle moves (points) inside it.
  • Field line: the path along which a hypothetical free north pole would tend to move.
  • Right-hand thumb rule: imagine that you are holding a current-carrying straight conductor in your right hand such that the thumb points towards the direction of current. Then your fingers will wrap around the conductor in the direction of the field lines of the magnetic field.
  • Solenoid: a coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire wrapped closely in the shape of a cylinder.
  • Electromagnet: a magnet formed when a magnetic material like soft iron is placed inside a current-carrying solenoid; it consists of a core of soft iron wrapped around with a coil of insulated copper wire.
  • Fleming's left-hand rule: stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of your left hand such that they are mutually perpendicular. If the first finger points in the direction of the magnetic field and the second finger in the direction of the current, then the thumb will point in the direction of motion or the force acting on the conductor.
  • Short-circuiting: when the live wire and the neutral wire come into direct contact (damaged insulation or a fault in the appliance), the current in the circuit abruptly increases.
  • Overloading (explanation, not a textbook definition): too large a current in a circuit — your textbook gives its causes: short-circuiting, an accidental hike in the supply voltage, or connecting too many appliances to a single socket.
  • Earth wire: a wire with green insulation connected to a metal plate deep in the earth; it provides a low-resistance conducting path for leaked current so that the user does not get a severe shock.
  • Direct current: current that always flows in one direction. Alternating current: current that reverses its direction periodically. (NCERT Exemplar wording.)
