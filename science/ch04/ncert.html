Intext p.61, Q1Electron-dot structure of carbon dioxide2 marks

What would be the electron dot structure of carbon dioxide which has the formula COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​?

Given: COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ — one carbon, two oxygens. Asked: electron-dot structure.

Concept used: atoms of non-metals share electron pairs until each has an octet (Topic 2). Why it applies: carbon and oxygen are both non-metals.

  1. 1

    Carbon (2, 4) has 4 valence electrons and needs 4 more. Oxygen (2, 6) has 6 and needs 2 more.

  2. 2

    Carbon is in the middle, joined to both oxygens.

  3. 3

    Each oxygen needs 2 → it shares two pairs with carbon (a double bond). Carbon then shares 2 + 2 = 4 pairs, which meets its need of 4.

  4. 4

    Each oxygen keeps its other 4 electrons as two lone pairs.

    O∙∙∙∙∙×∙×C×∙×∙O∙∙∙∙\begin{smallmatrix}\textstyle \overset{\bullet\bullet}{\underset{\bullet\bullet}{\mathrm{O}}}&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\bullet\times\\\bullet\times\end{smallmatrix}&\textstyle \mathrm{C}&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\times\bullet\\\times\bullet\end{smallmatrix}&\textstyle \overset{\bullet\bullet}{\underset{\bullet\bullet}{\mathrm{O}}}\end{smallmatrix}∙∙O​∙∙​​∙×∙×​​C​×∙×∙​​∙∙O​∙∙​​Swipe to see the whole equation →

  5. Check: electrons drawn = 4 (C) + 6 + 6 (O) = 16 = total valence electrons ✔. Round C: 4 shared pairs = 8 ✔. Round each O: 2 shared pairs + 2 lone pairs = 8 ✔.

    Common trap: Drawing single bonds C–O. Then carbon has only 4 electrons round it and each oxygen only 7.

Intext p.68, Q2Why so many carbon compounds?2 marks

What are the two properties of carbon which lead to the huge number of carbon compounds we see around us?

Given: carbon. Asked: two properties behind the huge number of its compounds.

Concept used: catenation and tetravalency (Topic 3).

  1. 1

    Catenation: carbon atoms bond with other carbon atoms, forming long chains, branched chains and rings, with single, double or triple bonds. The C–C bond is strong, so such molecules are stable.

  2. 2

    Tetravalency: carbon has a valency of 4, so each carbon atom can bond with four other atoms — carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur, chlorine and others — giving a huge variety of compounds.

  3. Check: both properties appear together in every example — e.g. butane uses catenation (a C–C–C–C chain) and tetravalency (each C has 4 bonds) ✔.

  4. Answer Catenation and tetravalency.

    Common trap: Writing "carbon is very common". Carbon is actually scarce in the earth's crust (about 0.02%).

Exercise Q15How soap cleans5 marks

Explain the mechanism of the cleaning action of soaps.

Given: soap, water, oily dirt. Asked: the cleaning mechanism.

Concept used: the two-ended soap molecule and micelles (Topic 8).

  1. 1

    Most dirt is oily, and oil does not dissolve in water.

  2. 2

    A soap molecule (e.g. CX17HX35COOX−NaX+\ce{C17H35COO^-Na+}CX17​HX35​COOX−NaX+) has a long hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail and a hydrophilic ionic head.

  3. 3

    In soapy water, the tails dissolve in the oily dirt while the heads stay in the water.

  4. 4

    Rubbing or agitating breaks the dirt into small droplets; soap molecules surround each droplet with tails inside and heads outside — a micelle.

  5. 5

    The micelles form an emulsion in water; the outward-facing ionic heads keep them in the water, so the dirt is pulled off the cloth.

  6. 6

    Rinsing carries the micelles, with the dirt trapped inside, away — leaving the cloth clean.

    (Diagram: an oil droplet with soap molecules arranged around it, tails in the oil and ionic heads pointing outwards into the water — see the micelle diagram in Topic 8.)

  7. Check: without soap, oil and water separate (NCERT Activity 4.10, tube A); with soap, the oil stays dispersed long enough to be rinsed away (tube B) ✔.

  8. Answer Soap molecules' hydrophobic tails dissolve in oily dirt and their hydrophilic heads face the water; agitation forms micelles around the dirt, which stay suspended in water as an emulsion and are rinsed away.

    Common trap: Drawing the micelle with the heads inside. In water the heads must face outwards.
