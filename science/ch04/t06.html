Topic 6 of 8

Chemical properties of carbon compounds

About 55 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Write balanced equations for the combustion of carbon and its compounds, and explain a clean blue flame versus a yellow sooty flame.
  • Describe the oxidation of alcohols to carboxylic acids by alkaline potassium permanganate or acidified potassium dichromate, and explain what an oxidising agent is.
  • Describe addition of hydrogen to unsaturated hydrocarbons with a nickel or palladium catalyst (hydrogenation of vegetable oils), and give a test to tell saturated from unsaturated hydrocarbons.
  • Describe the substitution reaction of saturated hydrocarbons with chlorine in sunlight.

On a well-tuned gas stove the flame is blue and the bottom of the kadhai stays clean. When the burner's air holes get clogged, the flame turns yellow and the pan bottom turns black with soot. Vanaspati ghee is made from liquid vegetable oil and hydrogen. And in sunlight, methane and chlorine swap atoms in a flash. This topic covers the four kinds of reactions behind these: combustion, oxidation, addition and substitution.

Quick reminder (Chapter 1)

Oxidation is the gain of oxygen (or loss of hydrogen). A catalyst changes the rate of a reaction without itself being used up. Balancing: change only the coefficients, never a formula.

  1. Combustion

Every form of carbon burns in oxygen, producing carbon dioxide and giving out heat and light. Most carbon compounds burn too: they form carbon dioxide and water and give out a great deal of heat and light. Burning in oxygen is combustion — an oxidation reaction.

This large release of energy is why carbon and its compounds — wood, coal, LPG, petrol, kerosene, CNG — are used as fuels.

Teacher demonstration — burning carbon compounds (NCERT Activity 4.3)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Your teacher burns a little naphthalene, camphor and alcohol, one at a time, on a spatula, and holds a metal plate above each flame.
See
Naphthalene and camphor burn with a yellow flame and a lot of black smoke that leaves a sooty deposit on the plate; alcohol burns with an almost clean flame and little or no soot.
Shows
naphthalene, an unsaturated compound, burns with a sooty yellow flame (camphor, rich in carbon, does too), while alcohol burns cleanly. Your textbook states the general rule: saturated hydrocarbons generally give a clean flame; unsaturated compounds give a yellow flame with lots of black smoke (soot is unburnt carbon).
Note
Safety: done only by your teacher, in a well-ventilated room, away from anything that can catch fire; the alcohol bottle is kept closed and well away from the flame.

The air supply matters too (NCERT Activity 4.4). Open the air hole at the base of a Bunsen burner and the flame is blue; close it and the flame turns yellow and sooty. Limiting the air causes incomplete combustion — even of saturated hydrocarbons — which gives soot. A gas or kerosene stove has inlets for air so that an oxygen-rich mixture burns with a clean blue flame. Pans that turn black underneath are a sign that the air holes are clogged — some fuel is not burning fully and is wasted.

Coal and petroleum also contain some nitrogen and sulphur. When they burn, these form oxides of sulphur and nitrogen — serious air pollutants.

Did you know?Not in board exam
NCERT information box

A flame forms only when gases burn. Charcoal in an angithi often just glows red, while wood first burns with a flame because its volatile substances turn to vapour and burn. The yellow light of a candle flame comes from tiny particles of hot, glowing soot.

Did you know?Not in board exam
NCERT information box

Coal formed from the remains of ancient trees and plants crushed deep under the earth over millions of years. Petroleum and natural gas formed from tiny sea plants and animals buried in silt, broken down by bacteria under high pressure, and trapped in porous rock. That is why they are called fossil fuels.

  1. Oxidation of alcohols

Burning is a complete oxidation. Carbon compounds can also be oxidised in a controlled way: an alcohol can be converted into a carboxylic acid.

Here [O] stands for oxygen supplied by the permanganate. (Your textbook shows only the alcohol and the acid, with the reagents written over the arrow; the [O] form above is the balanced version.) Acidified potassium dichromate does the same job.

Substances that add oxygen to others are called oxidising agents. Alkaline potassium permanganate and acidified potassium dichromate are oxidising agents: they add oxygen to the alcohol, turning it into an acid.

  1. Addition reactions

With a catalyst such as nickel or palladium, unsaturated hydrocarbons take up hydrogen (they add hydrogen) and become saturated hydrocarbons. The two atoms of HX2\ce{H2}HX2​ add across the double bond:

This hydrogenation is used to make vanaspati ghee: vegetable oils, which have long unsaturated carbon chains, are heated with hydrogen over a nickel catalyst to give solid fats. Animal fats have saturated chains. Saturated fatty acids are said to be harmful to health, so oils with unsaturated fatty acids are the better choice for cooking.

Telling saturated from unsaturated hydrocarbons. Your textbook's test is the flame: burn a little of each — a saturated hydrocarbon burns with a clean (blue) flame, an unsaturated one with a yellow, sooty flame. Another common laboratory test, not described in your textbook, uses bromine water (orange-brown): an unsaturated compound decolourises it quickly, because bromine adds across the double or triple bond; a saturated compound leaves it orange-brown.

  1. Substitution reactions

Saturated hydrocarbons are fairly unreactive and do not react with most reagents. In sunlight, however, chlorine reacts with them very fast, replacing hydrogen atoms one by one:

CHX4+ClX2→sunlightCHX3Cl+HCl\ce{CH4 + Cl2 ->[\text{sunlight}] CH3Cl + HCl}CHX4​+ClX2​sunlight​CHX3​Cl+HClSwipe to see the whole equation →

This is a substitution reaction: one type of atom (chlorine) takes the place of another (hydrogen). The CHX3Cl\ce{CH3Cl}CHX3​Cl formed can lose more hydrogens to chlorine in the same way, and with longer alkanes a number of products usually form.

Worked example 1

Burning propane

Question. LPG contains propane, CX3HX8\ce{C3H8}CX3​HX8​. Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of propane.

Given: propane burns in plenty of oxygen. Asked: balanced equation.

Concept used: complete combustion of a hydrocarbon gives carbon dioxide and water; balance one element at a time. Why it applies: propane is a hydrocarbon burning completely.

  1. 1

    Skeletal equation: CX3HX8+OX2→COX2+HX2O\ce{C3H8 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}CX3​HX8​+OX2​​COX2​+HX2​O

  2. 2

    Carbon: left 3, right 1 → put 3 in front of COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​.

  3. 3

    Hydrogen: left 8, right 2 → put 4 in front of HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O.

  4. 4

    Oxygen: right now 3 × 2 + 4 × 1 = 10 atoms; left 2 per molecule → put 5 in front of OX2\ce{O2}OX2​.

    CX3HX8+5 OX2→3 COX2+4 HX2O+heat and light\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O} + \text{heat and light}CX3​HX8​+5OX2​​3COX2​+4HX2​O+heat and lightSwipe to see the whole equation →

  5. Check: C 3 | 3, H 8 | 8, O 10 | 6 + 4 = 10 ✔. Propane is saturated, so with enough air it burns with a clean blue flame — the flame of an LPG stove ✔.

  6. Answer CX3HX8+5 OX2→3 COX2+4 HX2O\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}CX3​HX8​+5OX2​​3COX2​+4HX2​O

    Common trap: Balancing oxygen first. Leave the element that appears in two products (oxygen) until last.

Oxidising propanol

Given: propanol + alkaline KMnOX4\ce{KMnO4}KMnOX4​, heat. Asked: product and equation; observation; role.

Concept used: oxidising agents convert an alcohol into the carboxylic acid with the same number of carbons. Why it applies: propanol is an alcohol and alkaline KMnOX4\ce{KMnO4}KMnOX4​ is an oxidising agent.

  4. 4

    (c)

    It adds oxygen to propanol → it is an oxidising agent.

Worked example 3

Addition or substitution?

Question. Write the equation and name the type of reaction: (a) propene with hydrogen in the presence of nickel; (b) ethane with chlorine in sunlight (first step only).

Given: two hydrocarbons with two reagents. Asked: equations; reaction types.

Concept used: unsaturated hydrocarbons undergo addition; saturated ones undergo substitution with chlorine in sunlight. Why it applies: propene has a C=C double bond; ethane has only single bonds.

  1. 1

    (a)

    Propene, CHX2=CH−CHX3\ce{CH2=CH-CH3}CHX2​=CH−CHX3​, is unsaturated → the two H atoms add across the double bond.

    CHX2=CH−CHX3+HX2→NiCHX3−CHX2−CHX3\ce{CH2=CH-CH3 + H2 ->[\text{Ni}] CH3-CH2-CH3}CHX2​=CH−CHX3​+HX2​Ni​CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX3​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    One product, propane (saturated) → addition (hydrogenation).

  3. 3

    (b)

    Ethane, CHX3−CHX3\ce{CH3-CH3}CHX3​−CHX3​, is saturated → in sunlight one H is replaced by Cl.

    CHX3−CHX3+ClX2→sunlightCHX3−CHX2Cl+HCl\ce{CH3-CH3 + Cl2 ->[\text{sunlight}] CH3-CH2Cl + HCl}CHX3​−CHX3​+ClX2​sunlight​CHX3​−CHX2​Cl+HClSwipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Two products, chloroethane and hydrogen chloride → substitution.

  5. Check: (a) C 3 | 3, H 6 + 2 = 8 | 8 ✔. (b) C 2 | 2, H 6 | 5 + 1 = 6, Cl 2 | 1 + 1 = 2 ✔. Addition gave one product, substitution two — as the comparison table says ✔.

  6. Answer (a) addition — propene + hydrogen → propane (b) substitution — ethane + chlorine → chloroethane + hydrogen chloride (equations above)

    Common trap: Leaving out the conditions. Write Ni (or Pd) for addition of hydrogen and sunlight for chlorination — they are marking points.

Quick check · 3 of 3Short answer2 marks

The bottom of a pressure cooker turns black every time it is used on a gas stove, and the flame looks yellow. What is wrong, and what should be done?

Answer: The fuel is not burning completely — there is not enough air, so incomplete combustion gives soot (carbon), which blackens the cooker and makes the flame yellow. The air holes of the burner should be cleaned so that enough air mixes with the gas and it burns with a clean blue flame.

Black soot = unburnt carbon = not enough oxygen. It also means fuel is being wasted.

Common mistakes!

  • "Methane undergoes addition with chlorine."

    Methane is saturated; it has no double bond to add across.

    Saturated → substitution (in sunlight).

  • Writing addition of hydrogen without the catalyst

    Without Ni or Pd the reaction does not proceed.

    Write Ni (or Pd) over the arrow.

  • Forgetting "sunlight" for chlorination

    The condition is part of the answer.

    Write sunlight over the arrow.

  • "Combustion and oxidation are unrelated."

    Combustion is oxidation — complete oxidation to COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ and water.

    Combustion = burning in oxygen; alcohol → acid = controlled oxidation.

  • "A yellow flame is hotter."

    Yellow comes from glowing soot — a sign of incomplete combustion.

    A blue flame is the clean, efficient one.

  • "KMnOX4\ce{KMnO4}KMnOX4​ is a catalyst."

    It is used up as it adds oxygen.

    It is an oxidising agent.

  • "Hydrogenation turns oils into healthier fats."

    It makes saturated fats, which are said to be less healthy.

    Oils with unsaturated fatty acids are the better choice for cooking.

Key points

  • Combustion: carbon compounds burn in oxygen → COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ + HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O + heat + light — the reason they are used as fuels.
  • Clean blue flame = plenty of air (complete combustion); yellow sooty flame = limited air or an unsaturated compound.
  • Oxidation: alcohol → carboxylic acid with alkaline KMnOX4\ce{KMnO4}KMnOX4​ or acidified KX2CrX2OX7\ce{K2Cr2O7}KX2​CrX2​OX7​ (oxidising agents: they add oxygen).
  • Addition: unsaturated + HX2\ce{H2}HX2​ → saturated, with a Ni or Pd catalyst; used to hydrogenate vegetable oils. Flame test (your textbook's test) tells saturated from unsaturated (bromine water — not in your textbook).
  • Substitution: saturated hydrocarbon + ClX2\ce{Cl2}ClX2​ in sunlight → chlorine replaces hydrogen; HCl also forms.
