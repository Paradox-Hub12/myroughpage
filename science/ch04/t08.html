Topic 8 of 8

Soaps and detergents

About 50 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the structure of a soap molecule — an ionic (hydrophilic) head and a long hydrocarbon (hydrophobic) tail.
  • Explain how micelles form and how soap removes oily dirt.
  • Explain scum formation in hard water, why detergents work in hard water, and how soap is used to test for hard water.

Rub a greasy paratha plate under the tap and the oil just smears around — oil and water do not mix. Add a little soap and the grease lifts off. Yet bathe with water from a hand-pump and the same soap hardly lathers, leaving a grey scum on the bucket. Why does soap clean at all — and why does it sometimes fail?

Quick reminder (Topic 7)

Soaps are sodium or potassium salts of long-chain carboxylic acids, made by saponification.

  1. A molecule with two ends

A typical soap is sodium stearate, CX17HX35COONa\ce{C17H35COONa}CX17​HX35​COONa. Its molecule has two very different ends:

  • a long hydrocarbon chain — the tail — which does not dissolve in water but mixes with oil and grease. It is hydrophobic ("water-fearing").
  • an ionic end, −COOX−NaX+\ce{-COO^-Na+}−COOX−NaX+ — the head — which interacts with water. It is hydrophilic ("water-loving").

Because soap is the salt of a weak acid and a strong base, a soap solution is slightly basic: it turns red litmus blue.

  1. Micelles and the cleaning action

Most dirt is oily, and oil does not dissolve in water. When soap is added to water, the soap molecules gather round each oily drop:

  • the hydrocarbon tails push into the oil droplet;
  • the ionic heads stay outside, facing the water.

This cluster of soap molecules around an oil droplet is called a micelle. Its ionic heads keep it in the water, so the oily dirt held inside is pulled away from the cloth. Rinsing with water then carries the micelles — and the dirt — away.

Cleaning action, step by step

  1. Soap dissolves in water.
  2. The hydrocarbon tails dissolve in the oily dirt on the cloth; the ionic heads remain in the water.
  3. Rubbing, scrubbing or beating the cloth breaks the oily dirt into tiny droplets, each surrounded by soap molecules — micelles.
  4. The micelles stay spread through the water as an emulsion.
  5. Rinsing washes the micelles, with the dirt inside them, away.

If soap is dissolved in an oily liquid such as kerosene instead of water, the arrangement turns inside out: the ionic heads cluster in the middle and the tails face outwards.

NCERT information box

At the surface of water, soap molecules line up with their ionic heads in the water and their tails sticking out into the air. Micelles do not join together, because their ionic surfaces repel each other, so they stay spread through the water — which is also why dirt in them rinses away easily. Micelles are big enough to scatter light, so a soap solution looks cloudy.

  1. Hard water and scum

Lab: soap in soft and hard water (NCERT Activity 4.11) — see the virtual lab below. With distilled water or rain water (soft water), soap gives plenty of foam. With water from a tube-well or hand-pump (hard water), it gives little foam and a white, curdy precipitate.

Hard water contains calcium and magnesium salts (hydrogencarbonates, sulphates or chlorides). The soap reacts with the calcium and magnesium ions to form an insoluble substance called scum. The soap that forms scum is wasted, so a larger amount of soap is needed before any lather forms. This is also why soap is a simple test for hard water: little lather and a curdy scum means the water is hard.

  1. Detergents

Lab: soap or detergent in hard water? (NCERT Activity 4.12) Take 10 mL of hard water in each of two test tubes. Add five drops of soap solution to one and five drops of detergent solution to the other, and shake both for the same time. The detergent gives plenty of foam; the soap forms a curdy solid.

Detergents are generally sodium salts of sulphonic acids or ammonium salts with chloride or bromide ions. Like soaps, they have a long hydrocarbon chain and a charged end, so they clean in the same way. But their charged ends do not form insoluble precipitates with the calcium and magnesium ions of hard water, so detergents remain effective in hard water. Detergents are used in shampoos and in products for cleaning clothes.

What you do: Take three test tubes. Put 10 mL of distilled water in tube 1, 10 mL of tap water in tube 2 and 10 mL of hard water in tube 3 (if hard water is not available, your teacher can make it by dissolving a little calcium chloride or magnesium sulphate in water). Add the same number of drops (say 5) of the same soap solution to each tube. Close each tube with your thumb and shake it the same number of times (say 10). Measure the height of the foam with a ruler.

What you observe (sample readings — your values will differ, but the order will not):

Tube Water Height of foam Scum
1 distilled (soft) 4.5 cm none
2 tap water 2.5 cm a little
3 hard water 0.5 cm white, curdy

What it shows: the softer the water, the more foam the same amount of soap gives — soap cleans best in soft water. In hard water, part of the soap is used up in forming scum.

For a fair test: same volume of water, same soap, same number of drops, same number of shakes, same-sized test tubes.

Scum from a well

Question. Water from a village well contains dissolved magnesium sulphate. When it is used with soap (sodium stearate, CX17HX35COONa\ce{C17H35COONa}CX17​HX35​COONa), a grey scum forms. Explain why, and write a balanced equation for the formation of the scum.

Given: hard water containing MgSOX4\ce{MgSO4}MgSOX4​; soap CX17HX35COONa\ce{C17H35COONa}CX17​HX35​COONa. Asked: explanation; equation.

Concept used: soap reacts with calcium and magnesium ions in hard water to form an insoluble compound (this topic); double displacement (Chapter 1). Why it applies: magnesium sulphate makes the water hard.

    The well water is hard: it contains MgX2+\ce{Mg^2+}MgX2+ ions.

Worked example 2

Reading a foam test

Question. A student tested four water samples, P, Q, R and S, by shaking 10 mL of each with 5 drops of the same soap solution, ten times each. Foam heights: P 0.5 cm with white curd; Q 4.0 cm, no curd; R 1.5 cm with a little curd; S 4.0 cm, no curd. (a) Arrange the samples from hardest to softest. (b) Which samples could be rain water? (c) Suggest a cleaning agent for washing clothes in water like P, and explain your choice.

Given: foam heights and scum for four samples. Asked: order of hardness; soft samples; cleaning agent.

Concept used: the harder the water, the less foam and the more scum the soap gives; detergents do not form scum. Why it applies: the test was fair (same soap, drops, volume and shaking).

  1. 1

    Less foam + more curd → harder water. Foam: P 0.5 < R 1.5 < Q = S 4.0.

  2. 2

    (a) Hardest to softest: P, R, then Q and S (equally soft).

  3. 3

    (b) Rain water is soft → Q and S.

  4. 4

    (c) For water like P use a detergent: its charged ends do not form insoluble precipitates with calcium and magnesium ions, so it lathers and cleans even in hard water. Soap would be wasted in forming scum.

  5. Check: the sample with the most scum (P) has the least foam, as expected if soap is being used up in forming scum ✔.

  6. Answer (a) P > R > Q = S (hardest first) (b) Q and S (c) a detergent — it does not form scum in hard water.

    Common trap: Ranking by "most foam = hardest". Foam needs free soap; hard water uses the soap up as scum.

Worked example 3

The greasy plate

Question. Explain, step by step, how soap removes oil from a greasy plate. Why does the oil not come back on to the plate during rinsing?

Given: an oily plate, soap and water. Asked: the cleaning mechanism.

Concept used: the two-ended soap molecule and micelle formation (this topic). Why it applies: oil does not dissolve in water but does mix with hydrocarbon chains.

  1. 1

    Soap molecules have a hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail and a hydrophilic ionic head.

  2. 2

    The tails dissolve in the oil; the heads stay in the water.

  3. 3

    Scrubbing breaks the oil into small droplets, each surrounded by soap molecules — micelles, with tails inside and heads outside.

  4. 4

    The ionic heads on the outside keep each micelle in the water (an emulsion), so the oil is lifted off the plate.

  5. 5

    On rinsing, the water carries the micelles away; the oil stays trapped inside them, so it does not settle back.

  6. Check: without soap (NCERT Activity 4.10, test tube A) oil and water separate at once; with soap they stay mixed — the emulsion step is what makes the difference ✔.

  7. Answer Soap tails dissolve in the oil, heads face the water; scrubbing forms micelles that hold the oil inside and stay suspended in water; rinsing carries them away.

    Common trap: Drawing or describing the micelle with the heads inside. In water, the heads must face the water — outside.

Common mistakes!

  • Micelle drawn with heads inside, tails outside

    In water the hydrophilic heads must face the water.

    Tails inside (in the oil), heads outside.

  • "Hydrophilic tail, hydrophobic head"

    The words are swapped.

    Tail = hydrocarbon = hydrophobic; head = ionic = hydrophilic.

  • "Scum forms because of sodium ions."

    Soap is already a sodium salt.

    Calcium and magnesium ions of hard water form scum.

  • "Detergents form scum in hard water."

    Their charged ends do not precipitate with CaX2+\ce{Ca^2+}CaX2+/MgX2+\ce{Mg^2+}MgX2+.

    Detergents lather in hard water.

  • "A detergent can show whether water is hard."

    A detergent lathers in both soft and hard water.

    Use soap: little foam + scum = hard water.

  • "Soap solution is acidic."

    It is the salt of a weak acid and a strong base.

    Soap solution is slightly basic — red litmus → blue.

  • Calling scum "foam"

    Foam is lather; scum is a solid precipitate.

    Scum = insoluble, white and curdy.

Key points

  • A soap molecule has a hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail and a hydrophilic ionic head (−COOX−NaX+\ce{-COO^-Na+}−COOX−NaX+).
  • In water, soap molecules form micelles around oily dirt — tails inside the oil, heads outside in the water — giving an emulsion that rinses away.
  • Hard water contains calcium and magnesium salts; their ions react with soap to form insoluble scum, wasting soap.
  • Detergents (sodium salts of sulphonic acids, or ammonium salts with chloride/bromide ions) have long chains too, but form no scum, so they work in hard water.
  • Soap test for hard water: little foam and a curdy scum. Soap cleans best in soft water.
