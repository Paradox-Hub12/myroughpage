Chemistry toolkit — electrons, valency and bonds

About 15–20 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Write the electron arrangement of elements 1–20 and the valencies of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, chlorine and sulphur.
  • Tell ionic bonding (transfer of electrons) from covalent bonding (sharing of electrons).
  • Count the bonds round each atom in a structure (C 4, N 3, O 2, H 1, Cl 1).
Why this lesson exists

Everything in this chapter — drawing molecules, naming them, predicting their reactions — rests on one habit: counting outer electrons and bonds. If you remember electron arrangements from Class 9 and Chapter 3, prove it below and skip ahead to Topic 2.

A lump of coal, the gas in your kitchen cylinder, the sugar in your chai and the plastic of your water bottle look nothing alike. Yet all of them are built around the same atom — carbon — joined to its partners in a way you have not studied yet: not by giving electrons away, but by sharing them. Before you can draw these molecules, you need two tools: counting electrons, and counting bonds.

  1. Electrons in shells

An atom has a tiny nucleus with electrons arranged around it in shells — K, L, M, N from the inside out. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the atomic number. For the first 20 elements, the shells fill in the order 2, 8, 8, and then the N shell takes the rest.

Element Z Arrangement Element Z Arrangement
Hydrogen H 1 1 Sodium Na 11 2, 8, 1
Helium He 2 2 Magnesium Mg 12 2, 8, 2
Lithium Li 3 2, 1 Aluminium Al 13 2, 8, 3
Beryllium Be 4 2, 2 Silicon Si 14 2, 8, 4
Boron B 5 2, 3 Phosphorus P 15 2, 8, 5
Carbon C 6 2, 4 Sulphur S 16 2, 8, 6
Nitrogen N 7 2, 5 Chlorine Cl 17 2, 8, 7
Oxygen O 8 2, 6 Argon Ar 18 2, 8, 8
Fluorine F 9 2, 7 Potassium K 19 2, 8, 8, 1
Neon Ne 10 2, 8 Calcium Ca 20 2, 8, 8, 2

The electrons in the outermost shell are the valence electrons. They decide how an atom reacts.

  1. Valency: how many electrons an atom needs

The noble gases — helium (2), neon (2, 8), argon (2, 8, 8) — have a full outer shell and hardly react. Other atoms combine so that they, too, end up with a full outer shell: eight electrons (an octet), or two for hydrogen, whose only shell is the K shell (a duplet).

The number of electrons an atom must gain, lose or share to reach a full shell is its valency. For the non-metals in this chapter:

Atom Arrangement Valence electrons Electrons still needed Valency
H 1 1 1 (to make 2) 1
C 2, 4 4 4 4
N 2, 5 5 3 3
O 2, 6 6 2 2
S 2, 8, 6 6 2 2
Cl 2, 8, 7 7 1 1

Notice that for O, S, N and Cl the valency is not the number of valence electrons — it is the number still needed to reach eight. Carbon is the special case where the two numbers are the same (4 and 4).

  1. Two ways to a full shell: transfer or share

Quick reminder (Chapter 3)

A metal such as sodium gives its outer electron to a non-metal such as chlorine. The ions NaX+\ce{Na+}NaX+ and ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX− attract each other — an ionic bond.

When two non-metals meet, neither will give up electrons: both want to gain. So they share pairs of electrons, and each shared electron counts for both atoms. One shared pair makes one covalent bond.

Ionic bond Covalent bond
How the full shell is reached electrons transferred electrons shared in pairs
Usually between a metal and a non-metal two non-metals
Particles formed ions molecules (no ions)

  1. Counting bonds in a structure

In a structural formula, each covalent bond is drawn as a line between two atoms. A double bond (two shared pairs) is drawn as two lines and counts as two bonds for each atom; a triple bond counts as three.

The bond check: in every correct structure, the lines meeting at each atom must add up to its valency —

C = 4 · N = 3 · O = 2 · S = 2 · H = 1 · Cl = 1 · Br = 1

Use this check every time you draw or read a structure in this chapter. It catches most mistakes before they cost you marks.

Worked example 1

Arrangement, valence electrons and valency

Question. For sulphur (Z = 16), nitrogen (Z = 7), chlorine (Z = 17) and carbon (Z = 6), write the electron arrangement, the number of valence electrons and the valency.

Given: four atomic numbers. Asked: arrangement, valence electrons and valency for each.

Concept used: fill shells 2, 8, 8; valency = electrons still needed to reach eight. Why it applies: all four are non-metals among elements 1–20.

  1. 1

    Sulphur

    16 electrons: 2 in K, 8 in L, 6 left for M → 2, 8, 6.

  2. 2

    Six valence electrons; 8 − 6 = 2 still needed → valency 2.

  3. 3

    Nitrogen

    7 electrons: 2 in K, 5 in L → 2, 5.

  4. 4

    Five valence electrons; 8 − 5 = 3 still needed → valency 3.

  5. 5

    Chlorine

    17 electrons: 2, 8, then 7 in M → 2, 8, 7.

  6. 6

    Seven valence electrons; 8 − 7 = 1 still needed → valency 1.

  7. 7

    Carbon

    6 electrons: 2 in K, 4 in L → 2, 4.

  8. 8

    Four valence electrons; 8 − 4 = 4 still needed → valency 4.

  9. Check: compare with familiar formulas — HX2S\ce{H2S}HX2​S (S joined to two H), NHX3\ce{NH3}NHX3​ (N to three H), HCl\ce{HCl}HCl (Cl to one H) and CHX4\ce{CH4}CHX4​ (C to four H) each use exactly these valencies ✔.

  10. Answer S 2, 8, 6 → valency 2 · N 2, 5 → 3 · Cl 2, 8, 7 → 1 · C 2, 4 → 4

    Common trap: Writing "the valency of sulphur is 6" because it has six valence electrons. Valency is the number of electrons it still needs — two.

Common mistakes!

  • "The valency of oxygen is 6."

    6 is the number of valence electrons.

    Valency = electrons still needed: 8 − 6 = 2.

  • Counting a double bond as one bond

    A double bond is two shared pairs.

    Count it as 2 at each atom it joins (a triple bond as 3).

  • Hydrogen with two bonds

    Hydrogen's shell holds only 2 electrons — one shared pair fills it.

    Hydrogen always has exactly one bond.

  • "Two non-metals form ions with each other."

    Both want to gain electrons; neither gives any away.

    Two non-metals share electrons → covalent bond.

  • Chlorine = 2, 8, 8

    That is the chloride ion, after gaining one electron.

    Chlorine atom = 2, 8, 7.

Key points

  • Shells fill 2, 8, 8, then the rest (elements 1–20); outer-shell electrons are valence electrons.
  • Atoms combine to reach a full outer shell: an octet (8), or a duplet (2) for hydrogen.
  • Valency = electrons an atom still needs: H 1, C 4, N 3, O 2, S 2, Cl 1.
  • Metal + non-metal → electrons transferred (ionic). Non-metal + non-metal → electrons shared (covalent).
  • Bond check: the lines at each atom must equal its valency; a double bond counts 2, a triple bond 3.
