Topic 5 of 8

Naming carbon compounds

About 55 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Name compounds with up to five carbon atoms in the syllabus families (alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, haloalkanes, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids) from their structures.
  • Draw the structure of a compound from its name.
  • Avoid common naming errors — counting carbons, choosing the ending, impossible names and isomers.

Hand sanitiser, nail-polish remover and vinegar contain ethanol, propanone and ethanoic acid. Each name is a tiny code: the first part tells you how many carbon atoms the molecule has, and the ending tells you its family. Once you can read the code, a name becomes a structure — and a structure becomes a name.

Quick reminder (Topic 4)

Functional groups: –Cl/–Br (haloalkane), –OH (alcohol), –CHO (aldehyde), –CO– (ketone), –COOH (carboxylic acid), C=C\ce{C=C}C=C (alkene), C≡C\ce{C#C}C≡C (alkyne).

  1. The two parts of a name

Every name in this chapter is built from:

  • a stem that counts the carbon atoms in the chain, and
  • a prefix or suffix that shows the functional group.
Carbon atoms 1 2 3 4 5 6
Stem meth- eth- prop- but- pent- hex-
Alkane methane ethane propane butane pentane hexane

  1. The naming routine

Step 1 — Count the carbon atoms in the chain and write the alkane name. Three carbons → propane. Count every carbon, including the carbon inside –CHO, –CO– and –COOH. (A Cl or Br atom is not a carbon and is not counted.)

Step 2 — Find the functional group and look up its prefix or suffix:

Family Group Prefix or suffix Four-carbon example
Haloalkane –Cl, –Br prefix chloro-, bromo- bromobutane
Alcohol –OH suffix -ol butanol
Aldehyde –CHO suffix -al butanal
Ketone –CO– suffix -one butanone
Carboxylic acid –COOH suffix -oic acid butanoic acid
Alkene C=C\ce{C=C}C=C -ene in place of -ane butene
Alkyne C≡C\ce{C#C}C≡C -yne in place of -ane butyne

Step 3 — Join the parts.

  • A prefix goes in front: chloro + propane = chloropropane.
  • A suffix that begins with a vowel (-ol, -al, -one, -oic acid) replaces the final e of the alkane name: propane − e + ol = propanol; propan + one = propanone.
  • For a double or triple bond, change -ane to -ene or -yne: propane → propene, propyne.

  1. Reading the code backwards: name → structure

To draw a structure from a name, run the routine in reverse:

  1. Read the stem and draw that many carbons in a chain.
  2. Read the ending and put the group in place — –CHO and –COOH on an end carbon (the group's carbon is one of the chain carbons), –CO– on a carbon inside the chain, C=C\ce{C=C}C=C or C≡C\ce{C#C}C≡C between two carbons, –OH, –Cl or –Br on a chain carbon.
  3. Add hydrogens so that every carbon has four bonds.
  4. Check by naming your drawing.

  1. Names that cannot exist

Some names break the rules of structure:

  • "Methanone" and "ethanone" are impossible. A ketone's C=O carbon must sit between two other carbons, so the smallest ketone has three carbons — propanone.
  • "Methene" and "methyne" are impossible. A double or triple bond joins two carbons, so alkenes and alkynes start at ethene and ethyne.

  1. Isomers need different names

Moving a functional group along the chain can give a different compound with the same molecular formula — a structural isomer. For example, a bromine atom can sit on an end carbon of propane or on the middle carbon. Your textbook calls both bromopropane, and that name is enough for your board exam. The full IUPAC name adds a position number to tell them apart:

Worked example 1

Name six compounds

Question. Name these compounds:
(a) CHX3−CHX2−CHX2−CHO\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-CHO}CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX2​−CHO (b) CHX3−CHX2−CHX2−CHX2−CHX2−OH\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-CH2-CH2-OH}CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX2​−CHX2​−CHX2​−OH (c) CHX3−CHX2−Cl\ce{CH3-CH2-Cl}CHX3​−CHX2​−Cl (d) HC≡C−CHX2−CHX3\ce{HC#C-CH2-CH3}HC≡C−CHX2​−CHX3​ (e) CHX3−CHX2−CHX2−COOH\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-COOH}CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX2​−COOH (f) CHX3−CO−CHX2−CHX2−CHX3\ce{CH3-CO-CH2-CH2-CH3}CHX3​−CO−CHX2​−CHX2​−CHX3​

Given: six condensed formulae. Asked: their names.

Concept used: the naming routine — count carbons, find the group, join. Why it applies: each compound has a straight chain and one functional group.

  1. 1

    (a)

    Carbons: 3 in the CHX3−CHX2−CHX2X−\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-}CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX2​X− part + 1 in –CHO = 4 → butane.

  2. 2

    –CHO → -al: butane − e + al = butanal.

  3. 3

    (b)

    Carbons: 5 → pentane. –OH → -ol → pentanol.

  4. 4

    (c)

    Carbons: 2 → ethane. –Cl → prefix chloro- → chloroethane.

  5. 5

    (d)

    Carbons: 4 → butane. C≡C\ce{C#C}C≡C → -yne → butyne.

  6. 6

    (e)

    Carbons: 3 + 1 in –COOH = 4 → butane. –COOH → -oic acid → butanoic acid.

  7. 7

    (f)

    Carbons: 1 + 1 (in –CO–) + 3 = 5 → pentane. –CO– → -one → pentanone.

  8. Check: turn two names back into formulae — butanal has four carbons with –CHO at the end, CHX3−CHX2−CHX2−CHO\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-CHO}CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX2​−CHO ✔; pentanone has five carbons with C=O inside the chain ✔. Molecular formulae: (a) CX4HX8O\ce{C4H8O}CX4​HX8​O, (e) CX4HX8OX2\ce{C4H8O2}CX4​HX8​OX2​, (f) CX5HX10O\ce{C5H10O}CX5​HX10​O.

  9. Answer (a) butanal (b) pentanol (c) chloroethane (d) butyne (e) butanoic acid (f) pentanone (full IUPAC names, beyond the syllabus: (b) pentan-1-ol, (d) but-1-yne, (f) pentan-2-one)

    Common trap: Calling (a) "propanal" by counting only the carbons written before –CHO. The C in –CHO is part of the chain.

Worked example 3

Possible or impossible?

Question. A student wrote these names in a test: methanone, ethanol, ethanone, methene, propyne. Which names describe real compounds? Explain the others.

Given: five names. Asked: which are possible.

Concept used: a ketone's C=O carbon needs carbon on both sides (at least 3 C); a double or triple bond needs at least 2 C. Why it applies: each name gives a carbon count and a family.

  1. 1

    Methanone — 1 carbon with –CO–: the C=O carbon has no carbon neighbours → impossible.

  2. 2

    Ethanol — 2 carbons, –OH → CHX3−CHX2−OH\ce{CH3-CH2-OH}CHX3​−CHX2​−OH → possible.

  3. 3

    Ethanone — 2 carbons with –CO–: the C=O carbon would have only one carbon neighbour; with an H on the other side it is an aldehyde (ethanal) → impossible as a ketone.

  4. 4

    Methene — 1 carbon cannot form a C=C double bond → impossible.

  5. 5

    Propyne — 3 carbons with C≡C\ce{C#C}C≡C → HC≡C−CHX3\ce{HC#C-CH3}HC≡C−CHX3​ → possible.

  6. Check: the smallest members of each family agree — the first ketone is propanone (3 C) and the first alkene is ethene (2 C) ✔.

  7. Answer Possible: ethanol, propyne. Impossible: methanone and ethanone (a ketone needs at least 3 carbons), methene (a double bond needs 2 carbons).

    Common trap: Making up a ketone name for every carbon count by analogy with alcohols. Check the structure the name describes.

The name of CHX3−CHX2−CHX2−OH\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-OH}CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX2​−OH is:

-al is for –CHO.

How many carbon atoms are there in a molecule of butanoic acid?

Forgets the carbon inside –COOH.
but- = 4, and the –COOH carbon is one of the four: CHX3−CHX2−CHX2−COOH\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-COOH}CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX2​−COOH.
Adds the –COOH carbon on top of a four-carbon chain.
Six carbons would be hexanoic acid.
Quick check · 3 of 3MCQ1 mark

Which of these names describes a compound that cannot exist?

Methanal, HCHO\ce{HCHO}HCHO, is a real aldehyde (formaldehyde).
Ethanoic acid is acetic acid.
A ketone needs its C=O carbon between two carbons — at least three carbons in all.
Propene, CHX2=CH−CHX3\ce{CH2=CH-CH3}CHX2​=CH−CHX3​, is a real alkene.

Common mistakes!

  • Calling CHX3COOH\ce{CH3COOH}CHX3​COOH "methanoic acid"

    The carbon in –COOH counts: there are 2 carbons.

    Count every carbon → ethanoic acid.

  • Mixing up -ol, -al and -one

    They are three different groups.

    -ol = –OH; -al = –CHO (end); -one = –CO– (inside).

  • "Methanone", "ethanone"

    A ketone needs at least 3 carbons.

    The first ketone is propanone.

  • "Propaneone", "ethaneol"

    Drop the final e before a suffix starting with a vowel.

    propanone, ethanol.

  • "Propanebromide" or "propanebromo"

    Halogens go in front as a prefix.

    bromopropane.

  • Naming CHX2=CH−CHX3\ce{CH2=CH-CH3}CHX2​=CH−CHX3​ "propane"

    The double bond changes -ane to -ene.

    propene.

  • Thinking Br on an end carbon and Br on the middle carbon of propane is the same compound

    They are structural isomers — different compounds.

    Both are "bromopropane" at this level; (beyond the syllabus: 1-bromopropane and 2-bromopropane).

Key points

  • Name = stem (number of carbons: meth, eth, prop, but, pent, hex) + prefix or suffix (functional group).
  • Count every carbon, including the one in –CHO, –CO– or –COOH.
  • Prefix: chloro-, bromo-. Suffixes: -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid (drop the final e of the alkane name). Unsaturation: -ene, -yne.
  • The smallest ketone is propanone; the smallest alkene and alkyne are ethene and ethyne.
  • Moving a group along the chain gives an isomer; your textbook's name may be the same, but the compounds are different.
