Topic 3 of 8

Versatile carbon — chains, branches, rings and isomers

About 50 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain catenation and tetravalency, and why carbon forms far more compounds than silicon.
  • Distinguish saturated hydrocarbons (single bonds only) from unsaturated ones (double or triple bonds).
  • Draw straight chains, branched chains and rings (cyclohexane; benzene as the textbook shows it).
  • Draw structural isomers — two for butane and three for pentane — and avoid counting the same structure twice.

Chemists know the formulae of millions of carbon compounds — more than the compounds of all the other elements put together. You are made of them; so are your food, your clothes and most of your medicines. Yet the earth's crust has only about 0.02% carbon, and the air only 0.03% carbon dioxide. What makes this one element so productive?

Quick reminder (Topic 2)

Carbon shares its four valence electrons, forming four covalent bonds. A shared pair is a single bond; two pairs a double bond; three pairs a triple bond.

  1. Carbon's two special properties

Catenation. Carbon atoms can bond to other carbon atoms, again and again, building large molecules. This ability is called catenation. The carbon atoms can form long chains, branched chains or rings, linked by single, double or triple bonds. No other element does this to the same extent. Silicon forms compounds with hydrogen that have chains of only about seven or eight atoms, and these are very reactive. The carbon–carbon bond is very strong and so stable, which is why long carbon chains survive.

Tetravalency. With a valency of four, a carbon atom can bond to four other atoms — of carbon, or of other elements such as hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur and chlorine. Changing these partners gives compounds with quite different properties.

Why are carbon's bonds so strong? The carbon atom is small, so its nucleus holds the shared pairs of electrons tightly. Bigger atoms, such as silicon, form much weaker bonds.

Put together, catenation and tetravalency allow an almost endless variety of molecules.

  1. Saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons

Compounds of only carbon and hydrogen are hydrocarbons. To draw one, first join the carbon atoms, then use hydrogen atoms to complete each carbon's four bonds.

  • Saturated compounds have only single bonds between their carbon atoms. Their valencies are all satisfied by single bonds, and they are normally not very reactive. Saturated hydrocarbons are called alkanes — for example ethane, CX2HX6\ce{C2H6}CX2​HX6​.
  • Unsaturated compounds have at least one double or triple bond between carbon atoms. They are more reactive than saturated ones. Hydrocarbons with a double bond are alkenes (ethene, CX2HX4\ce{C2H4}CX2​HX4​); those with a triple bond are alkynes (ethyne, CX2HX2\ce{C2H2}CX2​HX2​).

  1. Structural isomers

Butane and its branched partner have the same molecular formula, CX4HX10\ce{C4H10}CX4​HX10​, but different structures. Compounds like this are called structural isomers. They are different substances: the straight-chain one boils at about −1 °C and the branched one at about −12 °C.

The number of isomers grows quickly with the number of carbon atoms: butane has 2, pentane has 3 (you will draw them in the NCERT questions) and hexane has 5.

Is it really a new isomer? Turning, flipping or bending a drawing does not change the molecule. Two drawings show the same compound if they have the same longest continuous carbon chain with the same branches in the same places. A chain is still "straight" even if you draw it with a bend in it.

Did you know?Not in board exam
NCERT information box

Carbon compounds were once thought to need a "vital force" inside living things to form, so they were called organic compounds. In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler made urea, a compound found in urine, from ammonium cyanate in his laboratory, and the idea collapsed. Carbon compounds — except carbides, oxides of carbon, carbonates and hydrogencarbonates — are still studied as organic chemistry.

Worked example 3

Saturated or unsaturated?

Question. Sort these hydrocarbons into saturated and unsaturated: propane CHX3−CHX2−CHX3\ce{CH3-CH2-CH3}CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX3​; propene CHX2=CH−CHX3\ce{CH2=CH-CH3}CHX2​=CH−CHX3​; propyne HC≡C−CHX3\ce{HC#C-CH3}HC≡C−CHX3​; butane CHX3−CHX2−CHX2−CHX3\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-CH3}CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX2​−CHX3​; cyclohexane (a ring of six carbons with single bonds); benzene.

Given: six hydrocarbons. Asked: saturated or unsaturated.

Concept used: saturated = only single bonds between carbons; unsaturated = at least one double or triple bond. Why it applies: all six are hydrocarbons whose bonds we can read.

  1. 1

    Propane — all single bonds → saturated.

  2. 2

    Propene — one C=C → unsaturated.

  3. 3

    Propyne — one C≡C\ce{C#C}C≡C → unsaturated.

  4. 4

    Butane — all single bonds → saturated.

  5. 5

    Cyclohexane — a ring, but all single bonds → saturated.

  6. 6

    Benzene — a ring drawn with double bonds → unsaturated.

What are structural isomers? Why is a straight chain of four carbons drawn with a bend not a new isomer of butane?

Answer: Structural isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structures. A bent drawing still has the same longest continuous chain of four carbons with no branch, so it is the same molecule — only the drawing has changed.

Common mistakes!

  • Counting a bent or flipped chain as a new isomer

    The atoms are joined in the same order; only the drawing changed.

    Trace the longest continuous chain and the branch positions.

  • "Pentane has 4 (or 5) isomers."

    Some drawings are repeats.

    Pentane has exactly 3: straight chain, one branch, two branches on one carbon.

  • "Saturated means solid; unsaturated means liquid."

    The words describe bonds, not the state.

    Saturated = only C–C single bonds.

  • "All rings are unsaturated."

    Cyclohexane is a ring with only single bonds.

    Look at the bonds, not the shape.

  • Isomers with different numbers of H atoms

    Isomers must have the same molecular formula.

    Count C and H in every structure you draw.

  • "Carbon forms many compounds because it is the commonest element."

    Carbon is scarce in the crust and air.

    The reasons are catenation and tetravalency (strong bonds from a small atom).

Key points

  • Catenation: carbon bonds to carbon, forming chains, branches and rings. Tetravalency: carbon bonds to four other atoms. Small atom → strong, stable bonds.
  • Silicon also shows catenation, but only in short chains (7–8 atoms) of very reactive compounds.
  • Saturated = only single C–C bonds (alkanes, not very reactive). Unsaturated = double (alkenes) or triple (alkynes) C–C bonds, more reactive.
  • Carbon skeletons can be straight chains, branched chains or rings (cyclohexane CX6HX12\ce{C6H12}CX6​HX12​, benzene CX6HX6\ce{C6H6}CX6​HX6​).
  • Structural isomers: same molecular formula, different structures. Butane 2, pentane 3, hexane 5.
