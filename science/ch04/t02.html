Topic 2 of 8

Covalent bonding

About 50 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why carbon forms covalent bonds — it cannot easily gain or lose four electrons.
  • Draw electron-dot structures of H₂, Cl₂, O₂, N₂, CH₄, CO₂, H₂O and NH₃ (and the S₈ ring) showing single, double and triple bonds.
  • Describe and explain the properties of covalent compounds — low melting and boiling points, poor conductors of electricity.

Common salt melts only at about 800 °C. Methane — the gas in CNG and biogas — boils at about −162 °C, so it is a gas even on the coldest Himalayan night. Ethanol, in your hand sanitiser, evaporates off your palm in seconds. Why are carbon compounds so different from the ionic compounds of Chapter 3? The answer is the way their atoms hold together.

Quick reminder (Topic 1)

Carbon is 2, 4: four valence electrons, valency 4. Non-metals reach a full shell by sharing electrons.

  1. Why carbon shares

To reach the octet of the nearest noble gas, carbon would have to gain or lose four electrons. Neither is practical:

  • Gaining four would give a CX4−\ce{C^4-}CX4− ion: a nucleus with only 6 protons would have to hold on to 10 electrons — four extra ones. That is very difficult.
  • Losing four would give a CX4+\ce{C^4+}CX4+ ion: removing four electrons takes a very large amount of energy, leaving 6 protons holding just 2 electrons.

So carbon takes a third route: it shares its four valence electrons with other atoms. A shared pair of electrons "belongs" to the outer shells of both atoms, so both reach a noble-gas arrangement. A bond formed by sharing an electron pair between two atoms is a covalent bond. Many other non-metals — hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, chlorine — form molecules in the same way.

  1. Drawing electron-dot structures of molecules

An electron-dot structure shows each atom's symbol with only its valence electrons. In this chapter, one atom's electrons are drawn as dots (•) and its partner's as crosses (×), so you can see where each electron came from. In carbon compounds, carbon's electrons are crosses and the other atoms' electrons are dots. (Dots and crosses are the same kind of electron — the symbols only keep track of their owner.) A shared pair sits between the two atoms; pairs that belong to one atom only are lone pairs (non-bonding pairs).

How to draw one — six steps

  1. Write the electron arrangement of each atom.
  2. Count each atom's valence electrons.
  3. Work out how many electrons each atom still needs — that is the number of pairs it must share.
  4. Place the atoms: the atom that needs the most bonds goes in the middle; hydrogen always goes on the outside.
  5. Put shared pairs between atoms until each atom's need is met. Draw any electrons left over as lone pairs.
  6. Check: 8 electrons round every atom (2 round H), and the total number of dots and crosses equals the total number of valence electrons.

  1. Properties of covalent compounds

Inside a covalent molecule the bonds are strong, but the forces between separate molecules are weak. That one fact explains two properties.

Property What you see Why
Melting and boiling points Low compared with ionic compounds Only the weak forces between molecules have to be overcome to melt or boil them
Conduction of electricity Poor conductors Electrons are shared, not transferred, so no ions (charged particles) are formed

NCERT information box

Pure carbon itself comes in different forms called allotropes. In diamond, every carbon atom holds on to four neighbours, building a rigid three-dimensional network — the hardest natural substance. In graphite, each carbon is bonded to three others in flat sheets of hexagons stacked in layers, so it is slippery and conducts electricity. Fullerenes, such as CX60\ce{C60}CX60​ shaped like a football, were named after the architect Buckminster Fuller.

Worked example 1

Hydrogen chloride and bromine

Question. Draw the electron-dot structures of (a) hydrogen chloride, HCl\ce{HCl}HCl, and (b) bromine, BrX2\ce{Br2}BrX2​. Bromine has 7 valence electrons. Name the type of bond in each.

Given: H (1), Cl (2, 8, 7), Br (7 valence electrons). Asked: electron-dot structures; type of bond.

Concept used: the six-step method for covalent molecules. Why it applies: both are made only of non-metals, so the atoms share electrons.

  1. 1

    (a) Hydrogen chloride

    H has 1 valence electron and needs 1 more; Cl has 7 and needs 1 more.

  2. 2

    One shared pair meets both needs. Cl's other six electrons stay as three lone pairs.

    H∙×Cl××××××\begin{smallmatrix}\textstyle \mathrm{H}&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\bullet\times\end{smallmatrix}&\textstyle \overset{\times\times}{\underset{\times\times}{\mathrm{Cl}}}{}^{\times}_{\times}\end{smallmatrix}H​∙×​​××Cl​××​××​​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    (b) Bromine

    Each Br has 7 valence electrons and needs 1 more → one shared pair; each Br keeps three lone pairs.

    ∙∙Br∙∙∙∙∙×Br××××××\begin{smallmatrix}\textstyle {}^{\bullet}_{\bullet}\overset{\bullet\bullet}{\underset{\bullet\bullet}{\mathrm{Br}}}&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\bullet\times\end{smallmatrix}&\textstyle \overset{\times\times}{\underset{\times\times}{\mathrm{Br}}}{}^{\times}_{\times}\end{smallmatrix}∙∙​∙∙Br​∙∙​​∙×​​××Br​××​××​​Swipe to see the whole equation →

  4. Check: (a) electrons drawn 1 + 7 = 8 = valence electrons of H and Cl ✔; H has 2, Cl has 8 ✔. (b) electrons drawn 7 + 7 = 14 ✔; each Br has 6 + 2 = 8 ✔.

  5. Answer Both have one shared pair — a single covalent bond: H−Cl\ce{H-Cl}H−Cl and Br−Br\ce{Br-Br}Br−Br.

    Common trap: Drawing HX+\ce{H+}HX+ and ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX− in brackets. Hydrogen chloride gas is made of molecules: the electrons are shared, not transferred.

Worked example 2

Ethane and ethene

Question. Ethane is CX2HX6\ce{C2H6}CX2​HX6​ and ethene is CX2HX4\ce{C2H4}CX2​HX4​. Draw the electron-dot structure of each and say how many electrons are shared between the two carbon atoms.

Given: C (2, 4), H (1). Asked: electron-dot structures; electrons shared between the carbons.

Concept used: carbon must share four pairs; hydrogen one. Why it applies: both are compounds of carbon and hydrogen only.

  1. 1

    Ethane

    Join the two carbons with one shared pair. Each carbon now needs 3 more pairs.

  2. 2

    Each carbon shares one pair with each of three H atoms (6 H in all ✔).

    HH∙×∙×H∙×C××C×∙H×∙×∙HH\begin{smallmatrix}&&\textstyle \mathrm{H}&&\textstyle \mathrm{H}&&\\&&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\bullet\\\times\end{smallmatrix}&&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\bullet\\\times\end{smallmatrix}&&\\\textstyle \mathrm{H}&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\bullet\times\end{smallmatrix}&\textstyle \mathrm{C}&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\times\times\end{smallmatrix}&\textstyle \mathrm{C}&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\times\bullet\end{smallmatrix}&\textstyle \mathrm{H}\\&&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\times\\\bullet\end{smallmatrix}&&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\times\\\bullet\end{smallmatrix}&&\\&&\textstyle \mathrm{H}&&\textstyle \mathrm{H}&&\end{smallmatrix}H​∙×​​H∙×​C×∙​H​××​​H∙×​C×∙​H​×∙​​H​Swipe to see the whole equation →

    Ethene

    Join the two carbons with one shared pair; with only four H atoms, each carbon can share with just two H.

  4. 4

    Each carbon then still needs one more pair → the carbons share a second pair.

    HH∙×∙×C××××C×∙×∙HH\begin{smallmatrix}\textstyle \mathrm{H}&&\textstyle \mathrm{H}\\\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\bullet\\\times\end{smallmatrix}&&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\bullet\\\times\end{smallmatrix}\\\textstyle \mathrm{C}&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\times\times\\\times\times\end{smallmatrix}&\textstyle \mathrm{C}\\\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\times\\\bullet\end{smallmatrix}&&\textstyle \begin{smallmatrix}\times\\\bullet\end{smallmatrix}\\\textstyle \mathrm{H}&&\textstyle \mathrm{H}\end{smallmatrix}H∙×​C×∙​H​××××​​H∙×​C×∙​H​Swipe to see the whole equation →

  5. Check: count round each carbon — ethane: 2 (C–C) + 3 × 2 (C–H) = 8 ✔; ethene: 4 (C=C) + 2 × 2 (C–H) = 8 ✔. Total electrons drawn: ethane 2 × 4 + 6 × 1 = 14 ✔; ethene 2 × 4 + 4 × 1 = 12 ✔.

  6. Answer Ethane: 2 electrons shared between the carbons (single bond). Ethene: 4 electrons shared (double bond).

    Common trap: Giving ethene four H on each carbon "because carbon makes four bonds". Two of carbon's four bonds are already used by the double bond.

Worked example 3

Counting bonds and shared electrons

Question. How many covalent bonds are there in a molecule of propane, CX3HX8\ce{C3H8}CX3​HX8​, and how many electrons are shared in all? How many electrons are shared between the two carbon atoms of ethyne, CX2HX2\ce{C2H2}CX2​HX2​?

Given: propane CX3HX8\ce{C3H8}CX3​HX8​; ethyne CX2HX2\ce{C2H2}CX2​HX2​. Asked: bonds and shared electrons.

Concept used: each covalent bond is one shared pair = 2 electrons; carbon forms 4 bonds, hydrogen 1. Why it applies: both are hydrocarbons, held together only by covalent bonds.

  1. 1

    Propane

    Three carbons in a chain are joined by 2 C–C bonds.

  2. 2

    Each of the 8 hydrogens forms 1 C–H bond → 8 C–H bonds.

  3. 3

    Total bonds = 2 + 8 = 10.

  4. 4

    Shared electrons = 10 × 2 = 20.

  5. 5

    Ethyne

    Each carbon bonds to one H, using 1 of its 4 valencies; the other 3 are used between the carbons → a triple bond.

  6. 6

    Electrons shared between the carbons = 3 × 2 = 6.

  7. Check: propane by valencies — total "bond ends" = 3 × 4 (C) + 8 × 1 (H) = 20; each bond has 2 ends → 20 ÷ 2 = 10 bonds ✔.

  8. Answer Propane: 10 covalent bonds, 20 shared electrons. Ethyne: 6 electrons shared between the carbons.

    Common trap: Counting only the C–C bonds. Every C–H line is a covalent bond too.

Quick check · 1 of 3MCQ1 mark

How many electron pairs are shared between the two atoms in a nitrogen molecule, NX2\ce{N2}NX2​?

One pair would give each N only 6 electrons.
Two pairs (a double bond) is oxygen's bond; each N would have only 7.
Nitrogen (2, 5) needs three electrons, so each atom shares three → three pairs, a triple bond.
Six is the number of shared electrons, not pairs.
Quick check · 2 of 3MCQ1 mark

Which molecule contains a double bond?

Oxygen shares one pair with each H — two single bonds.
Three single N–H bonds.
Each O needs two electrons and shares two with the other → two shared pairs.
Each Cl needs only one → a single bond.
Quick check · 3 of 3Short answer2 marks

Chloroform melts at 209 K, but sodium chloride melts at 1074 K. Chloroform also does not conduct electricity. Explain both facts.

Answer: Chloroform is a covalent compound: the forces between its molecules are weak, so little energy is needed to melt it. Its electrons are shared, so there are no ions to carry a current. Sodium chloride is ionic, with strong attraction between its ions.

Two different reasons: low melting point → weak forces between molecules; no conduction → no charged particles.

Common mistakes!

  • OX2\ce{O2}OX2​ drawn with one shared pair

    Each oxygen would have only 7 electrons.

    Oxygen needs 2 → two shared pairs (double bond).

  • NX2\ce{N2}NX2​ drawn with two shared pairs

    Each nitrogen would have only 7.

    Nitrogen needs 3 → three shared pairs (triple bond).

  • Leaving out lone pairs (e.g. O in HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O with only its two shared pairs)

    The structure must show all valence electrons.

    Count to 8: O in water has 2 shared pairs and 2 lone pairs.

  • Drawing inner-shell electrons too

    Only valence electrons are shown.

    Cl: 7 dots, not 17.

  • Carbon with 3 or 5 shared pairs

    Carbon always shares exactly four pairs.

    Count C's pairs: always 4 (a double bond is 2 of them).

  • Brackets and charges on covalent molecules

    No electrons are transferred, so there are no ions.

    Brackets and charges are only for ionic compounds.

  • Adding extra dots to "make it work"

    Electrons cannot appear from nowhere.

    Total dots + crosses = total valence electrons.

Key points

  • Carbon cannot easily form CX4−\ce{C^4-}CX4− or CX4+\ce{C^4+}CX4+, so it shares its four valence electrons — covalent bonds.
  • A shared pair of electrons = one covalent bond; electron-dot structures show valence electrons only.
  • Single bond: 1 shared pair (HX2\ce{H2}HX2​, ClX2\ce{Cl2}ClX2​, HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O, NHX3\ce{NH3}NHX3​, CHX4\ce{CH4}CHX4​); double: 2 pairs (OX2\ce{O2}OX2​, COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​); triple: 3 pairs (NX2\ce{N2}NX2​).
  • Check every structure: 8 electrons round each atom (2 round H); total electrons = total valence electrons.
  • Covalent compounds: strong bonds inside molecules, weak forces between molecules → low melting and boiling points; no ions → poor conductors.
