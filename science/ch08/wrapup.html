Wrap-up

Same rules, every cross

About 25 minutes · 6 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain dominant and recessive traits with a checkerboard.
  • Work out ratios, numbers and percentages; work back from offspring to parents.
  • Write the four kinds of gametes and fill the 16-box checkerboard.
  • Explain independent inheritance and new combinations.
  • Decide whether data is enough to call a trait dominant.
  • Explain that the father's chromosome decides the sex of the child.

Every cross in this chapter follows the same few rules: each parent has two copies of each gene; each gamete gets one copy; gametes meet by chance; and a dominant copy hides a recessive one. Once you can run the cross routine, a pea plant, a guinea pig or a human family all work the same way. This page puts the chapter on four cards and then works through five mixed questions.

Card 1 — The cross routine

Step What you write Example: Tt × Tt
1 Parents each parent's two copies + how it looks Tt (tall) × Tt (tall)
2 Gametes one copy of each gene per gamete T, t and T, t
3 Checkerboard top × side, one copy from each TT, Tt, Tt, tt
4 Count boxes by how they look (and make-up if asked) 3 tall, 1 short
5 Ratio simplest form 3 : 1 (look); 1 : 2 : 1 (make-up)
6 Answer fraction → percentage or number short = 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​ = 25%

Card 4 — Set out a cross in the exam

Write the cross as a column of labelled lines — write every line; marking schemes look for the gametes, the checkerboard and the ratio:

Line Write
Parents RRyy (round, green) × rrYY (wrinkled, yellow)
Gametes Ry · rY
F1 RrYy (round, yellow)
F1 × F1 RrYy × RrYy
Gametes of F1 RY, Ry, rY, ry (from each parent)
F2 16-box checkerboard
Result 9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green

Draw the checkerboard with a ruler, gametes outside the boxes, and write how they look under the letters.

Five mixed worked examples

Mixed example 1

F1 or F2?

Question. A pure round-seeded pea plant is crossed with a pure wrinkled-seeded plant, and the F1 is self-pollinated. What percentage of seeds are expected to be wrinkled (a) in the F1 and (b) in the F2? (c) What percentage of the F2 seeds are expected to be Rr?

Given: RR × rr; F1 self-pollinated. Asked: % wrinkled in F1 and F2; % Rr in F2.

Concept used: the ratio card (Card 2). Why it applies: a one-trait cross over two generations.

  1. 1

    F1 → RR × rr → all Rr → all round → wrinkled = 0%.

  2. 2

    F2 → Rr × Rr → checkerboard RR, Rr, Rr, rr → wrinkled (rr) = 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​ × 100% = 25%.

  3. 3

    Rr in F2 → 2 boxes of 4 → 24\dfrac{2}{4}42​ × 100% = 50%.

  4. Check: F2 make-up RR 25% + Rr 50% + rr 25% = 100% ✔.

  5. Answer (a) 0% (b) 25% (c) 50%

    Common trap: giving 25% for the F1. The recessive trait cannot show in the F1 of two pure parents.

Mixed example 2

No in-between pods

Question. Meena crosses a pure green-pod pea plant with a pure yellow-pod pea plant. Green pod colour is dominant. She expects some "yellowish-green" pods in the F1 or the F2. Will she find any? Give a reason.

Given: pure green × pure yellow; green dominant. Asked: whether in-between pods appear.

Concept used: traits do not blend (Topic 2). Why it applies: Meena is expecting a mixture of the two parental traits.

  1. 1

    F1 → GG × gg → all Gg → one G is enough → all green.

  2. 2

    F2 → Gg × Gg → GG, Gg, Gg (green) and gg (yellow) → green : yellow = 3 : 1.

  3. 3

    Every plant has either at least one G (green) or two g (yellow) — there is no in-between make-up.

  4. Check: Mendel found no "medium-height" plants in the same kind of cross ✔.

  5. Answer No — 0% yellowish-green in both the F1 and the F2. One dominant copy gives the full dominant trait, so the traits do not blend.

    Common trap: thinking Gg plants look "half green". A Gg plant looks exactly like a GG plant.

Mixed example 3

Spot the errors in a cross

Question. A student wrote: "Parents: TT × tt. Gametes: TT and tt. F1: Tt, Tt in the ratio 3 : 1. F2 of Tt × Tt: 1 tall : 2 medium : 1 short." Find and correct three errors.

Given: a student's cross. Asked: three errors with corrections.

Concept used: the cross routine (Card 1). Why it applies: each step has a rule.

  1. 1

    Gametes → a gamete carries one copy → TT gives T, tt gives t (not "TT" and "tt").

  2. 2

    F1 → all Tt, all tall — there is no 3 : 1 in the F1.

  3. 3

    F2 → no medium plants: 3 tall : 1 short (how they look), or 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt (make-up).

  4. Check: each correction matches a row of the Topic 2 Common-mistakes table ✔.

  5. Answer Gametes T and t; F1 all Tt (tall), no ratio; F2 3 tall : 1 short (or 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt) — no medium plants.

    Common trap: "correcting" the F1 to Tt and tt. Every F1 plant gets T from one parent and t from the other.

Mixed example 4

Working back with two traits

Question. A pea plant with round, yellow seeds is crossed with a plant with wrinkled, green seeds (rryy). All the offspring have yellow seeds; about half have round seeds and half wrinkled. What is the make-up of the round, yellow parent?

Given: round yellow × rryy; offspring all yellow; round : wrinkled about 1 : 1. Asked: the round yellow parent's make-up.

Concept used: working backwards, one trait at a time (Topics 2 and 3). Why it applies: each trait can be read separately.

  1. 1

    rryy parent → gives only ry gametes.

  2. 2

    Colour → all offspring yellow → the parent never gave a y → YY.

  3. 3

    Shape → about half wrinkled → the parent gave r to half → Rr.

  4. 4

    Parent → RrYY; gametes RY and rY.

    Gametes RY rY
    ry RrYy rrYy
  5. 5

    Offspring: RrYy (round, yellow) : rrYy (wrinkled, yellow) = 1 : 1 ✔.

  6. Check: all offspring have a Y → all yellow; half have R → half round ✔.

  7. Answer The round, yellow parent is RrYY.

    Common trap: writing RRYY. With RR, all the offspring would be round.

Mixed example 5

Whose Y?

Question. Arjun is a boy. Did his Y chromosome come from his mother's father or his father's father? Explain.

Given: Arjun is XY. Asked: which grandfather his Y came from.

Concept used: sex determination (Topic 5). Why it applies: a Y passes only from a father to a son.

  1. 1

    Arjun's mother is XX → she gave him an X.

  2. 2

    So his Y came from his father.

  3. 3

    His father is a male, XY; his father's Y came from his father — Arjun's father's father.

  4. 4

    Arjun's mother's father could pass his Y only to a son, never to his daughter (Arjun's mother).

  5. Check: each step uses "mothers give X; fathers give X or Y" ✔.

  6. Answer From his father's father (through his father).

    Common trap: "From the mother's side." A mother has no Y to pass on.
