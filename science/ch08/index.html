Chapter 8 · Unit II

Heredity

5 topicsAbout 6 hoursBoard exam
Why this chapter matters

Why do you have your mother's smile but your grandfather's ears? Why do two tall pea plants sometimes give a short one? And why does a family with three daughters still have a 50 : 50 chance of a son next time? A scientist who grew peas at his monastery and counted his plants worked out the rules more than a century ago — and they still let you predict what the next generation will look like.

This chapter follows those rules. You will see how variations build up from one generation to the next; how Mendel's crosses show dominant and recessive traits, with the ratios 3 : 1 and 9 : 3 : 3 : 1; how genes on chromosomes control traits and make both parents count equally; how to tell whether data is enough to call a trait dominant; and how the sex of a child is decided. It belongs to Unit II, World of Living. In the two CBSE sample papers it supplies a 4-mark question on two pea experiments, asking for percentages in the F1 and F2 (2026-27), and an MCQ on black and white rabbits, an assertion–reason item on an enzyme and plant height, and a 3-mark question on round green × wrinkled yellow seeds (2025-26).

What you'll learn

  • Explain how variations accumulate, why sexual reproduction gives more of them, why variation helps a species survive, and which trait probably arose earlier.
  • Describe the tall × short cross; explain dominant and recessive traits with TT, Tt and tt; run the cross routine and turn ratios into percentages and numbers; work back from offspring to parents.
  • Write the four kinds of gametes, fill the 16-box checkerboard, get 9 : 3 : 3 : 1, and explain independent inheritance and new combinations.
  • Explain gene → enzyme → hormone → height; explain how chromosomes make both parents contribute equally; decide whether data is enough to call a trait dominant; plan an experiment to find it.
  • Describe other strategies (reptiles, snails); explain XX and XY; show why the father's chromosome decides the sex of the child.
  • Use the cross routine, the ratio card, the "Can we tell?" card and the checkerboard builder on mixed questions.

Most of the marks in this chapter come from four skills, and every topic practises them:

  1. Explain "why" — why variation helps survival, why both parents contribute equally, why each gamete carries one copy.
  2. Write a cross — parents → gametes → checkerboard → count → ratio → the answer asked (percentage or number).
  3. Reason from data — decide whether a trait is dominant, or work back from the offspring to the parents.
  4. Sex determination — XX and XY, drawn as a cross.

The spaced-review sets come back to you after 1 day, 1 week and 1 month. Don't skip them — remembering something after a gap is what makes it stick.
