Topic 1 of 5

Variation and heredity

About 30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain how variations accumulate over generations, and why sexual reproduction gives more of them (Fig 8.1).
  • Explain why variation helps a species survive, and reason about which trait arose earlier in an asexual population.
  • Describe inherited traits, using free and attached earlobes (Activity 8.1).

Walk past a field of sugarcane and every plant looks almost the same. Now look around your classroom: no two faces match, and yet everyone has two eyes, one nose and ten fingers. Why are children so like their parents, and yet never exact copies of them?

Quick reminder (Chapter 7)

When a cell reproduces, it copies its DNA. The copying is never perfectly accurate, so small changes — variations — creep in. In sexual reproduction, DNA from two parents is combined, and each gamete (germ-cell) carries half the usual number of chromosomes.

Did you know?Not in board exam

Your textbook's ideas on evolution are in a formative-only box of the 2026-27 syllabus, so they are not in the board exam and are left out of this chapter.

  1. Same design, small differences

Heredity is the passing of traits (characteristics) from parents to their offspring. The rules of heredity explain how traits are reliably passed on — which is why a child has all the basic features of a human being.

But inheritance passes on two things at once:

  • a common basic body design, and
  • small changes in that design.

So a child looks like its parents but not exactly like them, and a human population shows a great deal of variation.

  1. Variations pile up over generations (Fig 8.1)

Picture one organism at the top of a family tree. It produces two offspring with the same basic body design, each slightly different. Each of those two then gives rise to two more.

Now look at the bottom row of four:

  • each one carries the differences it inherited from its parent, and
  • each one has some new differences of its own.

So the four are all different from each other. Each new generation adds new variations on top of the old ones — variations accumulate.

One parent or two?

  • With asexual reproduction (one parent), one bacterium divides into two, and each of those divides again. The four bacteria are very similar; the only differences come from small errors in DNA copying. That is why a sugarcane field, grown from stem pieces, shows very little variation.
  • With sexual reproduction (two parents), DNA from two different individuals is mixed in every generation, so even greater diversity appears. That is why people, and other animals that reproduce sexually, show clear differences from one another.

  1. Not every variation has the same chance

Will every variation survive equally well? No. It depends on what the variation is and on the surroundings.

  • Example: in a heat wave, bacteria that can withstand heat survive better than the others.

The environment "selects" the variants that suit it. Variation does not help every single individual — but because a population carries many different variants, some are likely to survive a change. That is how variation promotes the survival of the species.

  1. Which variation is older? (asexual population)

In a population that reproduces asexually, a new variation starts in one individual. It is then passed on, copy after copy, to all of that individual's descendants. The longer ago it arose, the more generations it has had to be copied — so, other things being equal, it is likely to be found in more of the population.

So if one trait is found in a large share of the population and another in a small share, the trait in the larger share has probably arisen earlier. (The word "probably" matters: a trait that helps survival could also spread faster.)

  1. Inherited traits — your earlobes (Activity 8.1)

The earlobe is the lowest part of the ear. In some people it hangs free; in others it is attached closely to the side of the head (Fig 8.2). These are two variants of one trait found in human populations.

What you do: look at the earlobes of every student in your class; list who has free and who has attached earlobes; work out the percentage of each. Then find out the earlobe type of each student's parents.

What you observe: both kinds are found in the class, and the two percentages add up to 100%. You can then set each student's earlobe type beside the types of their two parents.

What it shows: earlobe type is an inherited trait. Comparing children with their parents lets you suggest a possible rule for how it is inherited. One class's data can suggest a rule, but cannot prove which form is dominant — you will see why in Topic 4.

Worked example 1

Two bacterial lines in a heat wave

Question. A lab keeps two lines of the same bacterium in a warm room. Line P has no heat-tolerant variants. Line Q has a few heat-tolerant variants. One day the room's cooling fails and the temperature rises sharply for a week. What is likely to happen to each line? What does this show about variation?

Given: two lines; only line Q contains heat-tolerant variants; a sudden heat wave. Asked: the likely outcome; what it shows.

Concept used: variations do not all have an equal chance of surviving (this topic). Why it applies: the surroundings have changed.

  1. 1

    Line P → no individual can withstand the heat → the whole line is likely to die out.

  2. 2

    Line Q → most bacteria die, but the heat-tolerant variants survive and divide → the line carries on.

  3. 3

    What it shows → a population with variation is more likely to survive a sudden change, because some variants may suit the new conditions.

  4. Check: this matches your textbook's point that bacteria able to withstand heat survive better in a heat wave ✔.

  5. Answer Line P will probably die out; line Q survives through its heat-tolerant variants. Variation helps the species survive changes.

    Common trap: "All the bacteria in line Q survive." Only the variants that suit the heat survive.

Worked example 2

Which trait is older?

Question. In a large culture of an asexually reproducing bacterium, trait P is found in 5% of the cells and trait Q in 45%. Which trait is likely to have arisen earlier? Give a reason.

Given: asexual population; P in 5%, Q in 45%. Asked: which arose earlier; reason.

Concept used: in asexual reproduction a variation is passed to all descendants of the cell it arose in (this topic). Why it applies: the longer a trait has existed, the more generations have copied it.

  1. 1

    A new trait starts in one cell.

  2. 2

    Every time that cell's descendants divide, the trait is copied into more cells.

  3. 3

    A trait that arose earlier has had more generations to spread.

  4. 4

    Q (45%) is found in far more cells than P (5%) → Q has probably been copied for more generations.

  5. Check: 45% is nine times 5%, so Q has spread much further — a sign of a longer history ✔.

  6. Answer Trait Q is likely to have arisen earlier, because a trait that arose earlier has had more generations of reproduction to spread through the population.

    Common trap: "Q must be the dominant trait." How common a trait is tells you nothing about dominance.

  2. 2

Common mistakes!

  • "Asexual offspring are exact copies."

    DNA copying is never perfectly accurate.

    They are very similar, with minor differences.

  • "Every variation helps an organism survive."

    Variations do not all have an equal chance; it depends on the surroundings.

    Some variants survive a change (heat-tolerant bacteria in a heat wave).

  • "The commoner trait must be the dominant one."

    How common a trait is says nothing about dominance.

    Commoner (in an asexual population) → probably older. Dominance needs crosses (Topic 2).

  • "A class survey proves which earlobe type is dominant."

    One set of data can only suggest a rule.

    Use the survey to suggest a rule; deciding dominance needs the right data (Topic 4).

Key points

  • Heredity passes on a common body design plus small changes; children resemble parents but are not identical to them.
  • Variations are inherited and new ones are added in every generation, so they accumulate (Fig 8.1).
  • Asexual reproduction → very similar offspring (only small DNA-copying errors); sexual reproduction → more diversity.
  • Variants do not all survive equally; in a heat wave, heat-tolerant bacteria survive better → variation helps the species survive.
  • In an asexual population, a trait found in more individuals has probably arisen earlier.
  • Free and attached earlobes are two variants of an inherited human trait (Activity 8.1).
