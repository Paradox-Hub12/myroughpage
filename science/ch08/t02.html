Topic 2 of 5

Mendel's experiments — one trait at a time

About 50 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe Mendel's cross of tall and short pea plants — F1 all tall, F2 three tall to one short.
  • Explain dominant and recessive traits using two copies of each gene (TT, Tt, tt) and a checkerboard.
  • Work out ratios, numbers and percentages from a cross, and work back from the offspring to the parents.

Two tall pea plants are grown from the same packet of seeds. When their seeds are sown, most of the new plants are tall — but here and there a short plant appears. Neither parent was short. Where did "short" come from, and why does it turn up in roughly one plant out of every four?

Quick reminder (Chapter 7)

Self-pollination: pollen reaches the stigma of the same flower or plant. Cross-pollination: pollen is carried to a flower of a different plant. A pea flower normally pollinates itself, but a gardener can also cross two chosen plants by hand.

Quick reminder (Maths)

A ratio 3 : 1 means 3 parts and 1 part — 4 parts in all. So the first group is 34\dfrac{3}{4}43​ of the total and the second is 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​. To get a percentage, multiply the fraction by 100%; to get a number, multiply the fraction by the total.

  1. Mendel — the man who counted

Gregor Johann Mendel (1822–1884) studied science and mathematics, then went back to his monastery and grew garden peas. Many people before him had crossed plants. Mendel's new step was to count how many offspring showed each trait in every generation, and to look for a pattern in the numbers. The patterns led him to the laws of inheritance.

He chose pea traits that come in two clearly contrasting forms:

  • seed shape — round or wrinkled;
  • plant height — tall or short;
  • flower colour — violet or white.

  1. First cross: tall × short → the F1

Mendel crossed a tall pea plant with a short one and grew the seeds. The first generation of offspring is called the F1 generation.

Result: every F1 plant was tall. There were no "medium-height" plants — nothing halfway between the parents. Only one of the two parental traits showed; the two had not mixed.

  1. Second step: let the F1 pollinate itself → the F2

Were the tall F1 plants exactly like the tall parent? To test this, the tall parent plants and the F1 plants were each allowed to self-pollinate.

  • The tall parents' offspring: all tall.
  • The F1 plants' offspring — the F2 generation — were not all tall: one quarter of them were short.

So the F1 plants must have carried the "short" trait hidden inside them, even though they looked tall. Both traits were inherited by the F1; only tallness was expressed (shown).

  1. Mendel's explanation — two copies of each gene

Mendel proposed that a sexually reproducing organism has two copies of the factor that controls a trait. Today we call these factors genes. We write them with letters:

  • T = the copy for tallness, t = the copy for shortness.
Plant Its two copies How it looks
Pure tall parent TT tall
Pure short parent tt short
F1 plant Tt — one copy from each parent tall

A plant with TT or Tt is tall; only a plant with tt is short. A single T gives a tall plant; the plant is short only when both copies are t.

  • A trait that shows even when only one copy is present (like T) is called a dominant trait.
  • A trait that shows only when both copies are of that kind (like t) is called a recessive trait.

Gametes carry one copy. When a plant makes germ-cells, each gamete gets only one copy of each gene. A TT plant makes gametes that all carry T; a tt plant makes gametes with t; a Tt plant makes two kinds of gametes — half with T and half with t.

  1. The cross routine — use it for every cross

Every cross question can be solved with the same six steps (see the cross routine card below).

  1. Parents — write each parent's two copies and how it looks.
  2. Gametes — write the gametes: one copy of each gene per gamete.
  3. Checkerboard — one parent's gametes along the top, the other's down the side; each box gets one copy from the top and one from the side.
  4. Count — count the boxes by how they look, and by genetic make-up if asked.
  5. Ratio — write the ratio in its simplest form.
  6. Answer the question asked — turn the ratio into a fraction, a percentage or a number from a total.

The F1 × F1 cross (Tt × Tt), step by step:

  1. Parents: Tt (tall) × Tt (tall).
  2. Gametes: each parent gives T or t.
  3. Checkerboard:
Gametes T t
T TT (tall) Tt (tall)
t Tt (tall) tt (short)
  1. Count: tall boxes — TT, Tt, Tt (three); short boxes — tt (one).
  2. Ratios:
    - how they look: tall : short = 3 : 1;
    - genetic make-up: TT : Tt : tt = 1 : 2 : 1.
  3. Answer the question asked — e.g. short = 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​ = 25%.

So there are two different ratios for the same F2, and you must use the one the question asks for. The 3 : 1 is about how the plants look; the 1 : 2 : 1 is about their genetic make-up.

  1. From a ratio to a percentage or a number

For an F2 with tall : short = 3 : 1 (4 parts):

In the F2 Tall Short
Fraction 34\dfrac{3}{4}43​ 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​
Percentage 75% 25%
Number out of 800 F2 plants 600 200

Working for the last row: 34×800=600\dfrac{3}{4} \times 800 = 60043​×800=600 tall and 14×800=200\dfrac{1}{4} \times 800 = 20041​×800=200 short. Check: 600 + 200 = 800 ✔.

  1. Another trait, the same pattern — flower colour (Fig 8.4)

Your textbook's Fig 8.4 shows a plant with violet flowers crossed with a plant with white flowers. All the F1 plants have violet flowers. When the F1 self-pollinates, the F2 has violet and white flowers in the ratio 3 : 1. White disappears in the F1 and comes back in the F2, so violet is dominant and white is recessive.

  1. Checking the 1 : 2 : 1 (Activity 8.2)

All the tall F2 plants look the same, so how could we show that the F2 really is 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt?

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
let each tall F2 plant self-pollinate, and grow its seeds separately.
See
some tall plants give only tall offspring; others give tall and short offspring in a ratio of about 3 : 1. All short plants give only short offspring.
Shows
the plants giving only tall offspring are TT; those giving some short ones are Tt. There should be about twice as many of the Tt kind as of the TT kind. Together with the short (tt) plants, that confirms 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.

  1. Working backwards — from offspring to parents

Sometimes you are told the offspring and asked about a parent. Use these three patterns (each comes from a small checkerboard):

Cross Offspring (how they look)
TT × tt all tall
Tt × Tt tall : short = 3 : 1
Tt × tt tall : short = 1 : 1 (about half short)

So if a tall plant crossed with a short plant gives about half short offspring, the tall parent must be Tt.

Did you know?Not in board exam

Words you may meet in other books — not needed for the exam. Other books call the genetic make-up (such as TT or Tt) the genotype, and how the plant looks (tall or short) the phenotype. Each version of a gene (such as T or t) is called an allele, and the checkerboard is called a Punnett square. Your textbook does not use these words; in the exam, use your textbook's words: genes, traits, dominant, recessive, genetic make-up.

Memory hook

"3 : 1 is what you see; 1 : 2 : 1 is what the genes are."

Worked example 1

Green pods and yellow pods

Question. A pure-breeding pea plant with green pods is crossed with a pure-breeding plant with yellow pods. All the F1 plants have green pods. The F1 plants are allowed to self-pollinate and 960 F2 plants are grown. (a) Which pod colour is dominant? (b) Write the genetic make-up of the parents and the F1, using G and g. (c) How many F2 plants are expected to have yellow pods?

Given: pure green × pure yellow; F1 all green; 960 F2 plants. Asked: the dominant colour; the make-ups; the number of yellow-pod plants.

Concept used: the cross routine; dominant and recessive (this topic). Why it applies: this is a cross of two pure lines for one trait.

  1. 1

    Dominant → only green shows in the F1, so green is dominant and yellow is recessive.

  2. 2

    Parents → pure green = GG; pure yellow = gg.

  3. 3

    Gametes → GG gives G; gg gives g.

  4. 4

    F1 → every F1 plant gets G and g → Gg (green pods).

  5. 5

    F1 × F1 → Gg × Gg; gametes G, g from each parent.

    Gametes G g
    G GG (green) Gg (green)
    g Gg (green) gg (yellow)
  6. 6

    Count → green : yellow = 3 : 1, so yellow is 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​ of the F2.

  7. 7

    Number → 14×960=240\dfrac{1}{4} \times 960 = 24041​×960=240 yellow-pod plants.

  8. Check: green = 34×960=720\dfrac{3}{4} \times 960 = 72043​×960=720; 720 + 240 = 960 ✔.

  9. Answer (a) Green (b) parents GG and gg; F1 Gg (c) 240 plants with yellow pods.

    Common trap: writing the F1 gametes as "Gg". Each gamete carries one copy: G or g.

Worked example 2

How many of the axial plants are pure?

Question. In peas, flowers can grow along the stem (axial) or only at the tip (terminal). A pure axial plant crossed with a pure terminal plant gives F1 plants that are all axial. 400 F2 plants are grown from the F1. Using A and a: (a) how many F2 plants are expected to be AA, Aa and aa? (b) What percentage of all the F2 plants are pure axial (AA)? (c) Of the axial F2 plants only, what fraction are pure?

Given: F1 all axial → axial dominant; F1 is Aa; 400 F2 plants. Asked: numbers of each make-up; a percentage of all; a fraction of the axial ones.

Concept used: the genetic make-up ratio 1 : 2 : 1 (this topic). Why it applies: the F2 comes from Aa × Aa.

  1. 1

    Checkerboard for Aa × Aa → AA, Aa, Aa, aa → AA : Aa : aa = 1 : 2 : 1 (4 parts).

  2. 2

    (a) AA → 14×400=100\dfrac{1}{4} \times 400 = 10041​×400=100; Aa → 24×400=200\dfrac{2}{4} \times 400 = 20042​×400=200; aa → 14×400=100\dfrac{1}{4} \times 400 = 10041​×400=100.

  3. 3

    (b) AA as a percentage of all → 14×100%=25%\dfrac{1}{4} \times 100\% = 25\%41​×100%=25%.

  4. 4

    (c) Axial plants = AA + Aa = 100 + 200 = 300. Pure ones among them = 100. Fraction =100300=13= \dfrac{100}{300} = \dfrac{1}{3}=300100​=31​.

  5. Check: 100 + 200 + 100 = 400 ✔; terminal (aa) = 100 = 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​ of 400, matching 3 : 1 ✔.

  6. Answer (a) AA 100, Aa 200, aa 100 (b) 25% (c) 13\dfrac{1}{3}31​ of the axial plants are pure.

    Common trap: answering (c) with 14\dfrac{1}{4}41​. Read "of which plants?" — "of all the F2" and "of the axial ones" are different wholes.

Worked example 3

Working back from a half-and-half result

Question. A tall pea plant is crossed with a short pea plant. Of the offspring, 52 are tall and 49 are short. What is the genetic make-up of the tall parent? Show the cross.

Given: tall × short; 52 tall and 49 short offspring. Asked: the tall parent's make-up; the cross.

Concept used: working backwards (this topic). Why it applies: the offspring ratio tells us the parents.

  1. 1

    Short parent → short is recessive, so it must be tt; its gametes are all t.

  2. 2

    Ratio → 52 : 49 is very close to 1 : 1 — about half are short.

  3. 3

    If the tall parent were TT → every offspring would get a T → all tall. That does not fit.

  4. 4

    If the tall parent is Tt → gametes T and t:

    Gametes T t
    t Tt (tall) tt (short)
  5. 5

    Half the offspring are Tt (tall) and half are tt (short) → 1 : 1 ✔.

  6. Check: real counts are rarely exact; 52 and 49 are close enough to equal halves ✔.

  7. Answer The tall parent is Tt. Tt × tt → Tt (tall) : tt (short) = 1 : 1.

    Common trap: writing "TT" for every tall plant. A tall plant can be TT or Tt — the offspring tell you which.

A pure tall pea plant is crossed with a pure short pea plant. What will the F1 plants be like?

Every F1 plant is Tt, and one T is enough to make it tall.

Which gametes does a pea plant with the genetic make-up Tt produce?

A gamete carries one copy of each gene, never two.
Tt is the plant's own make-up; its gametes carry only one of the two copies.
Half the gametes carry T and half carry t.
The t copy also goes into half of the gametes.

Common mistakes!

  • "The F1 of tall × short is medium height."

    Traits do not blend; one T is enough for tallness.

    F1 = all tall (Tt). No halfway plants.

  • "The F1 shows a 3 : 1 ratio."

    3 : 1 appears only when the F1 self-pollinates.

    F1: all one kind. F2: 3 : 1.

  • "The F2 ratio is 1 : 2 : 1 tall : medium : short."

    1 : 2 : 1 is the ratio of genetic make-up (TT : Tt : tt).

    How they look: 3 tall : 1 short. Make-up: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.

  • "A Tt plant makes Tt gametes" or "gametes TT and tt".

    A gamete gets one copy of each gene.

    Tt → gametes T and t.

  • "25% of the F2 are Tt."

    Tt is 2 of the 4 boxes.

    Tt = 24\dfrac{2}{4}42​ = 50%; TT = 25%; tt = 25%.

  • "Every tall plant is TT."

    Tt plants are tall too.

    Tall = TT or Tt; only tt is short.

Key points

  • Tall × short → F1 all tall: no medium-height plants; the traits do not blend.
  • F1 self-pollinated → F2: 3 tall : 1 short; the hidden short trait reappears.
  • Each plant has two copies of each gene (TT, Tt or tt); each gamete has one copy.
  • A dominant trait shows with one copy (T); a recessive trait shows only with two copies (tt).
  • F2 ratios: 3 : 1 (how they look) and 1 : 2 : 1 (genetic make-up: TT : Tt : tt).
  • Cross routine: parents → gametes → checkerboard → count → ratio → answer the question asked.
  • Working back: tall × short → all tall means the tall parent is TT; about half short means it is Tt. Tall × tall → about 1 short in 4 means both are Tt.
