Intext p.129, Q1Which trait arose earlier?2 marks

If a trait A exists in 10% of a population of an asexually reproducing species and a trait B exists in 60% of the same population, which trait is likely to have arisen earlier?

Given: asexual population; trait A in 10%, trait B in 60%. Asked: which trait probably arose earlier.

Concept used: variations accumulate over generations (Topic 1). Why it applies: in asexual reproduction a new trait is copied into every descendant of the individual it arose in.

  1. 1

    A new trait starts in one individual → it arises from a small change in DNA copying in one organism.

  2. 2

    It spreads by copying → each time that organism's descendants reproduce, the trait is passed on to more individuals.

  3. 3

    Older trait, more copies → a trait that arose earlier has had more generations to spread through the population.

  4. 4

    Compare → B (60%) is found in many more individuals than A (10%) → B has probably been copied for more generations.

  5. Check: 60% is six times 10%, so B has spread much further ✔.

  6. Answer Trait B is likely to have arisen earlier, because in asexual reproduction a trait is copied into all the descendants of the individual it arose in, and an older trait has had more generations to spread.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • Trait B (found in 60%) is likely to have arisen earlier. (1)
    • In asexual reproduction a variation is passed on to all the offspring; the earlier it arose, the more generations it has spread through, so the larger the share of the population that has it. (1)
    Common trap: "B is dominant." Dominance has nothing to do with how common a trait is — and this is an asexual population.
Intext p.129, Q2How variation promotes survival2 marks

How does the creation of variations in a species promote survival?

Given: variation in a species. Asked: how it helps the species survive.

Concept used: variants do not all have an equal chance of surviving (Topic 1). Why it applies: the surroundings can change.

  1. 1

    Individuals differ → reproduction creates variations, so members of a species are not all alike.

  2. 2

    Surroundings change → for example, a heat wave.

  3. 3

    Some variants suit the change → bacteria that can withstand heat survive better in a heat wave; the others may die.

  4. 4

    Species survives → the surviving variants reproduce, so the species carries on. The environment "selects" the suitable variants.

  5. Check: without variation, a change that kills one individual would kill them all ✔.

  6. Answer Variations make individuals of a species different. When conditions change, some variants are better suited and survive (for example, heat-tolerant bacteria in a heat wave), so the species as a whole survives.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • Variations make some individuals better suited to changed conditions; these survive and reproduce, so the species does not die out. (1)
    • Example: in a heat wave, bacteria that can withstand heat survive better. (1)
    Common trap: "Variation helps every individual." Only the variants that suit the new conditions are helped.

Intext p.133, Q1Dominant and recessive traits3 marks

How do Mendel's experiments show that traits may be dominant or recessive?

Given: Mendel's cross of tall and short pea plants. Asked: how it shows dominant and recessive traits.

Concept used: the monohybrid cross and the cross routine (Topic 2). Why it applies: one trait (height) disappears and reappears.

  1. 1

    Cross → pure tall (TT) × pure short (tt).

  2. 2

    F1 → all plants are tall (Tt); no medium plants. Only the tall trait shows, although each F1 plant got a t from its short parent → tallness is dominant.

  3. 3

    F2 → the F1 plants self-pollinate. Tt × Tt gives:

    Gametes T t
    T TT (tall) Tt (tall)
    t Tt (tall) tt (short)

    → 3 tall : 1 short. The short trait, hidden in the F1, reappears in one quarter of the F2.

  4. 4

    Conclusion → shortness shows only when both copies are t → shortness is recessive. A trait that shows with one copy (T) is dominant.

  5. Check: F2 tall plants are TT or Tt; only tt is short — matches 3 : 1 ✔.

  6. Answer When pure tall and pure short plants are crossed, all the F1 plants are tall, so tallness is dominant. When the F1 self-pollinates, one quarter of the F2 plants are short: the short trait was hidden in the F1 and shows only when a plant has two copies of t, so shortness is recessive.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    • Pure tall (TT) × pure short (tt) → F1 all tall (Tt): only the tall trait is expressed → tall is dominant. (1)
    • F1 self-pollinated → F2 tall : short = 3 : 1 (TT, Tt, Tt, tt): the short trait reappears. (1)
    • The trait expressed with a single copy is dominant; the trait expressed only with two copies (tt) is recessive. (1)
    Common trap: stopping at the F1. The F2 is what shows the short trait was hidden, not lost.
Intext p.133, Q2Independent inheritance3 marks

How do Mendel's experiments show that traits are inherited independently?

Given: a cross of plants differing in two traits. Asked: how it shows independent inheritance.

Concept used: the two-trait cross (Topic 3). Why it applies: new combinations of traits appear in the F2.

  1. 1

    Cross → round, green seeds (RRyy) × wrinkled, yellow seeds (rrYY); gametes Ry and rY.

  2. 2

    F1 → all RrYy, round and yellow.

  3. 3

    F1 gametes → four kinds: RY, Ry, rY, ry — the shape copy and the colour copy go into gametes independently.

  4. 4

    F2 → 16-box checkerboard → 9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green.

  5. 5

    New combinations → round yellow and wrinkled green were not in the parents. Seed shape and seed colour did not stay together as they came in.

  6. 6

    Each trait alone → 3 : 1 for shape and for colour, and the colour ratio is the same 3 : 1 among round seeds (9 : 3) and among wrinkled seeds (3 : 1) → shape does not affect colour.

  7. Check: the same holds for your textbook's tall-round × short-wrinkled cross: the F2 includes tall-wrinkled and short-round plants ✔.

  8. Answer When plants differing in two traits are crossed (round green × wrinkled yellow) and the F1 is self-pollinated, the F2 shows not only the parental combinations but also new combinations (round yellow, wrinkled green) in the ratio 9 : 3 : 3 : 1, and the shape of a seed does not affect its colour. So the two traits are inherited independently of each other.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    • Round green (RRyy) × wrinkled yellow (rrYY) → F1 all round yellow (RrYy). (1)
    • F1 self-pollinated → F2: round yellow, round green, wrinkled yellow, wrinkled green in the ratio 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. (1)
    • New combinations appear, so seed shape and seed colour are inherited independently. (1)
    Common trap: describing only one trait. Independent inheritance needs two traits followed together.
Intext p.133, Q3Blood group A or O: can we tell?3 marks

A man with blood group A marries a woman with blood group O and their daughter has blood group O. Is this information enough to tell you which of the traits – blood group A or O – is dominant? Why or why not?

Given: father A, mother O, daughter O. Asked: is this enough to decide dominance; why.

Concept used: the "Can we tell?" card (Topic 4). Why it applies: this is one family where the child looks like one parent.

  1. 1

    Test rule 1 — A dominant → the father could carry one A copy and one hidden O copy. He passes O, the mother passes O → the daughter is O. ✔ This fits.

  2. 2

    Test rule 2 — O dominant → the daughter gets an O copy from her mother, and O (dominant) shows → the daughter is O. ✔ This also fits.

  3. 3

    Both rules fit → the data cannot tell them apart.

  4. 4

    What is needed → many more families and offspring, or crosses where one trait clearly disappears and reappears.

  5. Check: a child who looks like one parent is a "No" row of the card ✔.

  6. Answer No. The daughter's blood group O can be explained whether A is dominant (the father carries a hidden O and passes it on) or O is dominant (she gets O from her mother). One child from one family is too little data; we would need results from many families or crosses.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    • No, the information is not enough. (1)
    • If A is dominant, the father could carry a hidden O and pass it on; if O is dominant, the daughter gets O from her mother — both explain the daughter's blood group O. (1)
    • Data from a large number of offspring/families is needed to decide which trait is dominant. (1)
    Common trap: "O is dominant because the daughter has O like her mother." Looking like one parent does not show dominance.
Intext p.133, Q4Sex of the child3 marks

How is the sex of the child determined in human beings?

Given: human sex chromosomes. Asked: how the child's sex is decided.

Concept used: sex determination (Topic 5). Why it applies: in humans, sex is largely genetic.

  1. 1

    Sex chromosomes → besides 22 matching pairs, humans have one pair of sex chromosomes: women XX (a perfect pair), men XY (a mismatched pair; Y is short).

  2. 2

    Gametes → every egg carries X; sperms carry X or Y, in equal numbers.

  3. 3

    Fertilisation → egg (X) + sperm (X) → XX → girl; egg (X) + sperm (Y) → XY → boy.

    Gametes X (sperm) Y (sperm)
    X (egg) XX (girl) XY (boy)
  4. 4

    Decider → all children get X from the mother, so the father's chromosome (X or Y) decides the sex; half the children are expected to be girls and half boys.

  5. Check: matches Fig 8.6 — two kinds of sperm, one kind of egg ✔.

  6. Answer Women have XX and men have XY sex chromosomes. Every egg carries an X; half the sperms carry X and half carry Y. A child who inherits X from the father is a girl (XX); a child who inherits Y from the father is a boy (XY). So the sex of the child is decided by the chromosome from the father.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    • Women have a perfect pair of sex chromosomes, XX; men have a mismatched pair, XY. (1)
    • All eggs carry X; sperms carry X or Y. X-sperm + egg → XX (girl); Y-sperm + egg → XY (boy) — with a cross diagram. (1)
    • So the sex of the child depends on the chromosome inherited from the father. (1)
    Common trap: "The mother decides the sex." Every egg carries the same X; only the sperm differs.

Exercise Q1Genetic make-up of the tall parentMCQ1 mark

A Mendelian experiment consisted of breeding tall pea plants bearing violet flowers with short pea plants bearing white flowers. The progeny all bore violet flowers, but almost half of them were short. This suggests that the genetic make-up of the tall parent can be depicted as

Choose your answer first — the full solution opens after.

With TT, every offspring would get a T → all tall, not half short.
With ww, the parent would have white flowers, and all offspring would be white.
Tt → half the offspring get t from both parents → about half short; WW → every offspring gets a W → all violet.
With Ww, about half the offspring would get w from both parents → half white, not all violet.

Asked: the tall violet parent's make-up. (In this question your textbook uses W for the violet-flower copy and w for white.)

Concept used: working backwards, one trait at a time (Topics 2 and 3). The short white parent is ttww and gives only tw gametes.

  1. Reasoning for each option:

    • (a) ✘ With TT, every offspring would get a T → all tall, not half short.
    • (b) ✘ With ww, the parent would have white flowers, and all offspring would be white.
    • (c) ✔ Tt → half the offspring get t from both parents → about half short; WW → every offspring gets a W → all violet.
    • (d) ✘ With Ww, about half the offspring would get w from both parents → half white, not all violet.

  2. Answer (c) TtWW

Exercise Q2Light-coloured eyes3 marks

A study found that children with light-coloured eyes are likely to have parents with light-coloured eyes. On this basis, can we say anything about whether the light eye colour trait is dominant or recessive? Why or why not?

Given: a link — light-eyed children are likely to have light-eyed parents. Asked: can we decide dominance; why.

Concept used: the "Can we tell?" card (Topic 4). Why it applies: the data is only a link (correlation).

  1. 1

    What the data shows → light eye colour runs in families → it is an inherited trait.

  2. 2

    Rule 1 — light is dominant → light-eyed parents (with at least one light copy) can pass a light copy → light-eyed children. ✔ fits.

  3. 3

    Rule 2 — light is recessive → light-eyed parents have two light copies and pass only light copies → light-eyed children. ✔ also fits.

  4. 4

    What is missing → no information on what happens when the two forms are crossed: which form shows in the children, and which disappears and reappears, with counts over many families.

  5. Check: a "likely" link is in the "No" column of the card ✔.

  6. Answer No. The study only shows that eye colour is inherited. The finding fits both "light is dominant" and "light is recessive". To decide, we need to know the eye colour of children of parents with different eye colours, over many families — for example, whether light eyes disappear in one generation and reappear in the next.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    • No, we cannot say whether it is dominant or recessive. (1)
    • The data shows only that the trait is inherited; light-eyed children of light-eyed parents are expected whether the trait is dominant or recessive. (1)
    • We would need data from crosses of light-eyed and dark-eyed parents over many generations/offspring, showing which trait is expressed and which reappears. (1)
    Common trap: "Yes — light is dominant because children copy their parents." A link shows inheritance, not dominance.
Exercise Q3Project on coat colour in dogs5 marks

Outline a project which aims to find the dominant coat colour in dogs.

Given: dogs with different coat colours (for example, black and white). Asked: a plan to find the dominant colour.

Concept used: Mendel's method — crosses of pure lines, F1 and F2 (Topics 2 and 4). Why it applies: the trait shown by all F1 offspring is dominant; the hidden one reappears in the F2.

  1. 1

    Choose the trait and two forms → for example, black coat and white coat.

  2. 2

    Pure-breeding parents → select dogs whose families have produced only black puppies for several generations, and others that have produced only white puppies.

  3. 3

    Cross (parents) → mate a pure black dog with a pure white dog — and repeat with several such pairs, including black females with white males and white females with black males.

  4. 4

    Record the F1 → note the coat colour of every puppy in every litter.

  5. 5

    Decide → if all the F1 puppies have one coat colour (say black), that colour is dominant and the hidden one is recessive.

  6. 6

    Confirm (F2) → when the F1 dogs are grown, mate them with each other. The recessive colour should reappear in about one quarter of their puppies (black : white about 3 : 1).

  7. 7

    Use many litters and keep counts → a single litter is too small; real counts only come close to a ratio.

  8. Check: the project uses the same steps that showed tallness is dominant in peas ✔.

  9. Answer Select pure-breeding dogs of two coat colours; cross them (several pairs, both ways round); record the coat colour of all the puppies — the colour shown by all F1 puppies is dominant. Confirm by crossing the F1 dogs among themselves: the recessive colour should reappear in about a quarter of the F2 puppies. Use many litters and record the numbers.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    • Choose two contrasting coat colours and pure-breeding dogs for each (bred true for several generations). (1)
    • Cross the pure lines (several pairs, both ways). (1)
    • Record the coat colour of all puppies (F1); the colour shown by all F1 puppies is dominant. (1)
    • Cross F1 dogs among themselves; the recessive colour reappears in about ¼ of the F2 (3 : 1). (1)
    • Use a large number of litters/offspring and record counts before concluding. (1)
    Common trap: "Survey the dogs in your street and see which colour is commoner." How common a colour is does not show dominance.
Exercise Q4Equal contribution of both parents3 marks

How is the equal genetic contribution of male and female parents ensured in the progeny?

Given: sexual reproduction. Asked: how both parents contribute equally.

Concept used: chromosomes in germ cells (Topic 4). Why it applies: each parent contributes through its germ cell.

  1. 1

    Two copies in body cells → each body cell has two copies of each chromosome — one from the male parent and one from the female parent.

  2. 2

    One copy in germ cells → each germ cell (sperm or egg) takes one chromosome from each pair, so it has half the number — one set of genes.

  3. 3

    Fusion → at fertilisation a male germ cell and a female germ cell fuse; the zygote gets one set from each parent — an equal contribution.

  4. 4

    Number restored → the normal number of chromosomes is restored in the progeny, keeping the DNA of the species stable.

  5. Check: in humans: 23 chromosomes from the father + 23 from the mother = 23 pairs ✔.

  6. Answer Each body cell has two copies of each chromosome, one from each parent. Germ cells take only one chromosome from each pair. When a male and a female germ cell fuse, the progeny gets one set of chromosomes from each parent, so both contribute equally and the normal chromosome number is restored.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    • Body cells have two copies of each chromosome, one from each parent. (1)
    • Each germ cell gets only one chromosome of each pair (one gene set). (1)
    • Fusion of male and female germ cells gives the zygote one set from each parent — equal contribution, and the chromosome number is restored. (1)
    Common trap: "The mother contributes more because the egg is bigger." The genetic contribution — one chromosome of every pair — is equal.
