Topic 4 of 5

How traits are expressed — and why both parents count equally

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain gene → protein (enzyme) → trait, using plant height.
  • Explain why each germ cell carries one copy of each gene, and how chromosomes allow independent inheritance and restore the number.
  • Decide whether data is enough to call a trait dominant, and outline an experiment to find the dominant trait.

A seed packet says "tall variety". But a seed has no ruler inside it. How can a stretch of DNA make a plant grow tall — and why does a plant's height depend on both its parents?

Quick reminder (Chapter 7)

Chromosomes in the nucleus carry DNA. A gamete has half the number of chromosomes of a body cell; when two gametes fuse, the full number is restored in the zygote.

  1. From gene to trait — the height chain

  • DNA in a cell is the information source for making proteins.
  • A gene is a section of DNA that gives the information for one protein.

How can a protein decide height? Plants make hormones that can trigger growth, so a plant's height can depend on how much of a growth hormone it makes. The hormone is made with the help of an enzyme — and an enzyme is a protein.

Gene → enzyme → hormone → height:

  • If the gene gives an enzyme that works efficiently → a lot of hormone is made → the plant is tall.
  • If the gene has a change (alteration) that makes the enzyme less efficient → less hormone is made → the plant is short.

So genes control characteristics, or traits.

  1. Both parents contribute equally

In Mendel's crosses, each F1 plant got a T from its tall parent and a t from its short parent, and the hidden t came back in the F2 — so both parents had passed on a copy that counted. So both parents must contribute equally to the DNA of the offspring — each must give a copy of the same gene.

That means:

  • each pea plant has two sets of all its genes — one inherited from each parent; and
  • for this to work in every generation, each germ cell must carry only one gene set.

  1. How does a germ cell end up with one set? — chromosomes

Suppose a germ cell took one whole gene set as a single long piece from one parent. Then R and y, which came in together from the round-green parent, would always stay together — they would be linked. The F1 could then make only Ry and rY gametes, so the wrinkled-green seeds of Fig 8.5 could never appear, and the F2 would not be 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.

The answer is that a gene set is not one long thread of DNA. It is in separate, independent pieces — the chromosomes.

  • Each body cell has two copies of each chromosome, one from the male parent and one from the female parent.
  • Each germ cell takes one chromosome from each pair. Which one it takes — the copy that came from the mother or the one from the father — is a matter of chance, separately for each pair. This is why traits are inherited independently.
  • When two germ cells fuse, the normal number of chromosomes is restored in the offspring. This keeps the DNA of the species stable from generation to generation.

This one mechanism explains Mendel's results, and every organism that reproduces sexually uses it.

  1. "Can we tell which trait is dominant?" — the card

Calling a trait dominant needs the right kind of evidence.

Data Can we tell? Why
Two pure lines crossed; all offspring show one trait Yes The trait that shows in all the offspring is dominant.
A trait disappears in the offspring and reappears in the next generation Yes The hidden trait is recessive; it shows only with two copies.
Both parents show the same form, and a child shows the other form Yes That form must be recessive: it was hidden in both parents, and the child got one hidden copy from each. (This works for a trait controlled by one gene with two forms, like Mendel's pea traits.)
One family where the child simply looks like one of the parents No The result often fits either rule.
"Children with trait X are likely to have parents with trait X" (a link) No It shows the trait is inherited, not which form is dominant.
One form is more common No Dominant does not mean common or better.

Example (blood groups, one family). A man with blood group A and a woman with blood group O have a daughter with blood group O. If A were dominant, the father could carry a hidden O copy and pass it on — the daughter is O. If O were dominant, the daughter could get an O copy from her mother — she is O again. Both rules fit, so this information is not enough to decide.

  1. Planning an experiment to find the dominant trait

To find the dominant form of a trait in an animal (for example, coat colour in dogs):

  1. Choose pure-breeding parents — dogs whose families have shown only one coat colour for several generations (one line for each colour).
  2. Cross a dog of one colour with a dog of the other colour.
  3. Record the coat colour of all the puppies, in many litters.
  4. Decide — the colour shown by all the puppies is dominant.
  5. Confirm — cross these puppies (when grown) with each other; the other colour should reappear in about one quarter of their puppies.

Using many litters matters because one litter is a small sample, and real counts only come close to a ratio.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Gene to height

Question. Two pea plants grow side by side in the same soil and light. One is tall and one is short. Using your textbook's idea of how genes work, explain how a difference in one gene could cause this.

Given: same conditions; one tall and one short plant. Asked: how one gene could cause the difference.

Concept used: gene → enzyme → hormone → height (this topic). Why it applies: height depends on the amount of a growth hormone.

  1. 1

    Gene → a gene carries the information for making one protein — here, an enzyme that helps make a growth hormone.

  2. 2

    Tall plant → its gene gives an enzyme that works efficiently → a lot of hormone → more growth.

  3. 3

    Short plant → its gene has an alteration → the enzyme is less efficient → less hormone → less growth.

  4. Check: the soil and light are the same, so the difference must come from inside the plant — its genes ✔.

  5. Answer The gene controls an enzyme needed to make a growth hormone. An efficient enzyme gives a lot of hormone and a tall plant; an altered gene gives a less efficient enzyme, less hormone and a short plant.

    Common trap: "The gene itself makes the plant tall." The gene works through a protein (enzyme).

Worked example 2

Is it enough to tell?

Question. Ravi has curly hair and his wife has straight hair. Their only child has curly hair. (Suppose hair type is controlled by one gene with two forms.) Ravi says, "This proves curly hair is dominant." Is he right?

Given: curly × straight; one child, curly. Asked: whether this proves dominance.

Concept used: the "Can we tell?" card (this topic). Why it applies: this is one family where the child looks like one parent.

  1. 1

    If curly were dominant → Ravi could pass on a curly copy → the child is curly. ✔ fits.

  2. 2

    If straight were dominant → Ravi, showing curly, would have two curly copies; his wife could carry a hidden curly copy as well as her straight one; if the child got curly copies from both → the child is curly. ✔ also fits.

  3. 3

    Both rules fit the data → it cannot decide.

  4. 4

    What would help → many families, especially parents who both show one form and have a child with the other form.

  5. Check: a child who looks like one parent is a "No" row of the card ✔.

  6. Answer No. One child from one family fits either rule, so it does not prove which trait is dominant.

    Common trap: "The child looks like the father, so the father's trait is dominant." A child can show a recessive trait if it gets two recessive copies.

Worked example 3

A project on rabbit ears

Question. Suppose a breed of rabbit has either upright ears or drooping ears. Outline a project to find out which ear type is dominant.

Given: two forms of one trait. Asked: a plan to find the dominant form.

Concept used: crosses of pure lines; the trait that reappears is recessive (Topics 2 and 4). Why it applies: this is the same plan Mendel used for peas.

  1. 1

    Pure lines → find rabbits whose families have bred only upright ears for several generations, and others that have bred only drooping ears.

  2. 2

    Cross → mate upright-eared rabbits with drooping-eared ones.

  3. 3

    Record → note the ear type of every young rabbit in many litters.

  4. 4

    Decide → if all the young have one ear type, that type is dominant.

  5. 5

    Confirm → mate these young rabbits (when grown) with each other; the other ear type should come back in about one quarter of their young.

  6. Check: the project has pure parents, a cross, many litters, counting, and a second-generation check ✔.

  7. Answer Cross pure upright with pure drooping rabbits; the ear type shown by all the young is dominant; confirm by crossing the young among themselves — the recessive type returns in about a quarter.

    Common trap: "Count how many rabbits in a town have upright ears." How common a form is does not show dominance.

Which chain correctly shows how a gene controls the height of a pea plant?

The gene comes first: it carries the information for the enzyme.
An enzyme is made from the gene's information, not the other way round.
The gene gives the enzyme; the enzyme helps make the hormone; the amount of hormone decides height.
Height is the result at the end of the chain.
Quick check · 2 of 3MCQ1 mark

Why must each germ cell carry only one copy of each gene?

One copy from each parent restores the pair; the number stays constant.
Both parents contribute equally.
Germ cells do carry chromosomes — one from each pair.
Two germ cells with one copy each give two copies, not four.

A survey found that people with dimples are likely to have a parent with dimples. Can we say from this whether dimples are dominant? Give a reason.

Answer: No. The link shows only that the trait is inherited. To decide dominance we need crosses (or families) that show which form disappears and reappears, or how the offspring of pure parents look.

Common mistakes!

  • "A child who looks like one parent shows that parent's trait is dominant."

    One result often fits either rule.

    Need crosses of pure lines, or a trait that reappears, over many offspring.

  • "Children like their parents → that trait is dominant."

    A link shows inheritance, not dominance.

    Ask: which form disappears and reappears?

  • "The commoner trait is dominant."

    Dominant does not mean common or better.

    Dominant = shows with one copy.

  • "Each parent gives a whole gene set as one piece."

    Then genes would stay linked: the F1 could pass on only the gene sets it received (Ry, rY), never RY or ry, so 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 could not appear.

    Genes are on separate chromosomes; each germ cell takes one chromosome of each pair.

  • "The gene itself makes the plant tall."

    Genes work through proteins.

    Gene → enzyme → hormone → height.

Key points

  • A gene is a section of DNA that gives the information for one protein.
  • Height: gene → enzyme → amount of hormone → height; an altered gene → less efficient enzyme → less hormone → short.
  • Both parents contribute equally: each plant has two gene sets, one from each parent; each germ cell has one.
  • Genes are on separate chromosomes; each germ cell takes one chromosome from each pair → traits are inherited independently.
  • Fusion of two germ cells restores the chromosome number → stable DNA of the species.
  • Dominance needs the right data — pure-line crosses, or a trait that reappears; one family where the child is like one parent, or a "likely" link, is not enough.
