Topic 7 of 7

Oxidation, reduction and their effects in daily life

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Define oxidation and reduction in terms of gain/loss of oxygen or hydrogen.
  • Identify the substance oxidised and the substance reduced in a redox reaction.
  • Explain corrosion and rancidity as effects of oxidation, and how they are prevented.

Squeeze a packet of potato chips: it feels like a little pillow, mostly air. But it isn't air. Manufacturers fill it with nitrogen on purpose. Why go to that trouble? Because the oxygen in ordinary air would slowly attack the oil in the chips and make them smell and taste stale. That "attack by oxygen" has a name — oxidation — and it is the last big idea in this chapter.

Copper has gained oxygen. We say copper has been oxidised.

Part 2 — passing hydrogen over the hot black powder

  • What you observe: The black coating turns brown again.
  • What it shows: Hydrogen has taken the oxygen away from copper oxide, leaving copper metal and forming water.
CuO(s)+HX2(g)→heatCu(s)+HX2O(l)\ce{CuO(s) + H2(g) ->[\text{heat}] Cu(s) + H2O(l)}CuO(s)+HX2​(g)heat​Cu(s)+HX2​O(l)Swipe to see the whole equation →

Copper oxide has lost oxygen — it has been reduced. At the same time, hydrogen has gained oxygen (it became HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O) — hydrogen has been oxidised.

The definitions

In terms of oxygen In terms of hydrogen
Oxidation gain of oxygen loss of hydrogen
Reduction loss of oxygen gain of hydrogen
Memory tip

oxidation and reduction are opposites. If you remember "oxidation = gains Oxygen", flip everything else.

Redox: always a pair

Oxygen does not appear from nowhere or vanish. If one substance loses oxygen, another must gain it. So oxidation and reduction always happen together in the same reaction. Such a reaction is called an oxidation–reduction reaction, or a redox reaction (reduction + oxidation).

How to find what is oxidised and what is reduced — 3 steps

  1. Follow the oxygen (or hydrogen). For each reactant, ask: where did its oxygen/hydrogen go, or where did it get oxygen/hydrogen from?
  2. Gained O or lost H → oxidised. Lost O or gained H → reduced.
  3. Name the whole substance that changed — for example, copper oxide is reduced, not "copper". It is the compound that loses oxygen.

Oxidising agent and reducing agent (useful for exam questions)

Exemplar and sample-paper questions sometimes use two more terms:

  • The oxidising agent is the substance that supplies oxygen (or removes hydrogen) — it causes oxidation of the other substance. The oxidising agent itself gets reduced.
  • The reducing agent is the substance that removes oxygen (or supplies hydrogen) — it causes reduction of the other substance. The reducing agent itself gets oxidised.

In CuO+HX2→Cu+HX2O\ce{CuO + H2 -> Cu + H2O}CuO+HX2​​Cu+HX2​O: copper oxide is the oxidising agent (it gives its oxygen to hydrogen); hydrogen is the reducing agent (it takes oxygen away from copper oxide).

Shortcut

substance reduced = oxidising agent; substance oxidised = reducing agent. They are "crossed".

Oxidation in daily life 1: Corrosion

New iron articles are shiny grey. Leave them in damp air and they get a reddish-brown, flaky coating — rust. When the surface of a metal is slowly damaged by things in its surroundings — oxygen, moisture, acids — we say the metal corrodes. The process is called corrosion.

Metal What you see What it is
Iron reddish-brown flaky coat rust — hydrated iron(III) oxide, FeX2OX3 ⋅ x HX2O\ce{Fe2O3.xH2O}FeX2​OX3​⋅xHX2​O (x = varying number of water molecules)
Silver black coating on anklets, coins, old cutlery silver sulphide, AgX2S\ce{Ag2S}AgX2​S (silver reacts with sulphur compounds such as hydrogen sulphide in the air)
Copper green coating on old copper vessels and statues basic copper carbonate (copper reacts slowly with moist carbon dioxide and oxygen)

Why it matters: corrosion weakens bridges, railway tracks, car bodies, ships and iron railings. Huge amounts of money are spent every year replacing rusted iron.

Rusting needs both air (oxygen) and water. Iron kept in dry air, or in water with no dissolved air, does not rust easily.

How we prevent rusting (more in Chapter 3): stop air and water from reaching the iron —

  • painting (iron gates, grills, bridges),
  • oiling or greasing (cycle chains, machine parts),
  • galvanising — coating with zinc (iron buckets, roofing sheets),
  • chrome plating, and making alloys such as stainless steel (kitchen utensils).
Did you know?Not in board exam

The Iron Pillar in Delhi's Mehrauli area has stood in the open for about 1,600 years without rusting away. Metallurgists at IIT Kanpur found that its iron contains unusually high phosphorus (more than 0.1%, about 0.25% on average, against less than 0.05% in modern iron), which helped a very thin protective layer of an iron–oxygen–hydrogen compound called misawite form on its surface. (Source: R. Balasubramaniam, IIT Kanpur, studies of the Delhi Iron Pillar.)

Oxidation in daily life 2: Rancidity

Have you noticed that namkeen, chips or ghee kept open for weeks smell and taste "off"? When fats and oils are oxidised by oxygen in the air, they break down into smaller substances with an unpleasant smell and taste. They are said to have become rancid, and the process is called rancidity.

Rancidity is a chemical change caused by oxidation — it is not the same as food being spoilt by germs.

How we prevent rancidity — keep oxygen away, or slow the reaction:

  • Antioxidants — substances that prevent oxidation — are added to foods containing fats and oils.
  • Store food in airtight containers.
  • Flush packets with nitrogen — an unreactive gas that pushes out oxygen. (That's your chip packet!)
  • Refrigerate, and keep oily food away from light — low temperature slows the reaction.

Worked example 1

The basic case

Question. In CuO+HX2→Cu+HX2O\ce{CuO + H2 -> Cu + H2O}CuO+HX2​​Cu+HX2​O, identify the substance oxidised, the substance reduced, the oxidising agent and the reducing agent.

Given: the balanced equation. Asked: oxidised, reduced, oxidising agent, reducing agent.

Concept used: oxidation = gain of oxygen; reduction = loss of oxygen; the agents are "crossed". Why it applies: oxygen moves from one reactant to the other.

  1. 1

    Follow the oxygen: it starts in CuO and ends up in HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O.

  2. 2

    CuO lost oxygen (became Cu) → CuO is reduced.

  3. 3

    HX2\ce{H2}HX2​ gained oxygen (became HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O) → HX2\ce{H2}HX2​ is oxidised.

  4. 4

    CuO supplied the oxygen → oxidising agent = CuO.

  5. 5

    HX2\ce{H2}HX2​ removed the oxygen → reducing agent = HX2\ce{H2}HX2​.

  6. Check: the oxidising agent (CuO) is the substance reduced, and the reducing agent (HX2\ce{H2}HX2​) is the substance oxidised — they are crossed, as they must be ✔.

  7. Answer Oxidised: hydrogen. Reduced: copper(II) oxide. Oxidising agent: CuO. Reducing agent: HX2\ce{H2}HX2​.

    Common trap: Saying "CuO is the reducing agent because it is reduced". The substance reduced is the oxidising agent.

Worked example 2

Carbon as the remover of oxygen

Question. Zinc is extracted by heating zinc oxide with carbon: ZnO+C→Zn+CO\ce{ZnO + C -> Zn + CO}ZnO+C​Zn+CO. What is oxidised and what is reduced?

Given: the balanced equation. Asked: the substance oxidised and the substance reduced.

Concept used: follow the oxygen. Why it applies: the oxygen atom moves from zinc oxide to carbon.

  1. 1

    The O atom starts in ZnO and ends in CO.

  2. 2

    ZnO lost oxygen → ZnO is reduced to Zn.

  3. 3

    C gained oxygen → C is oxidised to CO.

  4. Check: Zn 1 | 1, O 1 | 1, C 1 | 1 ✔; one oxidation and one reduction, as in every redox reaction ✔.

  5. Answer Carbon is oxidised (to carbon monoxide); zinc oxide is reduced (to zinc). Carbon is the reducing agent.

    Why this matters: this is how many metals are obtained from their ores — you will see it again in Chapter 3.
    Common trap: Writing "zinc is reduced". Zinc is the product; zinc oxide was reduced.

Worked example 3

Using the hydrogen definitions

Question. MnOX2+4 HCl→MnClX2+2 HX2O+ClX2\ce{MnO2 + 4HCl -> MnCl2 + 2H2O + Cl2}MnOX2​+4HCl​MnClX2​+2HX2​O+ClX2​. Identify the substance oxidised and the substance reduced.

Given: the balanced equation. Asked: the substance oxidised and the substance reduced.

Concept used: oxidation also means loss of hydrogen; reduction means loss of oxygen. Why it applies: no substance gains oxygen here, so the hydrogen definition is needed for oxidation.

  1. 1

    Follow the oxygen: MnOX2\ce{MnO2}MnOX2​ has 2 O atoms; it becomes MnClX2\ce{MnCl2}MnClX2​, which has none. So MnOX2\ce{MnO2}MnOX2​ lost oxygen → MnOX2\ce{MnO2}MnOX2​ is reduced.

  2. 2

    Now look at HCl. Some HCl becomes ClX2\ce{Cl2}ClX2​ — chlorine with no hydrogen. So HCl lost hydrogen → HCl is oxidised (to chlorine).

  3. Check: Mn 1 | 1; O 2 | 2; H 4 | 4; Cl 4 | 2 + 2 = 4 ✔; one oxidation and one reduction ✔.

  4. Answer HCl is oxidised to ClX2\ce{Cl2}ClX2​; MnOX2\ce{MnO2}MnOX2​ is reduced to MnClX2\ce{MnCl2}MnClX2​.

    Common trap: Looking only for oxygen and saying "nothing gains oxygen, so HCl isn't oxidised". Losing hydrogen is oxidation too.

Worked example 4

The classic trap

Question. 2 PbO+C→2 Pb+COX2\ce{2PbO + C -> 2Pb + CO2}2PbO+C​2Pb+COX2​. A student writes: "Lead is reduced and carbon dioxide is oxidised." Find both mistakes and give the correct statements.

Given: the balanced equation and the student's two statements. Asked: the mistakes and the correct statements.

Concept used: oxidation and reduction describe what happens to a reactant. Why it applies: both of the student's statements name products.

  1. 1

    Lead (Pb) is a product; it was never a reactant, so "lead is reduced" is wrong. The reactant that lost oxygen is lead oxide, PbO.

  2. 2

    Carbon dioxide is also a product — it is what carbon becomes. The reactant that gained oxygen is carbon, C.

  3. 3

    Correct statements: PbO loses oxygen → lead oxide is reduced. C gains oxygen → carbon is oxidised.

  4. Check: Pb 2 | 2; O 2 | 2; C 1 | 1 ✔ — and both corrected statements name reactants ✔.

  5. Answer Correct: Lead oxide (PbO) is reduced; carbon (C) is oxidised. Both of the student's statements are incorrect.

    Common trap: This exact idea is tested in NCERT Exercise Q1. Always name the starting substance that gained or lost oxygen.

Common mistakes!

  • "In 2 PbO+C→2 Pb+COX2\ce{2PbO + C -> 2Pb + CO2}2PbO+C​2Pb+COX2​, lead is reduced."

    Lead is a product.

    Name the reactant: lead oxide is reduced.

  • Oxidation without reduction

    If one substance loses oxygen, another must gain it.

    Always find both in a redox reaction.

  • Ignoring hydrogen

    Loss of H is oxidation; gain of H is reduction.

    Use both columns of the definitions table.

  • Oxidising agent = the substance oxidised

    It is the other way round.

    Oxidising agent gets reduced; reducing agent gets oxidised.

  • "Rancidity is caused by germs."

    Rancidity is oxidation of fats and oils.

    Germs cause spoilage; oxygen causes rancidity.

  • "Rust is just iron oxide; only air is needed."

    Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide; both oxygen and water are needed.

    Air + moisture → rust. Keep both away to prevent it.

Key points

  • Oxidation: gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen. Reduction: loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen.
  • They always happen together → redox reaction.
  • Name the reactant that is oxidised or reduced (e.g. lead oxide is reduced, not lead).
  • Oxidising agent gets reduced; reducing agent gets oxidised.
  • Corrosion: metals attacked by air, moisture, acids — rust (iron, reddish-brown), black silver sulphide, green coating on copper. Prevent with paint, oil, zinc coating.
  • Rancidity: oxidation of fats and oils. Prevent with antioxidants, airtight storage, nitrogen flushing, refrigeration.
