Topic 6 of 7

Displacement, double displacement and precipitation reactions

About 40 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Identify displacement reactions and explain them using the idea that a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one.
  • Identify double displacement reactions as exchange of ions.
  • Identify a precipitate and a precipitation reaction.
  • Tell displacement and double displacement apart from an equation.

Dip a clean iron nail into blue copper sulphate solution and wait 20 minutes. The nail comes out with a brownish coat, and the blue solution has turned paler, almost green. Most students say, "The nail has rusted." It hasn't. Something far more interesting has happened.

The reactivity ladder

Some metals react more readily than others. Sodium fizzes violently in water; iron rusts slowly; gold stays shiny for thousands of years. We can arrange metals on a ladder from most reactive (top) to least reactive (bottom). You will study the full reactivity series in Chapter 3; for now, this simple ladder is enough:

Position Metal
Most reactive ↑ Potassium (K)
Sodium (Na)
Calcium (Ca)
Magnesium (Mg)
Aluminium (Al)
Zinc (Zn)
Iron (Fe)
Lead (Pb)
[Hydrogen (H) — for comparison]
Copper (Cu)
Silver (Ag)
Least reactive ↓ Gold (Au)
Rule

a metal higher on the ladder can push a metal lower on the ladder out of its compound. A lower metal cannot push out a higher one.

Displacement reactions: one element pushes another out

In a displacement reaction, a more reactive element takes the place of a less reactive element in its compound.

A+BC⟶AC+B\text{A} + \text{BC} \longrightarrow \text{AC} + \text{B}A+BC⟶AC+BSwipe to see the whole equation →

Notice the pattern: one reactant is a free element (A, on its own) and one product is a free element (B, now pushed out).

Iron nails in copper sulphate solution (a CBSE practical)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Clean two iron nails with sandpaper. Hang them by a thread in copper sulphate solution in test tube B for about 20 minutes. Keep a third nail aside, and keep the same copper sulphate solution in test tube A without a nail, for comparison.
See
The nails in B are coated with a reddish-brown layer. The solution in B is paler blue / light green compared with A.
Shows
Iron is more reactive than copper, so it displaces copper from copper sulphate. The reddish-brown coat is copper metal deposited on the nail — not rust. The blue colour (from copper sulphate) fades because copper sulphate is used up; the pale green colour comes from the iron sulphate formed.

More examples — both zinc and lead are above copper on the ladder:

Zn(s)+CuSOX4(aq)→ZnSOX4(aq)+Cu(s)\ce{Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)}Zn(s)+CuSOX4​(aq)​ZnSOX4​(aq)+Cu(s)Swipe to see the whole equation →
Pb(s)+CuClX2(aq)→PbClX2(aq)+Cu(s)\ce{Pb(s) + CuCl2(aq) -> PbCl2(aq) + Cu(s)}Pb(s)+CuClX2​(aq)​PbClX2​(aq)+Cu(s)Swipe to see the whole equation →

A metal above hydrogen can also displace hydrogen from a dilute acid — that is what happened with zinc and acid in Topic 1:

Zn(s)+HX2SOX4(aq)→ZnSOX4(aq)+HX2(g)\ce{Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g)}Zn(s)+HX2​SOX4​(aq)​ZnSOX4​(aq)+HX2​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →

When nothing happens: put a copper wire in iron sulphate solution and nothing changes. Copper is below iron, so it cannot displace it. Writing "no reaction" is the correct answer to such questions.

Double displacement reactions: two compounds swap partners

In a double displacement reaction, two compounds exchange their ions — like two pairs of dance partners swapping.

AB+CD⟶AD+CB\text{AB} + \text{CD} \longrightarrow \text{AD} + \text{CB}AB+CD⟶AD+CBSwipe to see the whole equation →

Here both reactants are compounds, and both products are compounds. No free element appears.

Sodium sulphate and barium chloride (a CBSE practical)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Mix about 3 mL of sodium sulphate solution with about 3 mL of barium chloride solution.
See
A white solid appears at once and makes the mixture cloudy; it slowly settles.
Shows
The ions have swapped partners. Barium ions (BaX2+\ce{Ba^2+}BaX2+) and sulphate ions (SOX4X2−\ce{SO4^2-}SOX4​X2−) meet to form barium sulphate, which does not dissolve in water, so it appears as a white solid. Sodium chloride stays dissolved.

Precipitate and precipitation reaction

A precipitate is an insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed. A reaction that forms a precipitate is a precipitation reaction.

  • In equations, a precipitate is shown by (s) or by a downward arrow ↓, for example BaSOX4↓\ce{BaSO4 v}BaSOX4​↓.
  • The colour of the precipitate is a useful clue: barium sulphate is white; lead iodide is bright yellow; silver chloride is white.

Most precipitation reactions in this chapter are also double displacement reactions — the swap creates a product that does not dissolve. But not every double displacement gives a precipitate. For example, an acid and a base swapping partners gives a salt and water, with no solid:

NaOH(aq)+HCl(aq)→NaCl(aq)+HX2O(l)\ce{NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)}NaOH(aq)+HCl(aq)​NaCl(aq)+HX2​O(l)Swipe to see the whole equation →

This is double displacement (and also called neutralisation, which you will study in Chapter 2), but not precipitation.

Telling them apart — the one-question test

Is one of the reactants a free element (a single element on its own, like Fe, Zn, Cu, ClX2\ce{Cl2}ClX2​)?

  • Yes → displacement.
  • No — two compounds swap partners → double displacement. Then ask: is an insoluble solid formed? If yes, it is also a precipitation reaction.

Worked example 1

Predict, write, observe

Question. A strip of zinc is placed in blue copper sulphate solution. (a) Will a reaction happen? Why? (b) Write the balanced equation. (c) What two changes would you see?

Given: zinc in copper sulphate solution. Asked: (a) whether it reacts and why; (b) the equation; (c) two observations.

Concept used: displacement — a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one. Why it applies: a free metal is placed in a solution of another metal's compound.

  1. 1

    Find zinc and copper on the ladder: zinc is above copper → zinc is more reactive.

  2. 2

    So zinc can displace copper from copper sulphate → yes, a reaction happens.

  3. 3

    Products: zinc takes copper's place → zinc sulphate (ZnSOX4\ce{ZnSO4}ZnSOX4​, both valency 2) + copper (Cu).

  4. 4

    Count: Zn 1 | 1; Cu 1 | 1; sulphate 1 | 1 → already balanced.

    Zn(s)+CuSOX4(aq)→ZnSOX4(aq)+Cu(s)\ce{Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)}Zn(s)+CuSOX4​(aq)​ZnSOX4​(aq)+Cu(s)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Observation (i): a reddish-brown layer of copper forms on the zinc.

  6. 6

    Observation (ii): the blue colour fades, because zinc sulphate is colourless.

  7. Check: the equation is balanced, and both observations come from the products: copper (brown solid) and zinc sulphate (colourless solution) ✔.

  8. Answer (a) Yes — zinc is more reactive than copper. (b) Zn(s)+CuSOX4(aq)→ZnSOX4(aq)+Cu(s)\ce{Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)}Zn(s)+CuSOX4​(aq)​ZnSOX4​(aq)+Cu(s) (c) Reddish-brown copper deposits on zinc; the blue solution becomes colourless.

    Common trap: Expecting a green colour, as with iron. Zinc sulphate is colourless; iron(II) sulphate is pale green.

Worked example 2

When there is no reaction

Question. Will copper react with iron sulphate solution? Will silver react with copper sulphate solution? Explain.

Given: two metal + solution pairs. Asked: whether each reacts, with a reason.

Concept used: a less reactive metal cannot displace a more reactive one. Why it applies: in both pairs, compare the free metal with the metal already in the compound.

  1. 1

    Copper vs iron: copper is below iron → copper is less reactive → it cannot displace iron → no reaction.

  2. 2

    Silver vs copper: silver is below copper → silver is less reactive → no reaction.

    Cu(s)+FeSOX4(aq)⟶no reaction\ce{Cu(s) + FeSO4(aq)} \longrightarrow \text{no reaction}Cu(s)+FeSOX4​(aq)⟶no reactionSwipe to see the whole equation →

  3. Check: compare the reverse pairs — iron in copper sulphate does react (Topic 6 lab activity), which fits iron being above copper ✔.

  4. Answer Neither reacts, because in each case the free metal is less reactive than the metal already in the compound.

    Common trap: Writing a "balanced" equation just because a question gives two substances. Always check the ladder first.

Worked example 3

Which type is it?

Question. Classify each reaction as displacement or double displacement. Say whether any is also a precipitation reaction.
(a) CaClX2(aq)+NaX2COX3(aq)→CaCOX3(s)+2 NaCl(aq)\ce{CaCl2(aq) + Na2CO3(aq) -> CaCO3(s) + 2NaCl(aq)}CaClX2​(aq)+NaX2​COX3​(aq)​CaCOX3​(s)+2NaCl(aq)
(b) Mg(s)+2 HCl(aq)→MgClX2(aq)+HX2(g)\ce{Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)}Mg(s)+2HCl(aq)​MgClX2​(aq)+HX2​(g)
(c) KOH(aq)+HNOX3(aq)→KNOX3(aq)+HX2O(l)\ce{KOH(aq) + HNO3(aq) -> KNO3(aq) + H2O(l)}KOH(aq)+HNOX3​(aq)​KNOX3​(aq)+HX2​O(l)

Given: three balanced equations with states. Asked: displacement or double displacement; any precipitation.

Concept used: the one-question test — is a reactant a free element? Why it applies: this single feature separates the two types; the state (s) shows a precipitate.

  1. 1

    (a)

    Reactants: CaClX2\ce{CaCl2}CaClX2​ and NaX2COX3\ce{Na2CO3}NaX2​COX3​ — both compounds; no free element.

  2. 2

    Partners swap: Ca goes with COX3\ce{CO3}COX3​; Na goes with Cl → double displacement.

  3. 3

    CaCOX3\ce{CaCO3}CaCOX3​ is marked (s) — an insoluble white solid forms from solutions → also precipitation.

  4. 4

    (b)

    Reactants: Mg (a free element) and HCl.

  5. 5

    Magnesium takes hydrogen's place; hydrogen comes out as HX2\ce{H2}HX2​ gas → displacement.

  6. 6

    (c)

    Reactants: KOH and HNOX3\ce{HNO3}HNOX3​ — both compounds.

  7. 7

    K goes with NOX3\ce{NO3}NOX3​; H goes with OH (forming water) → double displacement.

  8. 8

    No solid forms → not precipitation.

  9. Check: each equation is balanced — e.g. (a) Ca 1 | 1, Cl 2 | 2, Na 2 | 2, C 1 | 1, O 3 | 3 ✔.

  10. Answer (a) Double displacement + precipitation. (b) Displacement. (c) Double displacement (neutralisation), not precipitation.

    Common trap: In (b), calling it double displacement because HCl is a compound. Look for the free element first.

Common mistakes!

  • "The nail turns brown because it rusts."

    The brown layer is copper displaced from the solution.

    Displacement: Fe+CuSOX4→FeSOX4+Cu\ce{Fe + CuSO4 -> FeSO4 + Cu}Fe+CuSOX4​​FeSOX4​+Cu.

  • Mixing up displacement and double displacement

    They look similar at first glance.

    Is a reactant a free element? Yes → displacement; two compounds swapping → double displacement.

  • Writing an equation for Cu\ce{Cu}Cu + FeSOX4\ce{FeSO4}FeSOX4​

    Copper is less reactive than iron.

    Check the ladder; write "no reaction".

  • "All double displacement reactions give a precipitate."

    Acid + base gives salt + water with no solid.

    Precipitation only if an insoluble solid forms.

  • Thinking the blue fades because the solution "dilutes"

    Copper ions are used up as copper metal forms.

    The blue colour is due to copper sulphate; less of it → paler colour.

Key points

  • Displacement: a more reactive element pushes a less reactive one out of its compound: Fe+CuSOX4→FeSOX4+Cu\ce{Fe + CuSO4 -> FeSO4 + Cu}Fe+CuSOX4​​FeSOX4​+Cu.
  • Double displacement: two compounds exchange ions: NaX2SOX4+BaClX2→BaSOX4+2 NaCl\ce{Na2SO4 + BaCl2 -> BaSO4 + 2NaCl}NaX2​SOX4​+BaClX2​​BaSOX4​+2NaCl.
  • Precipitate = insoluble solid formed from solutions; shown as (s) or ↓. Barium sulphate is white; lead iodide is yellow.
  • Test: a free element among the reactants → displacement; two compounds swapping → double displacement.
  • In the iron-nail experiment: brown coating = copper; blue fades to pale green.
