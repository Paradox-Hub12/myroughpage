Topic 3 of 7

Writing and balancing chemical equations

About 60 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Convert a word statement into a word equation and a skeletal chemical equation, identifying reactants and products.
  • Explain why equations must be balanced using the law of conservation of mass.
  • Balance a chemical equation by the hit-and-trial method.
  • Add physical-state symbols (s, l, g, aq) and reaction conditions to an equation.

A cricket scorer does not write "the batter hit the ball very hard over the boundary for six runs". She writes "6". Chemists do the same thing: instead of a long sentence about burning magnesium, they write one neat line. In this lesson you learn to write that line — and to make sure it never "loses" a single atom.

Step 1: From a sentence to a word equation

Here is a sentence: "Magnesium burns in oxygen to form magnesium oxide."

Write the starting substances (reactants) on the left, the new substances (products) on the right, and an arrow between them:

Magnesium+Oxygen⟶Magnesium oxide\text{Magnesium} + \text{Oxygen} \longrightarrow \text{Magnesium oxide}Magnesium+Oxygen⟶Magnesium oxideSwipe to see the whole equation →

This is a word equation.

  • "+" on the left means "reacts with"; "+" on the right means "and".
  • The arrow means "gives" or "forms". It points from reactants to products.

Step 2: From a word equation to a skeletal equation

Replace each name with its formula (Topic 2 toolkit!). Remember oxygen gas is OX2\ce{O2}OX2​.

Mg+OX2→MgO(skeletal — not balanced yet)\ce{Mg + O2 -> MgO}\qquad\text{(skeletal — not balanced yet)}Mg+OX2​​MgO(skeletal — not balanced yet)Swipe to see the whole equation →

An equation that uses correct formulae but has not yet been balanced is called a skeletal chemical equation. It is a skeleton: the right bones, but not yet the right numbers.

Quick reminder: the law of conservation of mass

In a chemical reaction, mass is neither created nor destroyed. The total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.

Why? Because a reaction only rearranges atoms. Bonds break and new bonds form, but every atom you start with is still there at the end — none vanish, none appear from nowhere, and none change into a different element.

Step 3: Why the skeletal equation is not good enough

Count the atoms in Mg+OX2→MgO\ce{Mg + O2 -> MgO}Mg+OX2​​MgO:

Element Left side (reactants) Right side (products)
Mg 1 1
O 2 1

Two oxygen atoms go in, but only one comes out. One oxygen atom has "disappeared" — which is impossible. So we must balance the equation.

A balanced chemical equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides. This is how an equation obeys the law of conservation of mass.

The box rule — the most important rule in this chapter

Imagine a box drawn around every formula. You may never change anything inside a box. You may only write a number (a coefficient) in front of the box.

Why? Because the formula is the substance. HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O is water; HX2OX2\ce{H2O2}HX2​OX2​ is hydrogen peroxide. If you change a subscript, you are writing about a different chemical.

The hit-and-trial method — 6 steps

This is the method used in Class 10. You make sensible "trials" of coefficients until everything matches.

  1. Write the skeletal equation with correct formulae. Draw a mental box around each.
  2. Make an atom-count table — every element, left side and right side.
  3. Start with the formula that has the most atoms, and within it the element with the most atoms. (Leave elements that appear on their own, like OX2\ce{O2}OX2​, HX2\ce{H2}HX2​ or a metal, till later — they are the easiest to adjust at the end.)
  4. Balance one element at a time by putting a coefficient in front of a formula. Update the table after every change.
  5. Recount everything. A change to fix one element may upset another — go round again if needed.
  6. Use the smallest whole numbers. If all coefficients share a factor, divide it out.

Two shortcuts that save time:

  • Polyatomic ions as one unit. If a group like SOX4\ce{SO4}SOX4​, NOX3\ce{NO3}NOX3​ or OH\ce{OH}OH appears unchanged on both sides, count it as one block ("1 sulphate") instead of counting S and O separately.
  • Burning of fuels: balance C first, then H, then O last — because oxygen appears on its own as OX2\ce{O2}OX2​ and is easiest to fix at the end.

Making equations more informative: state symbols

A balanced equation still does not tell you whether each substance is a solid, liquid, gas or solution. We add state symbols in brackets after each formula:

Symbol Meaning Example
(s) solid Mg(s)\ce{Mg(s)}Mg(s), a precipitate such as BaSOX4(s)\ce{BaSO4(s)}BaSOX4​(s)
(l) liquid — the substance itself is a liquid HX2O(l)\ce{H2O(l)}HX2​O(l), mercury Hg(l)\ce{Hg(l)}Hg(l)
(g) gas OX2(g)\ce{O2(g)}OX2​(g), steam HX2O(g)\ce{H2O(g)}HX2​O(g)
(aq) aqueous — dissolved in water salt solution NaCl(aq)\ce{NaCl(aq)}NaCl(aq)

Careful: (l) and (aq) are different. Pure water is HX2O(l)\ce{H2O(l)}HX2​O(l). Salt dissolved in water is NaCl(aq)\ce{NaCl(aq)}NaCl(aq) — the salt is not a liquid, it is in water.

In textbooks and exams, state symbols are written whenever the question asks for them, or when they matter (for example, to show a precipitate or a gas).

Conditions over the arrow

Sometimes the reaction needs special conditions — heat, light, a catalyst, high pressure or temperature. These are written above or below the arrow:

CaCOX3(s)→heatCaO(s)+COX2(g)\ce{CaCO3(s) ->[\text{heat}] CaO(s) + CO2(g)}CaCOX3​(s)heat​CaO(s)+COX2​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →
6 COX2(g)+6 HX2O(l)→chlorophyllsunlightCX6HX12OX6(aq)+6 OX2(g)\ce{6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l) ->[\text{sunlight}][\text{chlorophyll}] C6H12O6(aq) + 6O2(g)}6COX2​(g)+6HX2​O(l)sunlightchlorophyll​CX6​HX12​OX6​(aq)+6OX2​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →

The second one is photosynthesis — your plants make glucose this way.

What a balanced equation tells you — and what it doesn't

A balanced equation tells you what reacts, what forms, and in what ratio (by number of particles). It does not tell you how fast the reaction is, or whether it gives out or takes in heat — unless we add that information.

Worked example 1

One coefficient fixes two elements

Question. Zinc reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to give zinc chloride and hydrogen. Balance: Zn+HCl→ZnClX2+HX2\ce{Zn + HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2}Zn+HCl​ZnClX2​+HX2​.

Given: the skeletal equation. Asked: the balanced equation.

Concept used: hit-and-trial balancing; change only coefficients. Why it applies: the formulae are already correct — only the number of HCl units is wrong.

  1. 1

    Count: Zn 1 | 1; H 1 | 2; Cl 1 | 2.

  2. 2

    Both H and Cl are 1 on the left and 2 on the right. They both come from HCl, so one change can fix both.

  3. 3

    Put 2 in front of HCl. Now H 2 | 2 and Cl 2 | 2.

    Zn+2 HCl→ZnClX2+HX2\ce{Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2}Zn+2HCl​ZnClX2​+HX2​Swipe to see the whole equation →

  4. Check: Zn 1 | 1; H 2 | 2; Cl 2 | 2 ✔.

  5. Answer Zn(s)+2 HCl(aq)→ZnClX2(aq)+HX2(g)\ce{Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)}Zn(s)+2HCl(aq)​ZnClX2​(aq)+HX2​(g)

    Common trap: Writing ZnCl\ce{ZnCl}ZnCl to avoid balancing. Zinc has valency 2, so zinc chloride is always ZnClX2\ce{ZnCl2}ZnClX2​.

Worked example 2

Burning magnesium

Question. Balance Mg+OX2→MgO\ce{Mg + O2 -> MgO}Mg+OX2​​MgO (the skeletal equation from above).

Given: the skeletal equation. Asked: the balanced equation with state symbols.

Concept used: hit-and-trial; box rule. Why it applies: oxygen does not match, and the formula MgO must not be changed.

  1. 1

    Count: Mg 1 | 1; O 2 | 1.

  2. 2

    Oxygen is short on the right. We cannot write MgOX2\ce{MgO2}MgOX2​ (box rule). Put 2 in front of MgO.

    Mg+OX2→2 MgO(partly balanced)\ce{Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}\qquad\text{(partly balanced)}Mg+OX2​​2MgO(partly balanced)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Recount: O 2 | 2 ✔, but Mg 1 | 2 ✘. Fixing O has upset Mg.

  4. 4

    Put 2 in front of Mg.

    2 Mg+OX2→2 MgO\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}2Mg+OX2​​2MgOSwipe to see the whole equation →

  5. Check: Mg 2 | 2; O 2 | 2 ✔.

  6. Answer 2 Mg(s)+OX2(g)→2 MgO(s)\ce{2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)}2Mg(s)+OX2​(g)​2MgO(s) (with state symbols)

    Common trap: Changing MgO to MgOX2\ce{MgO2}MgOX2​. Magnesium (valency 2) and oxygen (valency 2) always give MgO.

Worked example 3

The odd–even trick

Question. Hydrogen peroxide (the antiseptic sold in brown bottles) slowly breaks down into water and oxygen. Balance: HX2OX2→HX2O+OX2\ce{H2O2 -> H2O + O2}HX2​OX2​​HX2​O+OX2​.

Given: the skeletal equation. Asked: the balanced equation.

Concept used: hit-and-trial with the odd–even trick. Why it applies: oxygen ends up odd on one side, but OX2\ce{O2}OX2​ can only supply even numbers.

  1. 1

    Count: H 2 | 2; O 2 | 1 + 2 = 3.

  2. 2

    Oxygen on the right is odd (3). Any coefficient in front of OX2\ce{O2}OX2​ gives an even number, so we must change the other formulae.

  3. 3

    Put 2 in front of HX2OX2\ce{H2O2}HX2​OX2​: left O = 4, left H = 4.

    2 HX2OX2→HX2O+OX2(partly balanced)\ce{2H2O2 -> H2O + O2}\qquad\text{(partly balanced)}2HX2​OX2​​HX2​O+OX2​(partly balanced)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Hydrogen on the right must be 4 → put 2 in front of HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O.

    2 HX2OX2→2 HX2O+OX2\ce{2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2}2HX2​OX2​​2HX2​O+OX2​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Recount oxygen: left 4; right 2 + 2 = 4 ✔.

  6. Check: H 4 | 4; O 4 | 4 ✔. Coefficients 2, 2, 1 share no common factor ✔.

  7. Answer 2 HX2OX2(l)→2 HX2O(l)+OX2(g)\ce{2H2O2(l) -> 2H2O(l) + O2(g)}2HX2​OX2​(l)​2HX2​O(l)+OX2​(g)

    Tip: when an element is odd on one side and even on the other, double the formula with the odd count.

Worked example 4

The full method: iron and steam

Question. Iron reacts with steam to form iron(II,III) oxide, FeX3OX4\ce{Fe3O4}FeX3​OX4​, and hydrogen. Balance the equation and add state symbols.

Given: Fe+HX2O→FeX3OX4+HX2\ce{Fe + H2O -> Fe3O4 + H2}Fe+HX2​O​FeX3​OX4​+HX2​ (skeletal). Asked: balanced equation with state symbols.

Concept used: all six steps of hit-and-trial. Why it applies: three elements are unbalanced, so we need the full routine.

  1. 1

    Step 1 — Boxes. Treat Fe, HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O, FeX3OX4\ce{Fe3O4}FeX3​OX4​, HX2\ce{H2}HX2​ as fixed boxes.

  2. 2

    Step 2 — Count.

    Element Left Right
    Fe 1 3
    H 2 2
    O 1 4
  3. 3

    Step 3 — Start with the biggest formula. FeX3OX4\ce{Fe3O4}FeX3​OX4​ has 7 atoms — the most. Inside it, O (4 atoms) is the largest number, so balance O first.

    • Right side has 4 O; left side has 1 O (in HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O).
    • Put 4 in front of HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O: left O = 1 × 4 = 4 ✔.
    Fe+4 HX2O→FeX3OX4+HX2(partly balanced)\ce{Fe + 4H2O -> Fe3O4 + H2}\qquad\text{(partly balanced)}Fe+4HX2​O​FeX3​OX4​+HX2​(partly balanced)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    Step 4 — Next element: H. Left H = 2 × 4 = 8; right H = 2.

    • Put 4 in front of HX2\ce{H2}HX2​: right H = 2 × 4 = 8 ✔.
    Fe+4 HX2O→FeX3OX4+4 HX2(partly balanced)\ce{Fe + 4H2O -> Fe3O4 + 4H2}\qquad\text{(partly balanced)}Fe+4HX2​O​FeX3​OX4​+4HX2​(partly balanced)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 4

    Step 4 again — Last element: Fe. Left 1, right 3.

    • Put 3 in front of Fe: left Fe = 3 ✔.
    3 Fe+4 HX2O→FeX3OX4+4 HX2\ce{3Fe + 4H2O -> Fe3O4 + 4H2}3Fe+4HX2​O​FeX3​OX4​+4HX2​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 5

    Step 5 — Recount.

    Element Left Right
    Fe 3 3 ✔
    H 8 8 ✔
    O 4 4 ✔
  7. 6

    Step 6 — Smallest whole numbers? 3, 4, 1, 4 share no common factor ✔.

    State symbols: iron is a solid; water is used as steam, so it is a gas; FeX3OX4\ce{Fe3O4}FeX3​OX4​ is a solid; hydrogen is a gas.

  8. Check: the recount table in Step 5 shows every element equal ✔.

  9. Answer 3 Fe(s)+4 HX2O(g)→FeX3OX4(s)+4 HX2(g)\ce{3Fe(s) + 4H2O(g) -> Fe3O4(s) + 4H2(g)}3Fe(s)+4HX2​O(g)​FeX3​OX4​(s)+4HX2​(g)

    Common trap: Writing HX2O(l)\ce{H2O(l)}HX2​O(l) here. Iron reacts with steam, not liquid water — so the state is (g).

Worked example 5

Treat sulphate as one block

Question. Potassium hydroxide reacts with sulphuric acid. Balance KOH+HX2SOX4→KX2SOX4+HX2O\ce{KOH + H2SO4 -> K2SO4 + H2O}KOH+HX2​SOX4​​KX2​SOX4​+HX2​O.

Given: the skeletal equation. Asked: the balanced equation.

Concept used: hit-and-trial, counting the unchanged sulphate group as one block. Why it applies: SOX4\ce{SO4}SOX4​ passes from one side to the other without changing.

  1. 1

    Count using blocks:

    Item Left Right
    K 1 2
    SOX4\ce{SO4}SOX4​ (block) 1 1
    H 1 + 2 = 3 2
    O outside the SOX4\ce{SO4}SOX4​ block 1 (in KOH) 1 (in HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O)
  2. 2

    Sulphate is already balanced. Balance K: put 2 in front of KOH.

    2 KOH+HX2SOX4→KX2SOX4+HX2O(partly balanced)\ce{2KOH + H2SO4 -> K2SO4 + H2O}\qquad\text{(partly balanced)}2KOH+HX2​SOX4​​KX2​SOX4​+HX2​O(partly balanced)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Recount H: left = 2 (in 2KOH) + 2 (in HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​) = 4; right = 2. Put 2 in front of HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O: right H = 4 ✔.

    2 KOH+HX2SOX4→KX2SOX4+2 HX2O\ce{2KOH + H2SO4 -> K2SO4 + 2H2O}2KOH+HX2​SOX4​​KX2​SOX4​+2HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

  4. Check (atom by atom): K 2 | 2; S 1 | 1; O 2 + 4 = 6 | 4 + 2 = 6; H 4 | 4 ✔.

  5. Answer 2 KOH+HX2SOX4→KX2SOX4+2 HX2O\ce{2KOH + H2SO4 -> K2SO4 + 2H2O}2KOH+HX2​SOX4​​KX2​SOX4​+2HX2​O

    Common trap: Counting every oxygen atom from the start and getting lost. The block method keeps the numbers small.

Worked example 6

Brackets on both sides

Question. Some antacid tablets contain aluminium hydroxide. With nitric acid it gives aluminium nitrate and water. Balance Al(OH)X3+HNOX3→Al(NOX3)X3+HX2O\ce{Al(OH)3 + HNO3 -> Al(NO3)3 + H2O}Al(OH)X3​+HNOX3​​Al(NOX3​)X3​+HX2​O.

Concept used: count bracketed groups correctly (Topic 2) and treat NOX3\ce{NO3}NOX3​ as one block. Why it applies: both Al(OH)X3\ce{Al(OH)3}Al(OH)X3​ and Al(NOX3)X3\ce{Al(NO3)3}Al(NOX3​)X3​ have a subscript 3 outside a bracket.

  1. 1

    Count:

    Item Left Right
    Al 1 1
    NOX3\ce{NO3}NOX3​ (block) 1 3 (the subscript 3 outside the bracket)

Worked example 7

Criss-cross first, then balance two groups

Question. Calcium chloride solution reacts with sodium phosphate solution to give a white precipitate of calcium phosphate and a solution of sodium chloride. Write the balanced equation.

    Calcium chloride: Ca (2), Cl (1) → CaClX2\ce{CaCl2}CaClX2​.

  2. 2

Worked example 8

Burning a fuel (C, then H, then O)

Question. Methane (CHX4\ce{CH4}CHX4​, the main gas in CNG and biogas) burns in oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water. Balance the equation.

Given: CHX4+OX2→COX2+HX2O\ce{CH4 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}CHX4​+OX2​​COX2​+HX2​O (skeletal). Asked: the balanced equation.

Concept used: the fuel rule — balance C first, H second, O last. Why it applies: oxygen appears on its own as OX2\ce{O2}OX2​, so adjusting it last cannot upset C or H.

  1. 1

    C: left 1, right 1 ✔ — nothing to do.

  2. 2

    H: left 4, right 2. Put 2 in front of HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O: right H = 4 ✔.

    CHX4+OX2→COX2+2 HX2O(partly balanced)\ce{CH4 + O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}\qquad\text{(partly balanced)}CHX4​+OX2​​COX2​+2HX2​O(partly balanced)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    O (last): right O = 2 (in COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​) + 2 (in 2 HX2O\ce{2H2O}2HX2​O) = 4. Left has 2 in OX2\ce{O2}OX2​. Put 2 in front of OX2\ce{O2}OX2​: left O = 4 ✔.

    CHX4+2 OX2→COX2+2 HX2O\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}CHX4​+2OX2​​COX2​+2HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

  4. Check: C 1 | 1; H 4 | 4; O 4 | 4 ✔.

  5. Answer CHX4(g)+2 OX2(g)→COX2(g)+2 HX2O(g)\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}CHX4​(g)+2OX2​(g)​COX2​(g)+2HX2​O(g) (water forms as steam in the flame)

    Common trap: Balancing O first; then fixing H changes O again and you go round in circles.

Worked example 9

Decomposition with three products

Question. On heating, lead nitrate gives lead oxide, nitrogen dioxide and oxygen. Balance: Pb(NOX3)X2→PbO+NOX2+OX2\ce{Pb(NO3)2 -> PbO + NO2 + O2}Pb(NOX3​)X2​​PbO+NOX2​+OX2​.

Given: the skeletal equation. Asked: the balanced equation.

Concept used: hit-and-trial; when oxygen appears in several products, balance the other elements first and oxygen last; double if you get stuck. Why it applies: oxygen is in all three products.

  1. 1

    Count: Pb 1 | 1; N 2 | 1; O 6 | 1 + 2 + 2 = 5.

  2. 2

    N: put 2 in front of NOX2\ce{NO2}NOX2​: N 2 | 2 ✔.

    Pb(NOX3)X2→PbO+2 NOX2+OX2(partly balanced)\ce{Pb(NO3)2 -> PbO + 2NO2 + O2}\qquad\text{(partly balanced)}Pb(NOX3​)X2​​PbO+2NOX2​+OX2​(partly balanced)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    O: left 6; right 1 + 4 + 2 = 7. One too many on the right. We cannot use half an OX2\ce{O2}OX2​ in the final answer, so double the lead nitrate.

  4. 4

    Put 2 in front of Pb(NOX3)X2\ce{Pb(NO3)2}Pb(NOX3​)X2​: now Pb 2, N 4, O 12 on the left.

  5. 5

    Match Pb: 2 in front of PbO. Match N: 4 in front of NOX2\ce{NO2}NOX2​.

    2 Pb(NOX3)X2→2 PbO+4 NOX2+OX2\ce{2Pb(NO3)2 -> 2PbO + 4NO2 + O2}2Pb(NOX3​)X2​​2PbO+4NOX2​+OX2​Swipe to see the whole equation →

    Recount O on the right: 2 (in 2PbO) + 8 (in 4 NOX2\ce{4NO2}4NOX2​) + 2 (in OX2\ce{O2}OX2​) = 12 ✔.

    Common trap: Leaving 12OX2\dfrac{1}{2}\ce{O2}21​OX2​ in the final answer. Double everything instead.

Worked example 10

From a sentence to a balanced equation with state symbols

Question. "Solutions of silver nitrate and calcium chloride react to form insoluble silver chloride and a solution of calcium nitrate." Write the balanced equation with state symbols.

Given: a word statement. Asked: balanced equation with (s), (aq) etc.

Concept used: word → skeletal → balanced → state symbols. Why it applies: the sentence names every substance and tells us each one's state.

  1. 1

    Word equation: silver nitrate + calcium chloride → silver chloride + calcium nitrate.

  2. 2

    Formulae: AgNOX3\ce{AgNO3}AgNOX3​ (Ag 1, NOX3\ce{NO3}NOX3​ 1); CaClX2\ce{CaCl2}CaClX2​; AgCl\ce{AgCl}AgCl; Ca(NOX3)X2\ce{Ca(NO3)2}Ca(NOX3​)X2​ (Ca 2, NOX3\ce{NO3}NOX3​ 1).

    AgNOX3+CaClX2→AgCl+Ca(NOX3)X2(skeletal)\ce{AgNO3 + CaCl2 -> AgCl + Ca(NO3)2}\qquad\text{(skeletal)}AgNOX3​+CaClX2​​AgCl+Ca(NOX3​)X2​(skeletal)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Count: Ag 1 | 1; nitrate 1 | 2; Ca 1 | 1; Cl 2 | 1.

  4. 4

    Nitrate: put 2 in front of AgNOX3\ce{AgNO3}AgNOX3​ → nitrate 2 | 2, but now Ag 2 | 1.

  5. 5

    Silver and chlorine: put 2 in front of AgCl\ce{AgCl}AgCl → Ag 2 | 2 and Cl 2 | 2.

    2 AgNOX3+CaClX2→2 AgCl+Ca(NOX3)X2\ce{2AgNO3 + CaCl2 -> 2AgCl + Ca(NO3)2}2AgNOX3​+CaClX2​​2AgCl+Ca(NOX3​)X2​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    State symbols from the sentence: "solutions" → both reactants (aq); "insoluble silver chloride" → (s); "a solution of calcium nitrate" → (aq).

  7. Check: Ag 2 | 2; N 2 | 2; O 6 | 6; Ca 1 | 1; Cl 2 | 2 ✔. The only (s) is the substance the sentence calls insoluble ✔.

  8. Answer 2 AgNOX3(aq)+CaClX2(aq)→2 AgCl(s)+Ca(NOX3)X2(aq)\ce{2AgNO3(aq) + CaCl2(aq) -> 2AgCl(s) + Ca(NO3)2(aq)}2AgNOX3​(aq)+CaClX2​(aq)​2AgCl(s)+Ca(NOX3​)X2​(aq)

A student "balances" HX2+OX2→HX2O\ce{H2 + O2 -> H2O}HX2​+OX2​​HX2​O by writing HX2+OX2→HX2OX2\ce{H2 + O2 -> H2O2}HX2​+OX2​​HX2​OX2​. What is wrong?

The atoms match, but the equation now describes a different reaction.

Hydrogen and oxygen gases are diatomic — never written as H or O.

Balance: Al+OX2→AlX2OX3\ce{Al + O2 -> Al2O3}Al+OX2​​AlX2​OX3​.

  1. Oxygen: 2 on the left, 3 on the right. The smallest number both 2 and 3 divide into is 6.

(a) What do (aq) and (l) mean, and how are they different? (b) Why must a chemical equation be balanced?

Answer: (a) (aq) = dissolved in water (an aqueous solution); (l) = the substance itself is a liquid. Salt water is NaCl(aq)\ce{NaCl(aq)}NaCl(aq); pure water is HX2O(l)\ce{H2O(l)}HX2​O(l). (b) Because of the law of conservation of mass — atoms are not created or destroyed in a reaction, so each element must have the same number of atoms on both sides.

Common mistakes!

  • Changing subscripts (HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O → HX2OX2\ce{H2O2}HX2​OX2​)

    Changes the substance.

    Box rule: change only coefficients.

  • Miscounting brackets in Ca(OH)X2\ce{Ca(OH)2}Ca(OH)X2​

    The outside 2 doubles both O and H.

    Multiply everything inside the bracket.

  • Writing H, O, N, Cl for gases

    They are diatomic.

    HX2\ce{H2}HX2​, OX2\ce{O2}OX2​, NX2\ce{N2}NX2​, ClX2\ce{Cl2}ClX2​.

  • Breaking SOX4\ce{SO4}SOX4​ into S and O when it is unchanged

    Makes counting messy and error-prone.

    Count unchanged polyatomic ions as one block.

  • Stopping after one element balances

    Fixing one element can upset another.

    Always do a full recount at the end.

  • Mixing up (l) and (aq)

    (aq) means dissolved in water.

    Solutions are (aq); only true liquids are (l).

  • Leaving a fraction like 12OX2\dfrac{1}{2}\ce{O2}21​OX2​ in the final answer

    Class 10 answers use whole numbers.

    Multiply every coefficient by the denominator.

Key points

  • Sentence → word equation → skeletal equation → balanced equation → add state symbols and conditions.
  • Equations are balanced because of the law of conservation of mass: atoms are only rearranged.
  • Box rule: never change a formula; only add coefficients in front.
  • Hit-and-trial: count → start with the biggest formula → one element at a time → recount → smallest whole numbers.
  • Shortcuts: unchanged polyatomic ions as one block; for fuels balance C → H → O.
  • State symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq). Conditions such as heat or sunlight go above the arrow.
