CBSE Sample Question Paper 2026-27

CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section B, Q171 mark

Question: Chameleons contain crystals that reflect light and produce colour. Adjusting the spacing between these crystals, they can alter the wavelengths of light reflected, thus changing their skin colour. Based on the above information, we can infer that the change in colour by chameleons is a:

Given: a chameleon changes colour by changing the spacing between crystals in its skin. Asked: is this a physical or a chemical change, and why?

Concept used: in a chemical change a new substance forms; in a physical change the substance stays the same and only its arrangement, shape or state changes (Topic 1). Why it applies: we have to decide whether any new substance is made.

  1. 1

    What actually changes? Only the spacing between the crystals.

  2. 2

    The crystals are the same crystals before and after — no new substance is formed.

  3. 3

    The different spacing reflects a different colour of light, so the skin looks different.

  4. 4

    No new substance → physical change, and the reason is that only the spacing is adjusted → option C.

  5. Check: the change can be reversed simply by moving the crystals back, which is typical of a physical change ✔.

    Why the other options are wrong: A — a colour change is only a sign to look for; here no new substance forms, so it is not chemical. B — the reflected light changes because the spacing changes, not because a new substance is made. D — "physical change" is right, but the reason is not: crystals reflect light all the time; what causes the change is the new spacing.
    Common trap: Choosing A because "change in colour" is on the list of signs of a chemical reaction. Always ask: has a new substance formed?
C. Physical change because the space between crystals is adjusted. No new substance is formed; only the spacing between the crystals changes, which alters the wavelength of light reflected.
1 mark
CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section B, Q221 mark

Question: (Figure described in words: the ingredients label of a packet of flavoured biscuits. It lists wheat flour, vegetable oil, sugar, salt, malt extract, baking powder, flavour enhancers (E621, E635), onion powder, natural flavour, maltodextrin, chicken, spices, antioxidants (E304, E307b from soy) and an emulsifier (soy lecithin).) A food label is given below. E304 and E307b are added to the flavoured biscuits. Which of the following is added to food packets for the same reason?

Given: a biscuit label; E304 and E307b are listed as antioxidants. Asked: which substance is added to food packets for the same reason.

Concept used: rancidity and its prevention (Topic 7). Why it applies: the biscuits contain vegetable oil, and antioxidants are added to stop oils and fats from being oxidised.

  1. 1

    The label itself tells you what E304 and E307b are: antioxidants.

  2. 2

    Antioxidants prevent oxidation. In biscuits, they stop the vegetable oil from reacting with oxygen and turning rancid (stale smell and taste).

  3. 3

    So the "reason" is: keep the oil from being oxidised.

  4. 4

    Which option does the same job? Nitrogen — chips and biscuit packets are flushed with nitrogen to push out the oxygen, so the oil cannot be oxidised.

  5. 5

    Answer → option C.

  6. Check: both antioxidants and nitrogen flushing are listed in Topic 7 as ways of preventing rancidity ✔.

    Why the other options are wrong: A and B — salt and vinegar do help food keep, but by stopping germs from growing, not by preventing oxidation of fats. D — vanilla is added for flavour.
    Accuracy note

    the marking scheme says nitrogen "is also used as an antioxidant". Strictly, nitrogen is an unreactive gas: it does not react with anything in the food; it simply keeps oxygen away. It is added for the same reason as antioxidants — to prevent oxidation — which is what this question asks.

    Common trap: Choosing salt because it is a familiar "preservative". The question is about the reason written on the label — antioxidants.
C. Nitrogen. In the label, E304 and E307b are antioxidants; nitrogen is also added to food packets to prevent oxidation (rancidity) of the oils and fats.

CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section B, Q22 — alternative for visually impaired candidates1 mark

Question: In the food industry nitrogen is used as a/an

Given: nitrogen is used in the food industry. Asked: its role, from the four options.

Concept used: preventing rancidity (Topic 7). Why it applies: nitrogen is filled into packets of chips and other oily foods.

  1. 1

    Food packets are flushed with nitrogen to remove oxygen.

  2. 2

    Without oxygen, the fats and oils cannot be oxidised, so the food does not turn rancid.

  3. 3

    Something used to prevent oxidation is called an antioxidant in the marking scheme → option C.

  4. Check: nitrogen is unreactive, so it cannot be an oxidising agent (B), and it is colourless, so it cannot colour food (D) ✔.

    Why the other options are wrong: B — an oxidising agent causes oxidation, the opposite of what is wanted. D — nitrogen is a colourless gas. A — in everyday speech nitrogen flushing does help food last longer, but the marking scheme's answer is antioxidant, because its job is to stop oxidation.
    Common trap: Picking "oxidising agent" because the word "oxidation" is linked with rancidity. Nitrogen is used to stop oxidation, not to cause it.
C. Antioxidant

CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section B, Q27 part C (1 of the question's 3 marks)1 mark

Question (part C): Give reason for the following: An iron bridge near the sea rusts much faster than an iron bridge located in a dry hilly region.

Given: two iron bridges — one near the sea, one in a dry hilly region. Asked: why the bridge near the sea rusts faster.

Concept used: corrosion — rusting of iron needs both oxygen and water (moisture) (Topic 7). Why it applies: the two places differ in how much moisture the air holds.

  1. 1

    Rusting happens only when iron is in contact with both air (oxygen) and water.

  2. 2

    Air near the sea has a lot of moisture (high humidity).

  3. 3

    In a dry hilly region the air has little moisture.

  4. 4

    More moisture on the iron surface → rusting is faster near the sea; less moisture → rusting is much slower in the dry region.

  5. Check: this matches the rule from Topic 7 — iron kept in dry air does not rust easily ✔.

    Did you know?Not in board exam

    Sea air also carries tiny drops of salt water. Dissolved salt makes rusting even faster, which is why ships, piers and seaside railings need extra protection.

    Common trap: Saying "because of the sea water" without the key idea. Write the reason the examiner looks for: more moisture (humidity) in the air speeds up rusting.
C. Air near the sea contains high moisture (humidity), which speeds up rusting. In a dry hilly region moisture is low, so rusting occurs much more slowly.

CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section B, Q29 (A)5 marks

Question:
I. Refer to the dialogue between Alisha, Drishti and Bhushan in the chemistry lab. They are discussing rusting of iron and formation of ammonia gas. Who all do you agree with and why?
Alisha: The formation of ammonia by reaction of nitrogen with hydrogen is a reduction reaction.
Drishti: Both rusting of iron and formation of ammonia are redox reactions.
Bhushan: Rusting of iron is an oxidation reaction.
II. Assess the following chemical equations. Identify the error(s) and rewrite the equation after correction.
(i) MgClX2+AgNOX3→AgClX2+MgNOX3\ce{MgCl2 + AgNO3 -> AgCl2 + MgNO3}MgClX2​+AgNOX3​​AgClX2​+MgNOX3​
(ii) Al+HX2SOX4→AlX2SOX4+HX2O\ce{Al + H2SO4 -> Al2SO4 + H2O}Al+HX2​SOX4​​AlX2​SOX4​+HX2​O

Given: three students' statements about two reactions; two wrongly written equations. Asked: I. whom to agree with, and why; II. the errors, and the corrected equations.

Concept used: I — oxidation is gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen; reduction is loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen; the two always happen together (Topic 7). II — writing formulae from valencies (Topic 2) and balancing by hit and trial (Topic 3). Why it applies: each statement is about oxidation or reduction, and each equation has wrong formulae.

  1. 1

    Part I — the dialogue

    Ammonia: nitrogen combines with hydrogen.

    NX2(g)+3 HX2(g)→2 NHX3(g)\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}NX2​(g)+3HX2​(g)​2NHX3​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Nitrogen gains hydrogen → nitrogen is reduced. So Alisha is right that reduction takes place.

  3. 3

    But reduction never happens alone: whatever reduces the nitrogen is itself oxidised. Here that is hydrogen — so hydrogen is oxidised. The reaction is a redox reaction, not "only reduction".

  4. 4

    Rusting: iron combines with oxygen (in the presence of water) to form rust, hydrated iron(III) oxide, FeX2OX3 ⋅ x HX2O\ce{Fe2O3.xH2O}FeX2​OX3​⋅xHX2​O.

  5. 5

    Iron gains oxygen → iron is oxidised. So Bhushan is right that oxidation takes place.

  6. 6

    At the same time oxygen is used up by the iron → oxygen is reduced. So rusting is also a redox reaction, not "only oxidation".

  7. 7

    Drishti says both are redox reactions → she is fully correct.

  8. 8

    Alisha and Bhushan are each partially correct — each describes only one half of a redox reaction.

  9. 9

    Part II (i)

    First notice: the atoms in the printed equation already match on both sides (Mg 1, Cl 2, Ag 1, N 1, O 3). That does not make it correct — the formulae are wrong. Silver has valency 1 and chloride has valency 1, so silver chloride is AgCl\ce{AgCl}AgCl — not AgClX2\ce{AgCl2}AgClX2​.

  10. 10

    Magnesium has valency 2 and nitrate has valency 1, so magnesium nitrate is Mg(NOX3)X2\ce{Mg(NO3)2}Mg(NOX3​)X2​ — not MgNOX3\ce{MgNO3}MgNOX3​.

  11. 11

    Skeletal equation with correct formulae: MgClX2+AgNOX3→AgCl+Mg(NOX3)X2\ce{MgCl2 + AgNO3 -> AgCl + Mg(NO3)2}MgClX2​+AgNOX3​​AgCl+Mg(NOX3​)X2​.

  12. 12

    Balance Cl: 2 on the left, so put 2 in front of AgCl\ce{AgCl}AgCl. Then Ag needs 2 on the left: put 2 in front of AgNOX3\ce{AgNO3}AgNOX3​.

    MgClX2(aq)+2 AgNOX3(aq)→2 AgCl(s)+Mg(NOX3)X2(aq)\ce{MgCl2(aq) + 2AgNO3(aq) -> 2AgCl(s) + Mg(NO3)2(aq)}MgClX2​(aq)+2AgNOX3​(aq)​2AgCl(s)+Mg(NOX3​)X2​(aq)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  13. 13

    Part II (ii)

    Aluminium has valency 3 and sulphate has valency 2, so aluminium sulphate is AlX2(SOX4)X3\ce{Al2(SO4)3}AlX2​(SOX4​)X3​ — not AlX2SOX4\ce{Al2SO4}AlX2​SOX4​.

  14. 14

    A metal reacting with a dilute acid gives a salt and hydrogen gas — not water. So the second product is HX2\ce{H2}HX2​, not HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O.

  15. 15

    Skeletal equation with correct formulae: Al+HX2SOX4→AlX2(SOX4)X3+HX2\ce{Al + H2SO4 -> Al2(SO4)3 + H2}Al+HX2​SOX4​​AlX2​(SOX4​)X3​+HX2​.

  16. 16

    Balance SOX4\ce{SO4}SOX4​ as a block: 3 on the right, so put 3 in front of HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​.

  17. 17

    Balance Al: 2 on the right, so put 2 in front of Al.

  18. 18

    Balance H: 6 on the left (3 × 2), so put 3 in front of HX2\ce{H2}HX2​.

    2 Al(s)+3 HX2SOX4(aq)→AlX2(SOX4)X3(aq)+3 HX2(g)\ce{2Al(s) + 3H2SO4(aq) -> Al2(SO4)3(aq) + 3H2(g)}2Al(s)+3HX2​SOX4​(aq)​AlX2​(SOX4​)X3​(aq)+3HX2​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →

  19. Check: (i) Mg 1 | 1, Cl 2 | 2, Ag 2 | 2, N 2 | 2, O 6 | 6 ✔. (ii) Al 2 | 2, H 6 | 6, S 3 | 3, O 12 | 12 ✔. Ammonia: N 2 | 2, H 6 | 6 ✔.

    Common trap: In II, only adding numbers in front of the wrong formulae (AgClX2\ce{AgCl2}AgClX2​, AlX2SOX4\ce{Al2SO4}AlX2​SOX4​). Fix the formulae first, then balance — you can never change a formula to balance an equation.
I. I agree with Drishti. (Alisha and Bhushan are partially correct.)

CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section B, Q29 (B)5 marks

Question:
I. Refer to the dialogue between Daljeet and Avik in the chemistry lab. They have learnt about decomposition reactions. Who has understood decomposition reactions correctly? Justify your answer.
Daljeet: During a decomposition reaction, a substance decomposes to give two simpler compounds. MgCOX3\ce{MgCO3}MgCOX3​ decomposes on heating to magnesium oxide and carbon dioxide.
Avik: No, during a decomposition reaction, a substance decomposes to give two elements. Water on electrolysis gives hydrogen and oxygen, both are elements.
II. Analyse the observations given in following two cases. Are these observations correct? Give reasons to support your answer.
(i) Iron fillings are added to an aqueous solution of magnesium sulphate. Observation: The colourless solution turns greenish due to formation of iron sulphate.
(ii) Aluminium metal is added to dilute sulphuric acid. Observation: Brisk effervescence are seen due to evolution of a gas.

Given: two students' definitions of decomposition, each with an example; two lab observations. Asked: I. who is right, with a reason; II. whether each observation is correct, with reasons.

Concept used: I — a decomposition reaction is one in which a single compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances, which may be elements or compounds (Topic 5). II — a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution, and a metal above hydrogen displaces hydrogen from a dilute acid (Topic 6). Why it applies: I tests the definition; II tests the reactivity ladder.

  1. 1

    Part I — the dialogue

    Daljeet's example is correct:

    MgCOX3(s)→heatMgO(s)+COX2(g)\ce{MgCO3(s) ->[\text{heat}] MgO(s) + CO2(g)}MgCOX3​(s)heat​MgO(s)+COX2​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →

    Here both products are compounds.

  2. 2

    Avik's example is also correct:

    2 HX2O(l)→electric current2 HX2(g)+OX2(g)\ce{2H2O(l) ->[\text{electric current}] 2H2(g) + O2(g)}2HX2​O(l)electric current​2HX2​(g)+OX2​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →

    Here both products are elements.

  3. 3

    But each student's rule is too narrow. Daljeet says the products must be compounds; Avik says they must be elements. The real rule: a single compound breaks down into simpler substances, and these can be elements or compounds (or a mix).

  4. 4

    So both have understood decomposition only partially. Each example is a decomposition reaction, which proves the other student's rule wrong.

  5. 5

    Part II (i)

    Iron is below magnesium on the reactivity ladder.

  6. 6

    A less reactive metal cannot displace a more reactive one from its salt solution.

  7. 7

    So no reaction happens: no iron sulphate forms and the solution does not turn green. The observation is incorrect.

  8. 8

    Part II (ii)

    Aluminium is above hydrogen, so it does react with dilute sulphuric acid and gives hydrogen gas:

    2 Al(s)+3 HX2SOX4(aq)→AlX2(SOX4)X3(aq)+3 HX2(g)\ce{2Al(s) + 3H2SO4(aq) -> Al2(SO4)3(aq) + 3H2(g)}2Al(s)+3HX2​SOX4​(aq)​AlX2​(SOX4​)X3​(aq)+3HX2​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  9. 9

    The marking scheme marks the observation incorrect: hydrogen is formed, but it produces only a few bubbles. "Brisk effervescence" (rapid, vigorous fizzing) is the description used when carbon dioxide is formed — for example, when an acid reacts with a carbonate (Chapter 2).

    Note on this answer

    a gas is given off here, so the observation is only partly wrong; the scheme's objection is to the word "brisk". (Aluminium is normally covered by a thin protective layer of aluminium oxide — Chapter 3 — so with dilute acid the reaction starts slowly.) In the exam, write the scheme's reason: hydrogen is formed, giving a few bubbles; brisk effervescence is seen when carbon dioxide is formed.

  10. Check: I — both equations are balanced (Mg 1 | 1, C 1 | 1, O 3 | 3; H 4 | 4, O 2 | 2) ✔. II (i) — iron in magnesium sulphate is the "lower metal into a higher metal's salt" case, which never reacts ✔.

    Common trap: In II (i), saying "iron sulphate is green, so the observation is correct". First ask whether a reaction can happen at all — iron is less reactive than magnesium.
I. Both have understood decomposition partially. A decomposition reaction is when a single substance breaks down into simpler substances. The substance undergoing decomposition has to be a compound, while the products formed can be either elements or compounds. MgCOX3\ce{MgCO3}MgCOX3​ decomposes on heating to magnesium oxide and carbon dioxide; even though both products are compounds, it is decomposition.
2 marks
Water on electrolysis gives hydrogen and oxygen, both elements. It is also a decomposition reaction.
1 mark
II. (i) Incorrect observation: iron is less reactive than magnesium, so it cannot displace magnesium.
1 mark
(ii) Incorrect: hydrogen gas is formed, which produces only a few bubbles; brisk effervescence is seen when carbon dioxide is formed.

Question (paraphrased): Which of these equations are redox reactions, and what are p and q?
Equation 1: FeX2OX3(s)+2 Al(s)→AlX2OX3(s)+p Fe(l)+heat\ce{Fe2O3(s) + 2Al(s)} \rightarrow \ce{Al2O3(s)} + p\,\ce{Fe(l)} + \text{heat}FeX2​OX3​(s)+2Al(s)→AlX2​OX3​(s)+pFe(l)+heat

    Finding p

    Fe on the left: 2 (in FeX2OX3\ce{Fe2O3}FeX2​OX3​).

    Fe on the right: p.

  3. 3

    So p=2p = 2p=2.

  4. 4

  5. 5

    H on the right: q×2q \times 2q×2.

  6. 6

    2q=202q = 202q=20

  7. 7

    q=10q = 10q=10

  9. 9

    Equation 2: oxygen gas is a reactant; butane gains oxygen and loses hydrogen (→ COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​, HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O) → oxidised; oxygen is reduced → redox.

  10. 10

    Both redox, p = 2, q = 10 → option D.

  11. Check: in equation 2, O on the left = 13 × 2 = 26; O on the right = 8 × 2 + 10 × 1 = 26 ✔. In equation 1, Fe 2 | 2 ✔.

    Why the other options are wrong: A and C wrongly say only one equation is redox. B has q = 4, which would leave only 8 H on the right instead of 20.

D. Both equations 1 and 2 are redox reactions, p = 2 and q = 10

CBSE SQP 2025-26, Section B, Q191 mark

    In P, iron displaces copper → a brown (reddish-brown) coating of copper forms on the nail:

CBSE SQP 2025-26, Section B, Q273 marks

Question (paraphrased): Amrita set up an electrolysis circuit with distilled water, expecting gases at both electrodes. The bulb in the circuit did not glow. She then added a substance to the water and electrolysis took place.
A. Which gas did she expect at the anode, and which at the cathode?
B. Why did the bulb not glow with distilled water?
C. Which substance could she have added to get the expected result?

Given: electrolysis of distilled water fails (bulb does not glow) until a substance is added. Asked: A, B, C as above.

Concept used: electrolysis of water (Topic 5) — electricity decomposes water into hydrogen and oxygen, but only if the liquid conducts electricity. Why it applies: the case is exactly this set-up.

  1. 1

    A.

    Water splits as 2 HX2O(l)→electric current2 HX2(g)+OX2(g)\ce{2H2O(l) ->[\text{electric current}] 2H2(g) + O2(g)}2HX2​O(l)electric current​2HX2​(g)+OX2​(g).

  2. 2

    Hydrogen is released at the negative electrode (cathode).

  3. 3

    Oxygen is released at the positive electrode (anode).

  4. 4

    B.

    A bulb glows only when current flows through the whole circuit.

  5. 5

    Current flows through a liquid only if the liquid has ions that can move.

  6. 6

    Distilled water has almost no ions, so it is a very poor conductor → no current → no glow and no electrolysis.

  7. 7

    C.

    She must add an electrolyte — a substance that gives plenty of ions in water.

  8. 8

    A few drops of dilute sulphuric acid is the standard choice (as in the NCERT activity).

  9. 9

    The marking scheme also accepts common salt (NaCl\ce{NaCl}NaCl) or any other strong electrolyte.

  10. Check: the answer to C explains B — once ions are present, current flows and the bulb glows ✔.
