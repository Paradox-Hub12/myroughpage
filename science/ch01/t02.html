Formula toolkit

About 25–30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Write correct chemical formulae using valency and common ions, including polyatomic ions.
  • Count the number of atoms of each element in a formula, including formulae with brackets.
  • Recall that hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and chlorine gases are diatomic (H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂).
Why this lesson exists

Most students who "can't balance equations" actually can't write formulae. If your formula is wrong, no amount of balancing will fix the equation. This short lesson gives you the toolkit. If you already have it, prove it below and skip ahead to Topic 3.

Think of a formula as a recipe card. "HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O" says: two hydrogen atoms + one oxygen atom. Change the card to "HX2OX2\ce{H2O2}HX2​OX2​" and you get hydrogen peroxide — a bleach used to clean wounds, not something you drink. One small number changes the whole substance. That is why formulae must be exactly right.

  1. Symbols and formulae

Every element has a symbol — one capital letter, sometimes followed by one small letter: H, O, C, Na (sodium), Cl (chlorine), Fe (iron), Cu (copper), Ag (silver), Pb (lead), Zn (zinc).

A formula shows which elements are in a substance and how many atoms of each. The small number written low and to the right is a subscript. It belongs only to the symbol (or bracket) just before it.

  • COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​: 1 carbon, 2 oxygen.
  • HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​: 2 hydrogen, 1 sulphur, 4 oxygen.

  1. Valency — the "combining power"

Valency is the number of bonds an atom or ion can make — think of it as the number of "hands" it has to hold others. When two parts join, all hands must be held.

  1. Ions you must know

Many substances in this chapter are made of ions — atoms or groups of atoms carrying a charge. The size of the charge is the valency. A group of atoms that behaves as one unit with a charge is a polyatomic ion (for example, sulphate, SOX4X2−\ce{SO4^2-}SOX4​X2−).

Memory tip

names ending in -ide are usually a single element (chloride, oxide, sulphide — hydroxide is the exception). Names ending in -ate or -ite contain oxygen (sulphate, nitrate, carbonate, sulphite).

When a metal like iron or copper can have two valencies, the name tells you which: ferrous / iron(II) = 2, ferric / iron(III) = 3. So ferrous sulphate is FeSOX4\ce{FeSO4}FeSOX4​ and ferric oxide is FeX2OX3\ce{Fe2O3}FeX2​OX3​.

  1. The criss-cross method

To write the formula of an ionic compound:

  1. Write the symbols — positive ion first, negative ion second.
  2. Write each valency above its symbol.
  3. Cross them over: the valency of one becomes the subscript of the other.
  4. Simplify if both subscripts share a common factor (e.g. 2 and 2 → 1 and 1).
  5. Use a bracket around a polyatomic ion if you need more than one of it.
  6. Do not write the subscript 1.

  1. Brackets and counting atoms

A subscript outside a bracket multiplies everything inside the bracket.

Ca(OH)X2  :Ca=1,O=1×2=2,H=1×2=2\ce{Ca(OH)2}\;:\quad \text{Ca} = 1,\quad \text{O} = 1 \times 2 = 2,\quad \text{H} = 1 \times 2 = 2Ca(OH)X2​:Ca=1,O=1×2=2,H=1×2=2Swipe to see the whole equation →

A coefficient — the big number written in front of a formula — multiplies the whole formula.

3 HX2O  :H=2×3=6,O=1×3=3\ce{3H2O}\;:\quad \text{H} = 2 \times 3 = 6,\quad \text{O} = 1 \times 3 = 33HX2​O:H=2×3=6,O=1×3=3Swipe to see the whole equation →

A dot in a formula, as in FeSOX4 ⋅ 7 HX2O\ce{FeSO4.7H2O}FeSOX4​⋅7HX2​O (green crystals of ferrous sulphate), means "together with". This crystal contains seven water molecules for each FeSOX4\ce{FeSO4}FeSOX4​; these are called water of crystallisation. Count them separately and add.

  1. Elements that come in pairs (diatomic)

Some elements never exist as single atoms on their own. Their smallest stable unit is a pair, so we always write them with a subscript 2 when they appear as the element:

HX2,OX2,NX2,ClX2(also FX2 gas, BrX2 liquid, IX2 solid)\ce{H2},\quad \ce{O2},\quad \ce{N2},\quad \ce{Cl2}\qquad(\text{also } \ce{F2}\ \text{gas},\ \ce{Br2}\ \text{liquid},\ \ce{I2}\ \text{solid})HX2​,OX2​,NX2​,ClX2​(also FX2​ gas, BrX2​ liquid, IX2​ solid)Swipe to see the whole equation →

These are called diatomic ("two-atom") molecules. Metals and carbon are written as single symbols: Mg, Fe, Zn, C.

Memory tip

"Have No Fear Of Ice Cold Brew" — H, N, F, O, I, Cl, Br are the seven diatomic elements.

Worked example 1

Writing formulae by criss-cross

Question. Write the formulae of (a) magnesium oxide, (b) sodium sulphate, (c) calcium hydroxide, (d) aluminium chloride.

Given: four compound names. Asked: their formulae.

Concept used: criss-cross method — valencies swap to become subscripts; simplify; bracket polyatomic ions when more than one is needed. Why it applies: each compound is made of a positive part and a negative part whose valencies we know from the ion table.

  1. 1

    (a) Magnesium oxide

    Symbols: Mg and O.

  2. 2

    Valencies: Mg = 2, O = 2.

  3. 3

    Cross over: MgX2OX2\ce{Mg2O2}MgX2​OX2​.

  4. 4

    Simplify: 2 and 2 share a factor of 2 → divide both by 2 → MgO\ce{MgO}MgO.

  5. 1

    (b) Sodium sulphate

    Symbols: Na and SOX4\ce{SO4}SOX4​.

  6. 2

    Valencies: Na = 1, SOX4\ce{SO4}SOX4​ = 2.

  7. 3

    Cross over: Na gets 2, SOX4\ce{SO4}SOX4​ gets 1.

  8. 4

    Only one sulphate group, so no bracket is needed → NaX2SOX4\ce{Na2SO4}NaX2​SOX4​.

  9. 1

    (c) Calcium hydroxide

    Symbols: Ca and OH.

  10. 2

    Valencies: Ca = 2, OH = 1.

  11. 3

    Cross over: Ca gets 1, OH gets 2.

  12. 4

    Two hydroxide groups → bracket them → Ca(OH)X2\ce{Ca(OH)2}Ca(OH)X2​.

  13. 1

    (d) Aluminium chloride

    Symbols: Al and Cl.

  14. 2

    Valencies: Al = 3, Cl = 1.

  15. 3

    Cross over: Al gets 1, Cl gets 3 → AlClX3\ce{AlCl3}AlClX3​.

  16. Check: total positive charge = total negative charge in each. For AlClX3\ce{AlCl3}AlClX3​: +3 from Al and 3 × (−1) = −3 from chlorine. For Ca(OH)X2\ce{Ca(OH)2}Ca(OH)X2​: +2 and 2 × (−1) = −2 ✔.

  17. Answer (a) MgO\ce{MgO}MgO (b) NaX2SOX4\ce{Na2SO4}NaX2​SOX4​ (c) Ca(OH)X2\ce{Ca(OH)2}Ca(OH)X2​ (d) AlClX3\ce{AlCl3}AlClX3​

    Common trap: Writing CaOHX2\ce{CaOH2}CaOHX2​ without the bracket. That would mean 1 O and 2 H — wrong. The bracket tells you the whole OH group is doubled.

Worked example 2

Counting atoms with brackets, coefficients and dots

Question. Count the atoms of each element in (a) Ca(NOX3)X2\ce{Ca(NO3)2}Ca(NOX3​)X2​, (b) 3 AlX2(SOX4)X3\ce{3Al2(SO4)3}3AlX2​(SOX4​)X3​, (c) FeSOX4 ⋅ 7 HX2O\ce{FeSO4.7H2O}FeSOX4​⋅7HX2​O.

Given: three formulae. Asked: the number of atoms of each element.

Concept used: a subscript outside a bracket multiplies everything in the bracket; a coefficient multiplies the whole formula; a dot means "add the next part". Why it applies: each formula uses one of these three features.

  1. 1

    (a) Ca(NOX3)X2\ce{Ca(NO3)2}Ca(NOX3​)X2​

    Ca: 1.

  2. 2

    N: 1 inside the bracket × 2 = 2.

  3. 3

    O: 3 inside the bracket × 2 = 6.

  4. 1

    (b) 3 AlX2(SOX4)X3\ce{3Al2(SO4)3}3AlX2​(SOX4​)X3​

    First count one unit, AlX2(SOX4)X3\ce{Al2(SO4)3}AlX2​(SOX4​)X3​: Al = 2; S = 1 × 3 = 3; O = 4 × 3 = 12.

  5. 2

    Multiply by the coefficient 3: Al = 2 × 3 = 6; S = 3 × 3 = 9; O = 12 × 3 = 36.

  6. 1

    (c) FeSOX4 ⋅ 7 HX2O\ce{FeSO4.7H2O}FeSOX4​⋅7HX2​O

    The FeSOX4\ce{FeSO4}FeSOX4​ part: Fe = 1, S = 1, O = 4.

  7. 2

    The 7 HX2O\ce{7H2O}7HX2​O part: H = 2 × 7 = 14; O = 1 × 7 = 7.

  8. 3

    Add the oxygen from both parts: O = 4 + 7 = 11.

  9. Check: in (b) there are 4 O for every S (36 ÷ 9 = 4), matching the sulphate group SOX4\ce{SO4}SOX4​; in (a) there are 3 O for every N, matching NOX3\ce{NO3}NOX3​ ✔.

  10. Answer (a) Ca = 1, N = 2, O = 6 (b) Al = 6, S = 9, O = 36 (c) Fe = 1, S = 1, O = 11, H = 14

    Common trap: In (b), forgetting to multiply by the coefficient and reporting O = 12.

How many oxygen atoms are there in 2 Al(OH)X3\ce{2Al(OH)3}2Al(OH)X3​?

Common mistakes!

  • CaOHX2\ce{CaOH2}CaOHX2​ for calcium hydroxide

    Without a bracket, the 2 only doubles H.

    Bracket any polyatomic ion you need more than one of: Ca(OH)X2\ce{Ca(OH)2}Ca(OH)X2​.

  • Reading Ca(OH)X2\ce{Ca(OH)2}Ca(OH)X2​ as 1 O and 1 H

    Ignores the subscript outside the bracket.

    Multiply everything inside the bracket by the outside number.

  • Writing H, O or N for the gases

    These gases exist as pairs of atoms.

    Always HX2\ce{H2}HX2​, OX2\ce{O2}OX2​, NX2\ce{N2}NX2​, ClX2\ce{Cl2}ClX2​ when they are the free element.

  • FeSOX4\ce{FeSO4}FeSOX4​ for ferric sulphate

    Ferric means iron(III), valency 3.

    Ferrous = Fe(II) → FeSOX4\ce{FeSO4}FeSOX4​; ferric = Fe(III) → FeX2(SOX4)X3\ce{Fe2(SO4)3}FeX2​(SOX4​)X3​.

  • Writing MgX2OX2\ce{Mg2O2}MgX2​OX2​

    Not simplified.

    Divide both subscripts by any common factor → MgO\ce{MgO}MgO.

Key points

  • A formula is a recipe: symbols tell you which atoms, subscripts tell you how many.
  • Criss-cross: swap the valencies to become subscripts, simplify, bracket polyatomic ions.
  • A subscript outside a bracket multiplies everything inside; a coefficient in front multiplies the whole formula.
  • HX2\ce{H2}HX2​, OX2\ce{O2}OX2​, NX2\ce{N2}NX2​, ClX2\ce{Cl2}ClX2​ (and FX2\ce{F2}FX2​, BrX2\ce{Br2}BrX2​, IX2\ce{I2}IX2​) are diatomic.
  • Get the formula right first — balancing comes next.
