Topic 1 of 7

How do we know a chemical reaction has happened?

About 25 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • List the observable signs of a chemical reaction (change in state, colour, temperature, evolution of gas).
  • Use these signs to decide whether a given everyday change is a chemical reaction.

Leave a glass of milk on the kitchen counter on a hot May afternoon. By evening it smells sour and has gone lumpy. Nobody added anything to it — so what changed? And how could you prove that something new was made?

Quick reminder: physical change or chemical change?

You met these two ideas in earlier classes.

  • In a physical change, the substance stays the same substance. Only its shape, size or state changes. Ice melting into water is still water. Sugar dissolving in tea is still sugar — taste the tea and you can tell.
  • In a chemical change, one or more new substances are formed, with new properties. Burnt paper does not turn back into paper. Sour milk does not turn back into fresh milk.

A chemical reaction is simply the process in which a chemical change happens. The substances you start with are called reactants. The new substances formed are called products.

You cannot see atoms, so look for clues

Atoms are far too small to watch. So how does a scientist know a new substance has been made? They look for evidence you can observe. There are four common signs.

Sign What you notice Everyday example
1. Change in state A solid forms from two liquids, or a solid turns into a gas, etc. Milk curdles into solid lumps (paneer-making with lemon juice)
2. Change in colour The colour of the substance changes A cut apple or brinjal turns brown; a shiny iron gate turns reddish-brown
3. Evolution of a gas Bubbles or fizzing; sometimes a smell Fruit salt (antacid powder) fizzes in water; idli batter rises overnight
4. Change in temperature The mixture gets hotter or colder Wet cement or lime plaster warms up; a burning matchstick

"Evolution of a gas" is the scientist's way of saying "a gas is given off".

Three lab reactions that show these signs

Your textbook uses three short activities. Here is what each one teaches you.

Reaction 1 — burning a magnesium ribbon

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Rub a short magnesium ribbon with sandpaper, hold it with tongs, and light it in a flame. (Never look straight at the flame for long.)
See
It burns with a brilliant, dazzling white light and leaves a white powder.
Shows
A shiny grey metal has become a white powder — a change in state and colour. Heat and light are given out. The powder is a new substance, magnesium oxide, made when magnesium joins with oxygen from the air.

Reaction 2 — mixing two colourless solutions

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Pour potassium iodide solution into lead nitrate solution.
See
A bright yellow solid suddenly appears in the test tube.
Shows
Two clear liquids produced a coloured solid — a change in state and colour. The yellow solid is a new substance, lead iodide.

Reaction 3 — zinc in dilute acid

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Add dilute sulphuric acid (or hydrochloric acid) to a few zinc granules. Touch the bottom of the test tube.
See
Bubbles form on the zinc, and the tube feels warm.
Shows
A gas is given off (it is hydrogen) and there is a change in temperature.

A clue is not a proof

Be careful. These signs are clues, not guarantees.

  • Boiling water also gives off a gas (steam) — but it is still water, so it is a physical change.
  • Dissolving some salts in water makes the water colder — and you can get the salt back by evaporating the water, so it is a physical change.

The real test is always: has a new substance with new properties been formed? Use the four signs as evidence, then ask that question.

Why this matters for the rest of the chapter

Each type of reaction you will meet in Topic 4–Topic 7 begins with one of these observations — a hot beaker, a colour that fades, a yellow solid, bubbles at a wire. Learning to notice is the first skill of chemistry.

Did you know?Not in board exam

The black "seasoning" on a well-used iron kadhai is a thin layer formed when oil is heated on the iron again and again. It is a chemical change — and it actually helps protect the pan from rusting.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Is it a chemical reaction?

Question. Decide whether each change is a chemical reaction. Give the sign(s) that support your answer.
(a) Milk turns sour and lumpy when left out in summer.
(b) Ghee melts in a warm kitchen.

Given: two everyday changes. Asked: chemical reaction or not, with evidence.

Concept used: A chemical reaction makes a new substance. The four signs (state, colour, gas, temperature) are clues that this has happened. Why it applies: both changes show at least one sign, so we must decide whether a new substance really formed.

  1. 1

    (a) Sour milk

    Look for signs: the milk becomes lumpy (solid lumps form from a liquid) → change in state.

  2. 2

    Look for more signs: the smell and taste change to sour → a property has changed.

  3. 3

    Ask the key question: can you turn the sour milk back into fresh milk by any simple method? No.

  4. 4

    So a new substance has been formed. (Bacteria turn the milk sugar into an acid.)

  5. Answer Chemical reaction — change in state and new smell/taste; the change cannot be reversed.

  6. 1

    (b) Melting ghee

    Look for signs: solid ghee becomes liquid → a change in state.

  7. 2

    Ask the key question: is it a new substance? Cool it and it becomes solid ghee again, with the same smell and taste.

  8. 3

    So no new substance has been formed.

  9. Answer Not a chemical reaction — it is a physical change, even though the state changed.

  10. Check: In (a) a new property (sourness) appeared; in (b) every property returned on cooling. That fits our definitions.

    Common trap: "Change in state" alone does not prove a reaction. Melting, freezing and boiling change state but make nothing new.

Worked example 2

Reading a lab observation

Question. A student drops a few zinc granules into dilute hydrochloric acid. She sees bubbles and finds the test tube becomes warm. Which signs of a chemical reaction did she observe? What else could she do to be sure that a new substance has formed?

Given: bubbles + warmth when zinc meets dilute acid. Asked: signs observed; a further check.

Concept used: The four signs of a chemical reaction. Why it applies: the question lists what the student saw and felt, and asks us to match each observation to a sign.

  1. 1

    Bubbles mean a gas is being given off → evolution of a gas.

  2. 2

    The tube becomes warm → change in temperature.

  3. 3

    To be sure the gas is a new substance, test it. Bring a burning splint near the mouth of the tube.

  4. 4

    If the gas burns with a "pop" sound, it is hydrogen — a substance that was not there at the start.

  5. Answer Two signs: evolution of a gas and change in temperature. Testing the gas (the "pop" test for hydrogen) confirms a new substance.

  6. Check: Zinc is a solid and the acid is a liquid; neither is hydrogen gas. So the gas must be a product.

    Common trap: Writing "the acid boiled" — the bubbles are hydrogen gas, not steam. The tube is only warm, not boiling.

Quick check · 1 of 3MCQ1 mark

Which of these is not one of the four common signs of a chemical reaction?

Colour change is a sign — think of a cut apple turning brown.
Gas given off is a sign — think of fruit salt (antacid powder) fizzing.
In a closed system, total mass stays the same in every chemical reaction (law of conservation of mass), so a change in mass is not a sign.
Temperature change is a sign — think of wet lime getting hot.
Quick check · 2 of 3True/False with justification1 mark

True or false? "Whenever bubbles appear, a chemical reaction is taking place." Justify.

Answer: False. Boiling water bubbles because liquid water turns into steam — still water, so no new substance. Bubbles are a clue; you must check that a new substance has formed.

Quick check · 3 of 3Short answer2 marks

When lemon juice is squeezed into hot milk, solid lumps of paneer separate out. Is this a physical or chemical change? Give one sign as evidence.

Answer: Chemical change. Evidence: a change in state — solid lumps form from a liquid — and the lumps cannot be turned back into milk. (The acid in lemon juice changes the milk proteins.)

If you said "physical because nothing was heated", remember: what matters is whether a new substance forms, not whether heat is used.

Common mistakes!

  • "Melting / boiling is a chemical reaction because the state changed."

    No new substance forms; cooling brings back the original.

    Ask: is there a new substance with new properties?

  • "Mass decreases when magnesium burns."

    Mass is never lost. The magnesium oxide is actually heavier than the ribbon because oxygen from the air has joined it.

    Remember the law of conservation of mass (reminder in Topic 3).

  • "The bubbles on zinc are the acid boiling."

    The tube is only warm. The gas is hydrogen, a new substance.

    Name the gas; test it with a burning splint.

Key points

  • A chemical reaction makes new substances (products) from starting substances (reactants).
  • Four signs: change in state, change in colour, evolution of a gas, change in temperature.
  • The signs are clues; confirm that a new substance has formed.
  • Burning magnesium gives a dazzling white flame and white magnesium oxide; lead nitrate + potassium iodide gives a yellow solid; zinc + dilute acid gives hydrogen bubbles and warmth.
