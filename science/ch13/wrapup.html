Wrap-up

Follow the food and the energy

About 25 minutes · 7 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Write food chains with arrows from the food to the eater.
  • Name the trophic levels and consumer types in a chain.
  • Use the 10 per cent law forwards and backwards.
  • Explain one-way, diminishing energy flow.
  • Explain biological magnification.
  • Explain the cause of ozone depletion and the steps taken.
  • Suggest ways of dealing with waste.

Almost every question in this chapter asks you to follow something along a food chain — food, energy or a harmful chemical — or to link an environmental problem to its cause and its solution. This page puts the chapter on three cards and then works through four mixed questions.

Card 1 — Food chains

Ask yourself… Rule
Which way does the arrow point? From the food to the eater ("is eaten by")
What comes first? A producer (green plant or algae)
What is the level number? Count from the producer = 1
Primary, secondary or tertiary consumer? Level 2, 3 or 4 (name = level − 1)
Are the Sun and decomposers levels? No

Card 2 — Energy and pesticides

Going up the chain Energy Non-degradable pesticide
Each step ÷ 10 (only 10 % passes on) accumulates — concentration rises
Greatest at producers top level (humans)
Direction one-way; never returns builds up; not broken down

Card 3 — Environmental problems

Problem Cause and effect What is done
Ozone depletion CFCs → more UV reaches the earth (skin cancer) 1987 UNEP agreement; CFC-free refrigerators
Non-biodegradable waste plastics, packaging, disposables → persist; harm organisms; enter food chains reduce, reuse, recycle; cloth bags
Biodegradable waste in heaps too much, mixed and dumped → smell, flies, disease separate and compost

Mixed example 1

One chain, three questions

Question. In a marsh: aquatic plants → aquatic insects → frog → snake. (a) Name the trophic level of the frog and its consumer type. (b) The snakes receive 6 kJ of energy. How much energy did the aquatic plants trap? (c) A non-degradable pesticide reaches the marsh. Which organism will contain it in the highest concentration?

Given: a four-level chain; snakes (level 4) = 6 kJ; a non-degradable pesticide. Asked: the frog's level and type; the producers' energy; the organism with most pesticide.

Concept used: trophic levels (Card 1); the 10 per cent law backwards (Card 2); biological magnification (Card 2). Why it applies: the same chain answers all three parts.

  1. 1

    (a) Count from the producers: aquatic plants 1, aquatic insects 2, frog 3 — a secondary consumer.

  2. 2

    (b) Steps from level 4 down to level 1: 3 steps, so multiply by 10 three times:

    6×10×10×10=6000 kJ6 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 6000\ \text{kJ}6×10×10×10=6000 kJSwipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    (c) Top of the chain: the snake (fourth level) — the pesticide accumulates at each level.

  4. Check: forward again: 6000÷10=6006000 \div 10 = 6006000÷10=600, 600÷10=60600 \div 10 = 60600÷10=60, 60÷10=660 \div 10 = 660÷10=6 kJ ✔; and the snake, with the least energy, has the most pesticide — the two trends go opposite ways, as Card 2 says.

  5. Answer (a) Third trophic level; secondary consumer. (b) 6000 kJ6000\ \text{kJ}6000 kJ (c) The snake.

    Common trap: in (b), multiplying four times because there are four organisms. Four organisms make three steps.

Mixed example 2

Does energy come back?

Question. Arjun says: "Leaves fall from the trees of a forest, decomposers break them down, and the trees take up what is released. So the energy of the leaves goes back to the trees." Is he right? Explain.

Given: Arjun's claim about fallen leaves. Asked: is energy returned to the trees?

Concept used: decomposers return substances to the soil (Topic 1); energy flow is unidirectional (Topic 3).

  1. 1

    What the decomposers do: they break the leaves down into simple inorganic substances that go into the soil and are used again by the trees — so materials are recycled.

  2. 2

    What happens to the energy: the decomposers use the energy in the leaves for their own life activities, and it is lost as heat. The inorganic substances taken up by the roots carry no food energy.

  3. 3

    Where the trees' energy comes from: only from sunlight, which they capture by photosynthesis.

  4. Check: if energy really came back, a forest could keep going without sunlight — but no ecosystem can; every food chain starts with producers capturing sunlight ✔.

  5. Answer Arjun is only half right. The materials in the leaves are recycled to the trees through the decomposers, but the energy is not — it is used and lost as heat. Energy flow is unidirectional; the trees get new energy only from sunlight.

    Common trap: treating materials and energy alike. Materials go round; energy goes one way.

Mixed example 3

An old refrigerator

Question. A shop wants to throw its old refrigerator, which uses a CFC refrigerant, onto an open garbage dump. Give two reasons why this is harmful, and say what should be done instead.

Given: an old CFC refrigerator dumped in the open. Asked: two harms; a better way.

Concept used: CFCs and ozone depletion (Topic 5); non-biodegradable waste and electronic items (Topic 6).

  1. 1

    Harm 1 — ozone: the refrigerant can escape; CFCs are linked to the depletion of the ozone layer, which shields the earth from UV radiation.

  2. 2

    Harm 2 — waste: the refrigerator's metal, plastic and other parts are non-biodegradable and some materials in electrical goods are hazardous; on a dump they persist and can pollute the soil and water.

  3. 3

    Better way (a sensible step, beyond your textbook): hand it to an authorised collector or the company for safe disposal and recycling, so that the refrigerant is removed safely and the materials are recycled; buy a CFC-free replacement.

  4. Check: each harm names its cause and its effect on the environment (CFC → less ozone → more UV; persistent parts → pollution), and the solution removes both causes ✔.

  5. Answer (1) Escaping CFCs deplete the ozone layer, letting more harmful UV reach the earth. (2) Its parts are non-biodegradable and some are hazardous, so they persist and pollute. Instead, send it for safe disposal and recycling, and replace it with a CFC-free refrigerator.

    Common trap: "the refrigerator is harmless because it is just metal". The refrigerant inside, and the hazardous materials in its parts, are the main concerns.

Mixed example 4

From sunlight to carnivores

Question. Sunlight with 2 900 000 J of energy falls on the leaves of the grass in a meadow. The food chain is grass → rabbit → fox. Find the energy reaching (a) the grass, (b) the rabbits, (c) the foxes.
