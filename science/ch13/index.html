Chapter 13 · Unit V

Our Environment

6 topicsAbout 7–8 hoursBoard exam
Why this chapter matters

A pond near your school, a field of wheat and even a fish tank in a shop are all little worlds in which living things depend on each other and on sunlight, water and soil. A frog that eats grasshoppers, a snake that eats the frog — each meal passes on only a small part of the energy the Sun gave the grass. Chemicals sprayed on crops travel up the same chains and collect in the animals at the top, including us. High above, a thin layer of ozone shields all life from harmful ultraviolet rays, and at home the waste we throw away either rots back into the soil or stays for years. This chapter explains all of it.

In this chapter you will learn what an ecosystem is and who its producers, consumers and decomposers are, how to write food chains and food webs and name trophic levels, how energy flows through an ecosystem (the 10 per cent law), why harmful chemicals pile up at the top of a food chain (biological magnification), how the ozone layer protects us and why it got thinner, and how to deal with biodegradable and non-biodegradable waste. It is the whole of Unit V, Natural Resources (5 marks). In the CBSE sample papers it supplies a 1-mark question on a fish's trophic level, a 1-mark assertion–reason on energy transfer and a 3-mark question on a pond food chain (2026-27), and two 1-mark questions, an assertion–reason and a 2-mark food web (2025-26).

What you'll learn

  • Define an ecosystem, tell its living and non-living parts apart, and describe producers, consumers and decomposers.
  • Write food chains with arrows the right way round, name trophic levels, and explain food webs.
  • Find the energy at any trophic level, and explain why energy flows one way and food chains are short.
  • Explain how pesticides enter food chains and why they are most concentrated at the top.
  • Explain how ozone forms, how it protects us, and why it has been depleted.
  • Tell biodegradable from non-biodegradable waste, explain their effects, and suggest ways of dealing with waste.
  • Follow the food and the energy through any ecosystem, and link each environmental problem to its cause and solution.

Most of the marks in this chapter come from four skills, and every topic practises them:

  1. Write food chains the right way round — each arrow points from the food to the eater ("is eaten by"), and the chain starts with a producer.
  2. Use the 10 per cent law — number the levels from the producers, then divide by 10 for each step up (or multiply by 10 for each step down).
  3. Explain causes and effects — what happens when a level is removed, why pesticides collect at the top, why CFCs are harmful.
  4. Sort and handle waste — biodegradable or not, its effects, and what we can do about it.
