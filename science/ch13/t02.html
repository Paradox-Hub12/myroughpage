Topic 2 of 6

Food chains, trophic levels and food webs

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Define a food chain and a trophic level, and write a food chain with arrows from the food to the eater.
  • Name the trophic levels and consumer types in a given chain (Figs 13.1–13.2).
  • Explain a food web (Fig 13.3), and predict the effect of removing an organism or a whole trophic level.

In a rice field, grasshoppers chew the leaves, frogs snap up the grasshoppers, snakes swallow the frogs and an eagle swoops down on a snake. If you could follow one mouthful of rice leaf, where would it end up? Biologists answer that question with a simple line of names and arrows — a food chain — and the arrows must point the right way.

Quick reminder (Topic 1)

Producers make food; consumers eat producers or other consumers; decomposers break down dead remains.

  1. What is a food chain?

In Activity 13.2 you wrote the organisms of your aquarium in order of who eats whom. (Your textbook calls it "Activity 13.4" at this point; it means Activity 13.2.)

A food chain

is a series of organisms feeding on one another: each organism eats the one before it and is eaten by the one after it.

How to write one — the arrow rule. In a food chain, the arrow means "is eaten by". It points from the food to the eater, which is also the way the energy goes.

grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle
Read it as: grass is eaten by the grasshopper, which is eaten by the frog, which is eaten by the snake, which is eaten by the eagle.

A food chain always starts with a producer (a green plant, or algae in water), because only producers can make food from sunlight. The Sun gives the energy but is not a member of the chain.

In this chapter, an arrow drawn between two organisms always means "is eaten by".

  1. Food chains in nature (Fig 13.1)

Your textbook shows three food chains:

  • (a) In a forest: trees → deer → tiger
  • (b) In a grassland: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle (the textbook's picture shows a large bird of prey at the top)
  • (c) In a pond: aquatic plants → aquatic insects → fish → heron (a long-legged water bird)

  1. Trophic levels

Each step or level of the food chain forms a trophic level.

Number the levels starting from the producers (Fig 13.2):

Trophic level Who is there Also called
First autotrophs (green plants) producers
Second herbivores primary consumers
Third small carnivores secondary consumers
Fourth larger carnivores tertiary consumers

The producers fix up the solar energy and make it available for the heterotrophs (consumers).

Notice the trap in the names: the primary consumer is at the second level, the secondary consumer at the third, the tertiary consumer at the fourth — the consumer's name is always one less than its level number.

Decomposers are not given a trophic level in a food chain: they break down the dead remains and wastes of every level.

  1. Food webs

In nature a chain of single organisms is rare. Each organism is generally eaten by two or more other kinds of organisms, which in turn are eaten by several other organisms. So instead of a straight-line food chain, the feeding relationships are shown as a series of branching lines, called a food web (Fig 13.3).

A food web

is a network of many food chains joined together, because most organisms eat — and are eaten by — more than one kind of organism.

Food chain Food web
one straight line of organisms many chains linked together, with branching lines
each member eats one kind of organism of the level below a member can feed on several kinds of organisms, from other chains too

  1. Removing an organism or a whole level

Because the members of an ecosystem depend on each other, removing one kind of organism changes the numbers of others, both above it (they lose food) and below it (they are no longer eaten).

  • In a single chain grass → deer → tiger, if all the deer disappear, the tigers lose their food and decrease, and the grass, no longer eaten, increases.
  • In a web, an animal that loses one food may eat more of another, so the effect spreads to other chains too.
  • Removing all the producers would leave every other level without food — the whole ecosystem would collapse.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Putting a jumbled chain in order

Question. These organisms live near a lake: snake, water plants, eagle, frog, aquatic insects. (a) Arrange them in a food chain. (b) Name the trophic level of the frog and of the eagle.

Given: five organisms living near a lake. Asked: the food chain; the trophic levels of the frog and the eagle.

Concept used: a food chain starts with a producer and each arrow points from the food to the eater; levels are numbered from the producer (this topic). Why it applies: exactly one of the five is a producer.

  1. 1

    Find the producer — water plants (green plants make food).

  2. 2

    Who eats water plants? The aquatic insects. Who eats aquatic insects? The frog. Who eats frogs? The snake. Who eats snakes? The eagle.

  3. 3

    Write the chain: water plants → aquatic insects → frog → snake → eagle.

  4. 4

    (b) Count from the producer: water plants 1st, aquatic insects 2nd, frog 3rd, snake 4th, eagle 5th.

  5. Check: read each arrow as "is eaten by": water plants are eaten by the aquatic insects ✔, the insects by the frog ✔, the frog by the snake ✔, the snake by the eagle ✔. Had one arrow been reversed ("the frog is eaten by the insects"), the sentence would be plainly false.

  6. Answer (a) water plants → aquatic insects → frog → snake → eagle. (b) The frog is at the third trophic level (a secondary consumer); the eagle is at the fifth trophic level.

    Common trap: writing eagle → snake → frog → aquatic insects → water plants ("the eagle eats the snake"). The arrow shows where the food goes, so it points to the eater.

Worked example 2

Naming levels in a pond

Question. In a pond, algae are eaten by water fleas, water fleas by small fish, and small fish by a kingfisher. (a) Write the food chain. (b) For each organism, give its trophic level and say whether it is a producer, primary, secondary or tertiary consumer.

Given: four organisms and who eats whom. Asked: the chain; the level and the name of each member.

Concept used: producers are at the first level, primary consumers at the second, secondary at the third, tertiary at the fourth (this topic; Fig 13.2).

  1. 1

    (a) Chain: algae → water fleas → small fish → kingfisher.

  2. 2

    (b) Algae — 1st level — producers (they photosynthesise).

  3. 3

    Water fleas — 2nd level — primary consumers (they eat the producers).

  4. 4

    Small fish — 3rd level — secondary consumers.

  5. 5

    Kingfisher — 4th level — tertiary consumer.

  6. Check: each consumer name is one less than its level number — primary at 2, secondary at 3, tertiary at 4 ✔.

  7. Answer (a) algae → water fleas → small fish → kingfisher. (b) Algae: first level, producers; water fleas: second level, primary consumers; small fish: third level, secondary consumers; kingfisher: fourth level, tertiary consumer.

    Common trap: "the small fish is at the second level because it is a secondary consumer". The secondary consumer sits at the third level.

Worked example 3

When the frogs disappear

Question. A small grassland food web contains these chains:
grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk
grass → rabbit → snake
grass → grasshopper → sparrow → hawk
A disease kills all the frogs. Predict what happens (a) to the grasshoppers, (b) to the snakes, (c) to the rabbits.

Given: a food web of three chains; the frogs disappear. Asked: effects on grasshoppers, snakes and rabbits.

Concept used: removing an organism affects the organisms that ate it and the organisms it ate; in a web, eaters can switch to other foods (this topic). Why it applies: the frog is food for snakes and an eater of grasshoppers.

  1. 1

    (a) Grasshoppers — fewer of them are eaten (no frogs), so their numbers increase, at least at first; they then eat more grass. (Sparrows still eat some of them.)

  2. 2

    (b) Snakes — they lose one food, the frogs. In this web they can still eat rabbits, so they survive, but they depend more on rabbits.

  3. 3

    (c) Rabbits — the snakes now eat more rabbits, so the rabbits decrease.

  4. Check: follow the arrows into and out of the frog: the arrow into it comes from the grasshopper (its food — now less eaten), the arrow out of it goes to the snake (its eater — now short of food). Every effect above comes from one of these two arrows or from the snake's other food ✔.

  5. Answer (a) The grasshoppers increase (fewer are eaten). (b) The snakes lose the frogs as food, so they eat more rabbits. (c) The rabbits therefore decrease.

    Common trap: "only the snakes are affected, because only snakes eat frogs". Removing one organism affects the level below it too (the grasshoppers), and in a web the effect spreads to other chains (the rabbits).

Did you get it?

Common mistakes!

  • Drawing arrows from the eater to the food (tiger → deer ✘).

    The arrow shows where the food and its energy go.

    Arrow = "is eaten by"; it points to the eater. Check by reading each arrow aloud.

  • Starting a chain with the Sun or with an animal; giving the Sun or the decomposers a trophic level.

    The first trophic level is the producers; the Sun only supplies energy, and decomposers act on every level.

    Start with a green plant (or algae); number it as level 1.

  • "The secondary consumer is at the second level."

    Producers take the first level, so every consumer is one level higher than its name.

    Primary → 2nd, secondary → 3rd, tertiary → 4th.

  • "Removing one level affects only the level above it."

    The level below is no longer eaten, so it changes too; in a web the effect spreads.

    Follow the arrows into and out of the removed organism.

  • Calling a food web "a long food chain".

    A web is many chains joined, with branches.

    Chain = one line; web = branching network of many chains.

Key points

  • A food chain is a series of organisms feeding on one another. Each arrow points from the food to the eater ("is eaten by"); a chain starts with a producer.
  • Each step of a food chain is a trophic level: producers (1st), herbivores = primary consumers (2nd), small carnivores = secondary consumers (3rd), larger carnivores = tertiary consumers (4th).
  • Examples (Fig 13.1): trees → deer → tiger (forest); grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle (grassland); aquatic plants → aquatic insects → fish → heron (pond).
  • Each organism is generally eaten by two or more kinds of organisms, so feeding relationships form a food web — branching lines of many food chains.
  • Removing one kind of organism affects the levels above it (less food) and below it (less eaten); removing the producers would starve every other level.
