Topic 3 of 6

Flow of energy and the 10 per cent law

About 45 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe how energy enters an ecosystem (about 1 % of the sunlight falling on leaves) and what happens to it at each step.
  • Use the 10 per cent law to find the energy at any trophic level, forwards and backwards.
  • Explain why energy flow is unidirectional and diminishing (Fig 13.4), why food chains have only three or four steps, and why lower levels have more individuals.

A cow eats grass all day long, yet only a small part of what it eats ends up as the cow's own body — most of the energy in the grass is used up in living, moving, keeping warm and digesting. A tiger, eating deer, faces the same loss again. That is why a forest has thousands of trees, hundreds of deer and only a few tigers. This topic shows you how to put numbers on that loss.

Quick reminder (percentages)

10 % of a number = the number ÷ 10; 1 % of a number = the number ÷ 100. For example, 10 % of 4800 is 480, and 1 % of 4800 is 48.

Quick reminder (energy changes form). Whenever energy changes from one form to another, some of it is lost to the surroundings in forms that cannot be used again — mostly as heat. (Your textbook refers here to a chapter on "Sources of Energy", which is not in the 2026-27 book; this one idea is all you need.)

  1. Where the energy comes from

Food is fuel: it gives every organism the energy it needs to live and work. In an ecosystem:

  • the autotrophs (green plants) trap the energy of sunlight and store it as chemical energy in the food they make;
  • every activity of every living thing in the ecosystem runs on this stored energy;
  • it passes from the autotrophs to the heterotrophs (consumers) and to the decomposers.

  1. What scientists have found about energy flow

Your textbook lists these findings, in short:

  1. On land, green plants turn only about 1 % of the sunlight that falls on their leaves into food energy.
  2. When a herbivore eats plants, much of the energy is lost as heat; some is used for digestion and for doing work; what is left goes into growth and reproduction.
  3. On average, only 10 % of the food an animal eats becomes part of its own body — and only that part can be eaten by the next level.
  4. So, as an average, 10 % of the organic matter at each step reaches the next level of consumers. This is often called the 10 per cent law.
The 10 per cent law (in one line)

on average, only 10 % of the energy at one trophic level reaches the next trophic level; the other 90 % is not passed on (most of it is lost as heat).

  1. Using the 10 per cent law — the routine

  1. Number the levels from the producers: producers = level 1.
  2. Count the steps between the level you know and the level you want.
  3. Going up one step: divide by 10. Going down one step (towards the producers): multiply by 10.
  4. Check by going the other way.

Here a "step" is one arrow. Your textbook also says food chains have "three or four steps", meaning three or four levels.

Sunlight is not a trophic level: from the sunlight falling on the leaves to the producers, use 1 % (divide by 100), not 10 %.

  1. Two things the energy-flow diagram makes clear (Fig 13.4)

Sunlight → Producers → Herbivores → Carnivores → Top carnivores

  1. The flow of energy is unidirectional (one-way). Energy trapped by the plants never returns to the Sun, and energy eaten by the herbivores never returns to the plants. Once it has moved up a level, the level below cannot get it back.
  2. Each level has less energy than the one below it, because some is lost at every step.

Materials are different: the decomposers return substances to the soil to be used again (Topic 1), but energy does not come back — it flows one way and is lost as heat on the way.

  1. Why food chains are short, and why the bottom is crowded

  • Most food chains have only three or four steps. Each step passes on so little that, after four levels, hardly any usable energy is left — too little to feed another level of consumers.
  • Lower levels usually have more individuals than higher ones, and the producers are the most numerous of all — the lower levels have the most energy to support living things.
  • Food chains differ a great deal in length and complexity from one ecosystem to another.

Did you get it?

Common mistakes!

  • "10 % is lost and 90 % is passed on."

    Only about 10 % of the energy reaches the next level; about 90 % is not passed on.

    Going up one level: ÷ 10.

  • Using 10 % for the step from sunlight to the plants.

    Plants capture only about 1 % of the sunlight falling on their leaves.

    Sunlight → producers: ÷ 100; then ÷ 10 per trophic level.

  • Dividing when working back down a chain, or counting organisms instead of steps.

    Lower levels have more energy; the number of steps is one less than the number of organisms.

    Down one level: × 10; count the arrows.

  • "Energy goes back to the plants through the decomposers."

    Decomposers return substances (minerals) to the soil; energy is lost as heat and never returns.

    Energy: one-way flow. Materials: recycled.

  • "Food chains are short because there is not enough food at the top."

    The real limit is energy: after four levels very little usable energy remains.

    Write: the loss of energy at each step limits the number of trophic levels.

Key points

  • Green plants capture about 1 % of the energy of the sunlight falling on their leaves and convert it into chemical energy in food.
  • At each step, a great deal of energy is lost as heat, some is used in digestion and work, and only about 10 % is passed to the next level (10 per cent law).
  • Up one level: ÷ 10. Down one level: × 10. Sunlight to producers: ÷ 100. Count the steps, not the organisms.
  • Energy flow is unidirectional — it never comes back to an earlier level — and diminishes at each level (Fig 13.4).
  • So little energy remains after four levels that food chains generally have only three or four steps; the lower levels have more individuals, the producers the most.
