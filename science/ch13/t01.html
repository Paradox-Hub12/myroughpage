Topic 1 of 6

The ecosystem and its components

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Define an ecosystem; name its biotic and abiotic components with examples; tell natural ecosystems from human-made ones.
  • Describe producers, consumers (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, parasites) and decomposers by how they obtain food, with examples.
  • Explain the role of decomposers and how the members of an ecosystem depend on each other (Activities 13.1–13.2), including why an aquarium needs cleaning but a pond does not.

A fish tank in a shop window has fish, a few water plants, gravel, a pump and a light — and every few weeks someone has to clean it. A village pond has fish, plants, mud and sunshine too, but nobody ever scrubs it. What does the pond have that the tank lacks? To answer that, you need to see the pond and the tank as ecosystems.

Quick reminder (Chapter 5)

Autotrophs (green plants) make their own food from simple inorganic substances — carbon dioxide and water — using sunlight and chlorophyll (photosynthesis). Heterotrophs (animals, fungi, most bacteria) cannot make their own food; they depend, directly or indirectly, on food made by autotrophs.

  1. What is an ecosystem?

Plants, animals, microorganisms and human beings all interact with each other and with their physical surroundings, and together they keep a balance in nature.

An ecosystem

is formed by all the interacting organisms in an area together with the non-living constituents of the environment.

So every ecosystem has two kinds of components:

Component What it is Examples
Biotic the living organisms plants, animals, bacteria, fungi
Abiotic the physical (non-living) factors temperature, rainfall, wind, soil, minerals

The growth, reproduction and other activities of the living organisms are affected by the abiotic components. A garden is an ecosystem: grasses, trees, roses, jasmine and sunflowers live there with frogs, insects and birds, and all of them depend on the sunlight, water, soil and temperature of the garden.

  1. Natural and human-made ecosystems

  • Natural ecosystems: forests, ponds and lakes — they formed on their own.
  • Human-made (artificial) ecosystems: gardens and crop-fields — people plant them, water them and decide what grows there. An aquarium is also a human-made ecosystem.

  1. Designing an aquarium (Activity 13.1)

Do
Plan an aquarium. The fish need space to swim (a large jar will do), water, oxygen (an aerator or oxygen pump) and fish food. Then add a few aquatic plants and small aquatic animals.
See
with plants and animals added, the aquarium can keep itself going for some time — the plants make food and give out oxygen, the animals feed. But it still has to be cleaned once in a while: waste and dead remains build up in the small volume of water.
Shows
the aquarium is a human-made ecosystem, small and incomplete. A pond or lake is a natural ecosystem: it is large, and its decomposers break down wastes and dead remains, so it keeps itself clean and does not need to be cleaned in the same way.

  1. Producers, consumers and decomposers

Organisms are grouped by how they obtain their food from the environment.

Producers. All green plants and certain bacteria that can make organic compounds such as sugar and starch from inorganic substances, using the radiant energy of the Sun in the presence of chlorophyll (photosynthesis), are called producers.

Consumers. Organisms which consume the food produced, either directly from producers or indirectly by feeding on other consumers, are consumers. They are of four kinds:

Kind Eats Examples
Herbivores plants only cow, deer, goat, grasshopper
Carnivores other animals tiger, lion, snake, eagle
Omnivores both plants and animals human beings, crow, bear
Parasites food from another living organism (the host), on or inside which they live lice, ticks, tapeworm, Cuscuta (a plant parasite)

Decomposers. Microorganisms — bacteria and fungi — that break down the dead remains and waste products of organisms are the decomposers. They break down complex organic substances into simple inorganic substances, which go into the soil and are used up once more by the plants.

  1. Why decomposers matter (and how everyone depends on everyone)

Imagine an aquarium that is never cleaned, in which some fish and plants have died. Without decomposers:

  • garbage, dead animals and dead plants would pile up and not rot away;
  • the substances locked in them would not return to the soil, so the natural replenishment of the soil would not take place and plants would eventually run short of minerals.

So decomposers clean up the ecosystem and recycle its materials.

Interdependence (Activity 13.2).

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
In groups, discuss how producers, consumers and decomposers depend on each other; write the aquatic organisms of your aquarium in order of who eats whom, as a chain of at least three steps (Topic 2 shows how); and ask what would happen if you put in an animal that eats the others.
See
a fish-eating animal in a small tank would soon eat the other fish. Consumers need producers for food; producers need the minerals that decomposers return to the soil (or water); decomposers need the dead remains and wastes of both.
Shows
the components of an ecosystem are interdependent. Producers come closest to being "of primary importance", because all other organisms depend on the food they make — but without decomposers the materials would not be recycled, so every group is needed.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

The school pond

Question. Here is a list of things found in a school pond: water lilies, sunlight, frogs, mud, small fish, temperature of the water, bacteria in the mud, minerals dissolved in the water, algae, rainfall. Sort them into biotic and abiotic components.

Given: ten things in a pond. Asked: which are biotic and which abiotic.

Concept used: biotic = living organisms; abiotic = physical factors such as temperature, rainfall, wind, soil and minerals (this topic). Why it applies: every part of the pond ecosystem is one or the other.

  1. 1

    Ask of each item: is it alive? Water lilies, frogs, small fish, bacteria and algae are living organisms.

  2. 2

    The rest: sunlight, mud (soil), temperature, dissolved minerals and rainfall are physical, non-living factors.

  3. Check: 5 biotic + 5 abiotic = 10 items, so none is left out; and only the abiotic list contains things that never grow or reproduce ✔.

  4. Answer Biotic: water lilies, frogs, small fish, bacteria, algae. Abiotic: sunlight, mud, temperature of the water, dissolved minerals, rainfall.

    Common trap: putting bacteria under abiotic because they are too small to see. They are living organisms — the pond's decomposers.

Worked example 2

Who is who on the farm?

Question. On a farm: mustard plants grow in a field; a cow grazes on grass; ticks cling to the cow's skin and suck its blood; the farmer eats roti, dal and eggs; a kite swoops down and carries off a rat; mushrooms grow on a rotting log. Name one producer, herbivore, carnivore, omnivore, parasite and decomposer.

Given: a farm scene. Asked: one organism of each kind.

Concept used: producers make food by photosynthesis; consumers are herbivores, carnivores, omnivores or parasites according to what they eat; decomposers break down dead remains (this topic). Why it applies: each organism's food tells us its group.

  1. 1

    Producer (makes its own food with sunlight): the mustard plants (also the grass).

  2. 2

    Herbivore (eats only plants): the cow.

  3. 3

    Carnivore (eats other animals): the kite, which eats the rat.

  4. 4

    Omnivore (eats plant and animal food): the farmer — roti and dal from plants, eggs from animals.

  5. 5

    Parasite (lives on another organism and feeds on it): the ticks on the cow.

  6. 6

    Decomposer (breaks down dead matter): the mushrooms (fungi) on the rotting log.

  7. Check: each organism's food matches its group's definition — for example, the ticks feed on a living cow, which is what makes them parasites rather than carnivores ✔.

  8. Answer Producer — mustard plants; herbivore — cow; carnivore — kite; omnivore — farmer; parasite — ticks; decomposer — mushrooms.

    Common trap: calling the mushrooms producers because they "grow like plants". Mushrooms are fungi — they have no chlorophyll and get their food by breaking down dead matter.

Worked example 3

A sealed jar with no decomposers

Question. Reena sets up a sealed glass jar with soil, small plants and a few earthworms, using soil that has been heated so that it has no bacteria or fungi. After some weeks, dead leaves and the bodies of earthworms lie on the soil unchanged, and the plants look pale and weak. Explain both observations.

Given: a closed jar ecosystem without decomposers. Asked: why dead matter does not rot; why the plants weaken.

Concept used: decomposers break down dead remains into simple inorganic substances that return to the soil and are used again by plants (this topic). Why it applies: the jar has no decomposers.

  1. 1

    Dead matter unchanged → rotting is the work of bacteria and fungi; with none in the soil, the dead leaves and bodies are not broken down.

  2. 2

    Minerals locked up → the substances in the dead matter are not returned to the soil.

  3. 3

    Plants weaken → the plants keep taking minerals from the soil, but nothing replenishes it, so they run short.

  4. Check: compare a pond, which has decomposers in its mud — dead leaves there do rot away, and its plants keep growing year after year ✔.

  5. Answer With no decomposers, the dead leaves and earthworms are not broken down, so they stay unchanged; the materials in them do not go back into the soil, so the soil is not replenished and the plants run short of minerals and weaken.

    Common trap: "the earthworms will decompose the dead matter". Your textbook names bacteria and fungi as the decomposers that break dead matter down into simple inorganic substances.

Common mistakes!

  • "Decomposers break down only dead plants."

    Bacteria and fungi break down the dead remains and wastes of all organisms — plants and animals.

    Decomposers: all dead remains and wastes → simple inorganic substances → soil.

  • "Decomposers are not really needed; dead things just disappear."

    Dead matter disappears because decomposers break it down; without them it piles up and the soil is not replenished.

    Name both jobs: clean up dead matter and recycle materials to the soil.

  • Calling mushrooms or other fungi producers.

    Fungi have no chlorophyll and cannot make food from sunlight.

    Producers = green plants and certain bacteria that photosynthesise.

  • Calling an aquarium or a crop-field a natural ecosystem.

    People set them up and look after them.

    Natural: forest, pond, lake. Human-made: garden, crop-field, aquarium.

Key points

  • An ecosystem = all the interacting organisms of an area + the non-living constituents of its environment.
  • Biotic components are the living organisms; abiotic components are physical factors such as temperature, rainfall, wind, soil and minerals.
  • Natural ecosystems: forests, ponds, lakes. Human-made (artificial): gardens, crop-fields, aquariums.
  • Producers (green plants, certain bacteria) make organic food from inorganic substances using sunlight and chlorophyll. Consumers — herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, parasites — depend on producers directly or indirectly.
  • Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down dead remains and wastes into simple inorganic substances that return to the soil and are used again by plants. All the components of an ecosystem are interdependent.
