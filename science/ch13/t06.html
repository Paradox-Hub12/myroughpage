Managing the garbage we produce

About 45 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Define biodegradable and non-biodegradable substances, give examples, and explain the difference (specific enzymes; Activity 13.5).
  • Explain how each kind of waste affects the environment, and how lifestyle, disposable items and packaging add to waste.
  • Suggest ways of dealing with waste and environment-friendly practices (Activities 13.7–13.9).

Bury a banana peel and a plastic milk packet in a flower pot and dig them up a month later. The peel has turned into dark, crumbly soil; the packet looks almost new — and it will still look like that years later. Why does nature eat one and not the other? And what should we do with the mountains of the second kind that every town produces?

Quick reminder (Chapter 5)

Food is digested by enzymes. Enzymes are specific in their action: each enzyme breaks down only a particular substance. That is why we get no energy by trying to eat coal — none of our enzymes can break it down.

  1. Biodegradable and non-biodegradable substances

Substances that are broken down by biological processes are said to be biodegradable.
Substances that are not broken down in this manner are said to be non-biodegradable.

Why the difference? The decomposers (bacteria and other saprophytes) break substances down with enzymes, and enzymes are specific. There are enzymes for natural materials such as peels, paper and cotton, but many human-made materials, like plastics, are not broken down by the action of bacteria or other saprophytes. Physical processes such as heat and pressure can act on them, but under the ordinary conditions of our environment they persist for a long time.

Biodegradable Non-biodegradable
vegetable and fruit peels, spoilt food, tea leaves, paper, cotton cloth, wood, leather, grass, dung plastic (polythene bags, bottles, packets), glass, many metals, pesticides such as DDT

Non-biodegradable substances may be inert and simply persist in the environment for a long time, or may harm the various members of the ecosystem.

Find out more (Activity 13.6). Look up how long different non-biodegradable materials last in the environment, and read about the new plastics sold as "biodegradable" — find out whether they really break down, and whether they harm the environment while they do.

  1. How waste affects the environment

Biodegradable waste (explanations, built on your textbook):

  • in large heaps it rots and smells, and attracts flies and other carriers of disease germs;
  • it needs time and space to break down, so huge amounts still pile up faster than they can rot.

Non-biodegradable waste:

  • it persists for a long time — litter and heaps keep growing;
  • it can harm the members of the ecosystem — for example, animals that swallow plastic bags; harmful chemicals such as some pesticides enter food chains and accumulate (Topic 4);
  • it pollutes the soil and water and makes places dirty and unhealthy.

Why we make more waste than before. Improvements in our lifestyle have resulted in greater amounts of waste. Changes in attitude play a part too, with more and more things becoming disposable (use once, then throw). Changes in packaging have made much of our waste non-biodegradable.

Think it over (not for the exam — a box in your textbook). Tea in trains was once served in plastic glasses that had to be returned to the vendor. Disposable plastic cups were welcomed for hygiene, but nobody thought about millions of them being thrown away every day. Kulhads (clay cups) were suggested instead — but making them on a large scale would remove the fertile top-soil. Now paper cups are used. Their advantages over plastic cups: paper is biodegradable, and it can be recycled.

  1. Dealing with the waste — what happens and what we can do

Find out (Activities 13.7–13.9):

Do
Find out how the waste of your home and school is collected, and whether the local body (panchayat, municipal corporation or resident welfare association) treats biodegradable and non-biodegradable waste separately; work out how much waste your home and classroom make in a day and how much of it is biodegradable; find out how the sewage of your locality and the wastes of local industries are treated; find out which hazardous materials are in discarded electronic items, and how plastics are recycled.
Note
What you find: often the two kinds of waste are mixed and dumped together; sewage and industrial wastes can pollute water bodies and soil if they are not treated; old electronic items contain hazardous materials that can harm the soil, water and people if they are thrown away carelessly (beyond your textbook: for example lead and mercury); recycling plastics saves new material, but the process itself uses energy and can pollute if it is not done properly.
Shows
the problem of waste can be reduced only if each of us and the local bodies act.

Ways of dealing with waste (from these activities and the NCERT Exemplar):

  1. Separate the waste at home into biodegradable and non-biodegradable, so that each can be handled properly.
  2. Compost the biodegradable kitchen waste — it rots into manure that enriches the soil.
  3. Recycle paper, plastics, glass and metals — send them to be made into new things.
  4. Reuse things instead of throwing them away; cut down on disposable items and heavy packaging; carry cloth bags for shopping.
  5. Treat sewage and industrial waste before it is released into water bodies or onto land.
  6. Hand over old electronic items for safe disposal instead of throwing them into ordinary garbage.

Environment-friendly practices (your textbook's examples): carrying cloth bags while shopping; switching off unnecessary lights and fans; walking to school instead of being dropped by scooter.

Worked example 2

Two kinds of trouble

Question. Give two ways in which (a) a heap of rotting vegetable waste in a market and (b) plastic bags dumped near the same market affect the environment.

Given: biodegradable vegetable waste; non-biodegradable plastic bags. Asked: two effects of each.

Concept used: effects of biodegradable and non-biodegradable waste (this topic). Why it applies: one kind rots, the other persists.

  1. 1

    (a) Rotting vegetable waste: (i) it smells foul as it rots; (ii) it attracts flies and other carriers of disease germs, which spread disease. (It does break down in time, but a large heap piles up faster than it rots.)

  2. 2

    (b) Plastic bags: (i) they are not broken down by decomposers, so they persist for a long time and keep piling up; (ii) they harm animals — cattle or stray animals that eat them can fall ill or die.

  3. Check: each effect in (a) comes from rotting, which happens only to biodegradable waste; each effect in (b) comes from not rotting — swap the two lists and the reasons no longer fit ✔.

  4. Answer (a) Foul smell; flies and disease germs. (b) The bags persist for a long time and pile up; animals that eat them are harmed.

    Common trap: writing "biodegradable waste causes no problem because it rots". Rotting in a heap is the problem — smell and disease — when the waste is not composted.

Worked example 3

A waste plan for Class 10B

Question. Class 10B produces scraps of paper, fruit peels from lunch, used pens and refills, and plastic wrappers every day. Suggest a plan, with three steps, to reduce the class's waste problem.

Given: four kinds of classroom waste. Asked: a three-step plan.

Concept used: separating, composting, recycling and reducing waste (this topic).

  1. 1

    Separate at source: two bins in the class — one for biodegradable waste (paper scraps, fruit peels), one for non-biodegradable waste (refills, plastic wrappers).

  2. 2

    Treat each kind properly: fruit peels go to the school compost pit; clean paper goes for recycling; plastic wrappers are collected for recycling.

  3. 3

    Reduce: use refillable pens, write on both sides of the paper, and bring lunch in reusable boxes instead of wrappers.

  4. Check: every item on the list ends up in one of the three steps — peels → compost, paper → recycling, wrappers → recycling or avoided, refills → avoided by refillable pens ✔.

  5. Answer (1) Two separate bins — biodegradable and non-biodegradable. (2) Compost the peels; send paper and plastic for recycling. (3) Reduce waste: refillable pens, both sides of paper, reusable lunch boxes.

    Common trap: a plan that only says "throw waste in the dustbin". Putting mixed waste in one bin does not reduce it; the two kinds must be kept apart so each can be handled.

Did you get it?

Common mistakes!

  • "Biodegradable waste does no harm."

    In heaps it rots, smells and spreads disease germs; it needs time and space to break down.

    Biodegradable waste must also be handled — composted, not dumped.

  • "Plastic breaks down if it is buried long enough."

    Decomposers have no enzymes for it; it persists for a long time under ordinary conditions.

    Plastic: non-biodegradable — reduce, reuse, recycle.

  • Calling leather, wood or paper non-biodegradable.

    They come from living things and are broken down by decomposers (more slowly than peels).

    Test: can bacteria and fungi break it down? If yes — biodegradable.

Key points

  • Biodegradable substances are broken down by biological processes (decomposers); non-biodegradable substances are not.
  • Enzymes are specific, so decomposers cannot break down many human-made materials such as plastics, which persist for a long time.
  • Non-biodegradable substances may stay inert for a long time or harm members of the ecosystem; biodegradable waste in heaps smells and spreads disease.
  • Better lifestyles, disposable items and non-biodegradable packaging have greatly increased our waste.
  • Solutions: separate the two kinds of waste, compost biodegradable waste, recycle and reuse, cut down on disposables, carry cloth bags, and treat sewage and industrial waste before release.
