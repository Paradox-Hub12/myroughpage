Biology toolkit — cells, diffusion and enzymes

About 15–20 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Name the parts of a cell (cell wall, cell membrane, nucleus, cytoplasm, chloroplast, mitochondria, vacuole) and state the job of each.
  • Explain diffusion and osmosis, and say why diffusion is fast over tiny distances but slow over long ones.
  • Explain what enzymes do.
Why this lesson exists

Every process in this chapter happens inside cells, moves materials by diffusion or osmosis, and is speeded up by enzymes. You met these ideas in Class 9. If you still remember them, prove it below and skip ahead to Topic 2.

Work through this lesson — it takes about 15 minutes. Then try the questions again.

Hang a wet kurta on the line and, an hour later, the whole room smells of the soap you washed it in. Drop a pinch of turmeric into a glass of still water and the yellow slowly spreads on its own. Leave a handful of dry chana in water overnight and the seeds swell. Nobody pushed anything — yet particles moved. Living cells use exactly these movements every second. Before you study how a body feeds, breathes and cleans itself, you need three tools: the cell, diffusion and osmosis, and enzymes.

  1. The cell and its parts

Every living thing is made of cells, and every life process in this chapter happens inside them.

Cell part Job Plant cell Animal cell
Cell wall Rigid outer layer; gives shape and support ✔ ✘
Cell membrane (plasma membrane) Thin layer that controls what enters and leaves the cell ✔ ✔
Nucleus Controls the cell's activities ✔ ✔
Cytoplasm Jelly-like fluid where many reactions happen — including the first step of respiration ✔ ✔
Mitochondria Release energy from food using oxygen ✔ ✔
Chloroplasts Contain the green pigment chlorophyll; carry out photosynthesis ✔ (in green parts) ✘
Vacuole Stores water, food and waste materials ✔ (usually one large one) small or temporary ones

Notice that plant cells have mitochondria too. Plants respire all the time, day and night, just as animals do.

  1. Diffusion

Diffusion is the spreading of particles from a region where they are more concentrated to a region where they are less concentrated, until they are evenly spread. It needs no energy from the cell. Oxygen enters a cell by diffusion because there is more oxygen outside than inside, where it is being used up; carbon dioxide leaves the same way.

Diffusion has one big weakness: it is fast over tiny distances but very slow over long ones. Across one cell — a fraction of a millimetre — it takes a moment. Across a body many centimetres thick it would take far too long. Keep this in mind: it is the reason large animals need lungs, a heart and blood vessels, and tall plants need xylem and phloem.

  1. Osmosis

Osmosis is a special case: the movement of water through a selectively permeable membrane (one that lets water through but not some dissolved substances) from a dilute solution (more water) to a concentrated solution (less water).

  • A raisin or a dry seed in plain water swells: water moves in.
  • A slice of cucumber sprinkled with salt goes limp and "sweats": water moves out, towards the salt.

Plants depend on osmosis: water enters root cells by osmosis, and guard cells open the stomata when water flows into them (Topic 2).

  1. Enzymes

Starch, proteins and fats are large, complex molecules that cannot pass through the wall of the gut until they are broken into small, simple ones. Outside the body this would take years; in the body it takes hours, because of enzymes.

An enzyme is a biological catalyst: a substance made by living cells that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up. Two facts matter most in this chapter:

  1. Each enzyme works on one kind of substance. An enzyme that digests starch does nothing to proteins.
  2. Each enzyme works best in a particular medium. One stomach enzyme needs an acidic medium; the enzymes in the small intestine need an alkaline one.

Many enzyme names end in -ase — amylase (acts on starch, from amylum, Latin for starch), lipase (acts on fats, from lipos, Greek for fat).

  1. ATP — the cell's energy currency

The energy released from food in respiration is first stored in a molecule called ATP, which the cell then "spends" wherever energy is needed — like a charged battery that can run a torch, a fan or a radio. ATP is the cell's energy currency (Topic 4).

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Diffusion or osmosis?

Question. Say whether each change is caused by diffusion or osmosis, and give the reason: (a) the smell of agarbatti fills a room; (b) dry rajma soaked overnight becomes plump; (c) oxygen passes from the air in a leaf's spaces into its cells; (d) a raw mango slice sprinkled with salt becomes soft and watery.

Given: four everyday changes. Asked: diffusion or osmosis, with a reason.

Concept used: diffusion is any particle spreading from more to less concentrated; osmosis is water moving through a membrane from a dilute to a concentrated solution. Why it applies: each case is particles or water moving on their own.

  1. 1

    (a) Scent particles spread from the stick (high concentration) through the air (low) → diffusion. No membrane, and the moving particles are not water.

  2. 2

    (b) Water moves from the soaking water (more water) through the seed coat and cell membranes into the dry cells (less water) → osmosis.

  3. 3

    (c) Oxygen moves from the air spaces (more oxygen) into the cells, where it is used up (less oxygen) → diffusion. The particles are oxygen, not water.

  4. 4

    (d) Water moves out of the mango cells (more water) towards the salty surface (less water) through the cell membranes → osmosis.

  5. Check: each "osmosis" answer involves water and a membrane; each "diffusion" answer involves some other particle ✔.

  6. Answer (a) diffusion (b) osmosis (c) diffusion (d) osmosis

    Common trap: Calling (d) "diffusion of salt". The salt hardly enters; what makes the mango watery is water leaving the cells — osmosis.

Worked example 2

Sweet at the bottom, plain at the top

Question. A sugar cube is dropped gently into a tall glass of cold water that is not stirred (cold, so that warm currents do not mix it). A sip from the bottom soon tastes sweet, but the water at the top stays almost unsweetened for a long time. (a) By what process does the sugar spread? (b) Why is the top so slow to become sweet? (c) What does this tell you about how a large animal must get oxygen to its innermost cells?

Given: dissolved sugar spreading through still water. Asked: the process; why it is slow; what it means for large bodies.

Concept used: diffusion — particles spread from more to less concentrated; fast over tiny distances, slow over long ones. Why it applies: nothing is pushing the sugar; it spreads on its own.

  1. 1

    (a) Sugar particles move from the bottom, where they are concentrated, to the top, where there are few → diffusion.

  2. 2

    (b) The top of the glass is several centimetres away. Diffusion over a few micrometres is almost instant, but over centimetres it takes a very long time.

  3. 3

    (c) A large animal's innermost cells are many centimetres from the air. If oxygen had to diffuse that far, the cells would run out long before enough arrived.

  4. 4

    So, just as stirring spreads the sugar quickly through the glass, a large body needs a way to move oxygen in bulk — lungs to take it in and blood pumped by a heart to carry it.

  5. Check: stirring the water makes it sweet everywhere in seconds — moving the liquid in bulk beats diffusion over long distances, which is exactly the job of a circulation ✔.

  6. Answer (a) Diffusion (b) diffusion is very slow over distances of centimetres (c) large animals cannot rely on diffusion alone; they need lungs and a blood circulation to move oxygen in bulk.

    Common trap: Concluding that "diffusion does not happen in large animals". It happens in every cell — but only over the last tiny distance, from blood to cell.

Worked example 3

Matching enzymes to their jobs

Question. Amylase, lipase and a protein-digesting enzyme are mixed separately with a food sample that contains only fat. Which one will break it down, and why will the other two not act?

Given: three enzymes; a fat-only sample. Asked: which enzyme acts; why the others do not.

Concept used: each enzyme acts on one kind of substance; -ase names hint at the substrate. Why it applies: the sample has only one kind of food molecule.

  1. 1

    Lipase acts on fats (lipos = fat) → it breaks the fat into smaller molecules.

  2. 2

    Amylase acts on starch (amylum = starch) → no starch is present, so it does nothing.

  3. 3

    The protein-digesting enzyme acts only on proteins → no protein is present, so it does nothing.

  4. Check: each enzyme has been matched to exactly one food type, and only one of the three types is in the sample ✔.

  5. Answer Lipase; enzymes are specific — amylase works only on starch and the protein-digesting enzyme only on proteins.

    Common trap: Thinking "an enzyme digests food" in general. Each enzyme digests one kind of food molecule.

Quick check · 2 of 3Short answer2 marks

State one difference between diffusion and osmosis, and give one example of each from a plant.

Answer: Osmosis is the movement of water only, through a selectively permeable membrane; diffusion is the spreading of any particles from more to less concentrated, with or without a membrane. Examples: carbon dioxide entering a leaf through the stomata (diffusion); water entering root cells from the soil (osmosis).

The key words for osmosis are water and membrane. If either is missing, it is diffusion.

Quick check · 3 of 3MCQ1 mark

Why does the body need enzymes to digest food?

Catalysts do not add energy to food.
Enzymes are catalysts: they speed up the breakdown of large molecules into small, absorbable ones.
Enzymes are not used up as food; they are reused.
It is the other way round — digestion makes complex molecules simple.

Common mistakes!

  • "Plant cells have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria."

    Plants respire all the time; their cells need mitochondria.

    Plant cells have both: chloroplasts (photosynthesis) and mitochondria (respiration).

  • "Osmosis is the diffusion of salt or sugar."

    In osmosis only water moves through the membrane.

    Osmosis = water moving from dilute to concentrated through a membrane.

  • "Diffusion is fast, so it can supply any body."

    It is fast only over tiny distances.

    Large organisms need special organs and a transport system.

  • "Enzymes digest all kinds of food."

    Each enzyme acts on one kind of substance.

    Match the enzyme to its food: amylase–starch, lipase–fat, protein-digesting enzymes–protein.

  • "Energy from food is used directly by the cell."

    It is first stored in ATP.

    Respiration → ATP → energy for cell work.

Key points

  • Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large vacuole; animal cells do not. Both have mitochondria.
  • Diffusion: particles spread from more to less concentrated — fast over tiny distances, slow over long ones.
  • Osmosis: water moves through a selectively permeable membrane from a dilute to a concentrated solution.
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts; each acts on one kind of substance and works best in a particular medium.
  • ATP is the cell's energy currency.
