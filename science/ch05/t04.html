Topic 4 of 7

Respiration

About 75 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the breakdown of glucose by different pathways — aerobic in mitochondria, anaerobic in yeast and in muscle cells — and the role of ATP.
  • Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration, and show experimentally that carbon dioxide is given out during respiration.
  • Describe the human respiratory system, how air is breathed in, how the alveoli are designed for gas exchange, and how oxygen and carbon dioxide are transported.
  • Explain gas exchange in plants by day and by night, and why aquatic animals breathe faster than land animals.

Sprint up four flights of stairs and your legs begin to burn. Leave idli batter or bread dough in a warm kitchen and it puffs up with bubbles. Both happen for the same reason: cells breaking down sugar to release energy — one without enough oxygen, one with none at all. This topic is about how every living cell, in you and in a blade of grass, gets energy out of food.

Quick reminder (Chapter 1)

Respiration is an exothermic reaction: glucose combines with oxygen, releasing energy. Carbon dioxide turns lime water milky.

  1. Respiration is not breathing

Breathing Respiration
What it is Moving air in and out of the lungs Breaking down food (glucose) in cells to release energy
Where Breathing organs — in humans, the lungs and air passages Every living cell (cytoplasm and mitochondria)
Energy Uses energy (muscles work) Releases energy
Done by Animals with breathing organs (lungs, gills) All living organisms — including plants and yeast

Breathing only supplies oxygen and removes carbon dioxide; respiration is the chemical process inside cells.

  1. Breaking down glucose — three pathways

In every pathway the first step is the same: glucose (a six-carbon molecule) is broken into pyruvate (a three-carbon molecule) in the cytoplasm, releasing a little energy. What happens to pyruvate next depends on oxygen:

Pathway Condition Where Pyruvate becomes Energy
Aerobic respiration oxygen present mitochondria carbon dioxide + water a lot
Anaerobic respiration in yeast (fermentation) no oxygen yeast cells ethanol (a two-carbon molecule) + carbon dioxide a little
In our muscles lack of oxygen (sudden hard exercise) muscle cells lactic acid (a three-carbon molecule) — no carbon dioxide a little

The build-up of lactic acid in muscles during sudden activity causes cramps. Aerobic respiration releases much more energy than the anaerobic pathways.

Overall, aerobic respiration can be summarised (Chapter 1) as:

  1. ATP

The energy released in respiration is used at once to make ATP. ATP is then broken down wherever energy is needed, releasing a fixed amount of energy that drives the cell's endothermic reactions — muscle contraction, building proteins, carrying nerve impulses. ATP is the energy currency of the cell.

Did you know?Not in board exam
NCERT information box

ATP is made from ADP and inorganic phosphate using the energy of respiration. When the end phosphate linkage of ATP is broken using water, energy equal to 30.5 kJ/mol is released.

  1. Gas exchange in plants

Plants exchange gases through stomata, and large intercellular spaces inside leaves, stems and roots keep every cell in contact with air. Oxygen and carbon dioxide move by diffusion, in whichever direction conditions demand.

  • At night, there is no photosynthesis, so giving out carbon dioxide is the main exchange.
  • During the day, the carbon dioxide made in respiration is used up in photosynthesis, so no carbon dioxide is released; giving out oxygen is the main event.

Plants respire all the time — the day-time oxygen release only means photosynthesis is faster than respiration.

  1. Breathing in water and on land

Animals living in water use the oxygen dissolved in it. There is much less dissolved oxygen in water than oxygen in air, so aquatic animals breathe much faster than land animals. A fish takes water in through its mouth and forces it over its gills, where blood takes up the dissolved oxygen.

Land animals breathe the oxygen in the air. Their breathing organs have a large surface area in contact with air; because this surface is fine and delicate, it is kept inside the body, with passages to bring air to it and a way to move air in and out.

  1. The human respiratory system

Air path: nostrils → nasal passage (fine hairs and mucus filter the air) → throat (pharynx, larynx) → trachea (wind pipe), held open by rings of cartilage so it does not collapse → bronchi → smaller and smaller bronchioles → alveoli, tiny balloon-like sacs in the lungs.

Breathing in: we lift the ribs and flatten the diaphragm; the chest cavity becomes larger, and air is sucked into the lungs, filling the expanded alveoli. (Breathing out is the reverse: the ribs move down, the diaphragm returns to its dome shape, the chest cavity becomes smaller and air is pushed out.)

Gas exchange in the alveoli: the walls of the alveoli contain an extensive network of blood vessels. Carbon dioxide carried from the body by the blood passes out into the alveoli; oxygen from the air in the alveoli passes into the blood, which carries it to every cell. The lungs always hold a residual volume of air, so there is enough time for oxygen to be absorbed and carbon dioxide released.

Why the alveoli work so well: millions of alveoli give a huge surface area; their thin walls keep the diffusion distance short; and the rich blood supply carries gases away at once.

  1. Carrying the gases

In large animals diffusion alone cannot deliver oxygen to every part of the body. A respiratory pigment takes up oxygen in the lungs and releases it in tissues that are short of it. In humans this pigment is haemoglobin, found in the red blood corpuscles; it has a very high affinity for oxygen. Carbon dioxide is more soluble in water than oxygen, so it is carried mostly dissolved in the blood.

Did you know?Not in board exam
NCERT information boxes

Spread out flat, the alveolar surface would cover about 80 m². If oxygen had to diffuse from the lungs to your toes, it would take an estimated 3 years — thank haemoglobin. Hair-like cilia in the airways sweep out germs and dust; smoking destroys them, which is one reason smokers suffer infections, cough and even lung cancer. Tobacco in any form — cigarettes, bidis, gutkha — harms the tongue, lungs, heart and liver; India has a high rate of mouth cancer from chewing tobacco.

Virtual lab — CBSE Practical 7: carbon dioxide is given out during respiration

What you do:

  1. Put a handful of germinating seeds (e.g. moong or gram, soaked for a day) in a conical flask.
  2. Hang a small test tube containing freshly prepared potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution inside the flask by a thread.
  3. Close the flask with a one-holed cork carrying a bent glass delivery tube; dip the free end in a beaker of water. Seal the cork with Vaseline so the set-up is air-tight.
  4. Mark the water level in the delivery tube. Leave the set-up for about one to two hours.

What you observe: The water rises in the delivery tube.

What it shows:

  1. The germinating seeds respire, taking in oxygen and giving out carbon dioxide.
  2. The KOH absorbs the carbon dioxide, so the amount of gas in the flask falls.
  3. The pressure inside drops, and the outside air pressure pushes water up the tube.
  4. So carbon dioxide is given out during respiration. (A control flask without KOH shows little or no rise, because the carbon dioxide given out roughly replaces the oxygen used.)

Why germinating seeds? They are respiring fast to supply energy for growth. Why air-tight? Any leak lets air in, and the water will not rise.

Safety

potassium hydroxide is corrosive — your teacher prepares it; do not touch it or let it splash into your eyes; handle glass tubes gently.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

The sprinter's cramp

Question. At the school sports day, Arjun runs the 400 m flat out. At the finishing line his thigh muscles ache and cramp. (a) Which substance has built up in his muscles, and why? (b) Write the pathway that formed it. (c) Why does it not happen when he walks to the stand?

Given: sudden, hard exercise; cramp. Asked: substance, reason, pathway, why not when walking.

Concept used: the three pathways of glucose breakdown (this topic). Why it applies: the muscles are working faster than oxygen can reach them.

  1. 1

    (a) During the sprint his muscle cells need energy faster than the blood can bring oxygen → his muscle cells run short of oxygen.

  2. 2

    Pyruvate is then converted to lactic acid, which builds up and causes the cramp.

  3. 3

    (b) Pathway: glucose → (in cytoplasm) pyruvate + energy → (lack of oxygen, in muscle cells) lactic acid + energy.

  4. 4

    (c) While walking, enough oxygen reaches the muscles, so pyruvate is broken down aerobically in the mitochondria to carbon dioxide and water — no lactic acid builds up.

  5. Check: the product is a three-carbon molecule (lactic acid), the same size as pyruvate — so no carbon is lost as carbon dioxide in this pathway ✔.

  6. Answer (a) Lactic acid, because his muscles ran short of oxygen (b) glucose → pyruvate → lactic acid + energy (c) walking needs less energy, so oxygen supply keeps up and respiration stays aerobic.

    Common trap: Writing "muscles make alcohol (ethanol) and carbon dioxide". That is yeast. Muscles make lactic acid only — no carbon dioxide.

Worked example 2

Why bread dough rises

Question. A baker mixes flour, water, sugar and a spoon of yeast, and leaves the dough covered in a warm place. In two hours it has doubled in size and is full of tiny holes. (a) Which process in the yeast caused this? (b) Name its products. (c) Why does the inside of the dough suit this process?

Given: yeast in dough with sugar; dough rises with gas holes. Asked: process, products, conditions.

Concept used: anaerobic respiration (fermentation) in yeast (this topic). Why it applies: yeast is breaking down sugar inside a dough that has little air.

  1. 1

    (a) The yeast breaks down sugar by anaerobic respiration — fermentation.

  2. 2

    (b) Glucose → pyruvate → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy.

  3. 3

    The carbon dioxide gas forms bubbles that are trapped in the sticky dough, so it swells and becomes spongy; the small amount of ethanol is driven off when the bread is baked.

  4. 4

    (c) Inside the covered dough there is very little oxygen, so the yeast uses the pathway that needs none; warmth helps the yeast work faster.

  5. Check: the gas could be tested: led into lime water, it would turn it milky — exactly as in Activity 5.5 ✔.

  6. Answer (a) Anaerobic respiration (fermentation) by yeast (b) ethanol and carbon dioxide (with energy) (c) the dough has almost no oxygen.

    Common trap: Writing "yeast makes lactic acid". Yeast gives ethanol + carbon dioxide; lactic acid is made in our muscles.

Worked example 3

Goldfish and girl: who breathes faster?

Question. Meena counts her goldfish opening and closing its mouth 70 times in a minute; she breathes in 18 times a minute while sitting. Explain the difference, and say where the goldfish takes up oxygen.

Given: fish ≈ 70 per minute; human ≈ 18 per minute. Asked: reason; site of oxygen uptake in the fish.

Concept used: aquatic animals use dissolved oxygen, which is scarce (this topic). Why it applies: both need oxygen for respiration, but from different sources.

  1. 1

    The fish uses oxygen dissolved in water; Meena uses oxygen in air.

  2. 2

    The amount of dissolved oxygen in water is fairly low compared with the oxygen in the same volume of air.

  3. 3

    To get enough oxygen, the fish must pass a much larger volume of water over its gills → it breathes much faster.

  4. 4

    The fish takes water in through its mouth and forces it past the gills, where the blood takes up the dissolved oxygen.

  5. Check: this is what NCERT Activity 5.6 asks you to observe — count a fish's mouth movements and compare them with your own breathing ✔.

  6. Answer Water holds much less dissolved oxygen than air holds oxygen, so the fish must breathe far faster; it absorbs oxygen into its blood at the gills.

    Common trap: Saying "fish breathe in water itself (HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O) for oxygen". Fish use the oxygen gas dissolved in water, not the oxygen atoms in water molecules.

Common mistakes!

  • "Respiration means breathing."

    Breathing moves air; respiration releases energy from food in cells.

    Keep them separate (table in section 1).

  • "Plants don't respire" or "plants respire only at night."

    All living cells respire all the time.

    By day photosynthesis is faster, so the plant gives out oxygen overall; at night it gives out carbon dioxide.

  • "Muscle cells make lactic acid and carbon dioxide."

    Lactic acid has 3 carbons, like pyruvate — no carbon is lost.

    Muscles: lactic acid + energy only. Yeast: ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy.

  • "Glucose is broken into pyruvate in the mitochondria."

    The first step is in the cytoplasm.

    Glucose → pyruvate in the cytoplasm; pyruvate + oxygen → COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ + HX2O\ce{H2O}HX2​O in the mitochondria.

  • "Anaerobic respiration releases more energy."

    It is the other way round.

    Aerobic releases much more energy.

  • "We breathe in by lowering the ribs and raising the diaphragm."

    That makes the chest cavity smaller.

    Breathing in: ribs lifted, diaphragm flattened, chest cavity larger.

  • "Haemoglobin carries most of the carbon dioxide."

    Carbon dioxide is more soluble in water.

    Haemoglobin carries oxygen; carbon dioxide is mostly dissolved in blood.

Key points

  • Respiration releases energy from food in every cell; breathing only moves air.
  • Glucose (6C) → pyruvate (3C) in the cytoplasm; then: oxygen present (mitochondria) → COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ + water + a lot of energy; no oxygen (yeast) → ethanol + COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​; lack of oxygen (muscles) → lactic acid (cramps).
  • Energy from respiration is stored in ATP, the cell's energy currency.
  • Plants exchange gases by diffusion through stomata and intercellular spaces; night → COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ out; day → OX2\ce{O2}OX2​ out (COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ used in photosynthesis).
  • Aquatic animals breathe faster (little dissolved oxygen); fish use gills.
  • Breathing in: ribs lifted, diaphragm flattened → chest cavity larger → air sucked in. Alveoli: huge surface, thin walls, rich blood supply. Haemoglobin in RBCs carries OX2\ce{O2}OX2​; COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ mostly dissolved.
  • Practical 7: germinating seeds + KOH → water rises → COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ is given out in respiration.
