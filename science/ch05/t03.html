Topic 3 of 7

Heterotrophic nutrition and human digestion

About 70 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the ways heterotrophs obtain food, with examples, and explain nutrition in Amoeba and Paramoecium.
  • Trace the digestion of carbohydrates, proteins and fats through the human alimentary canal, naming each secretion, enzyme and site, and explain how the small intestine absorbs digested food.
  • Draw and label the human alimentary canal.

A plate of rice, dal and a spoon of ghee goes in at your mouth. A few hours later the starch, protein and fat in it have become tiny molecules travelling in your blood to every cell — and what is left leaves the body a day or so later. Along the way, five different juices were poured onto that food, each at exactly the right place. Follow the plate and you have learnt the whole digestive system.

Quick reminder (Chapter 2)

Acids and bases neutralise each other; enzymes in different parts of the gut need an acidic or an alkaline medium. Chapter 4: soap breaks oil into tiny droplets (emulsifying) so it can be washed away.

  1. How heterotrophs get their food

The way an organism feeds depends on what its food is and how it gets it — a cow grazing on grass that stays still, a lion chasing a deer that runs. Three broad strategies:

Strategy What happens Examples
Break down food outside the body, then absorb it Enzymes are released onto the food; the simple products are soaked up fungi — bread moulds, yeast, mushrooms
Take in whole food and break it down inside the body Food is swallowed or engulfed, then digested Amoeba, Paramoecium, cow, lion, humans
Take food from a living plant or animal without killing it (parasitic nutrition) Lives on or in the host and feeds on it Cuscuta (amar-bel), ticks, lice, leeches, tapeworms

(Other books often call the first strategy saprophytic and the second holozoic nutrition. Your textbook describes them without these names.)

  1. Single-celled organisms

Amoeba pushes out temporary finger-like extensions of its cell surface (pseudopodia) around a food particle. They fuse over it, forming a food vacuole. Inside the vacuole the food is broken down into simpler substances, which then diffuse out into the cytoplasm. The undigested remains are moved to the cell surface and thrown out.

Paramoecium has a definite shape, so it cannot surround food. Food is taken in at one specific spot, and is moved there by the beating of cilia that cover its whole surface.

  1. Digestion in human beings — follow the food

The alimentary canal is a long tube from the mouth to the anus, with each region doing a special job. Food is pushed along by peristalsis — rhythmic contractions of the muscles in the wall of the canal, all along the gut.

Mouth. Teeth crush food into small pieces of similar texture. The salivary glands pour in saliva, which wets the food so it slides along the soft-lined canal. Saliva contains the enzyme salivary amylase, which breaks down starch (a complex molecule) into simple sugar. The muscular tongue mixes food with saliva while you chew.

Oesophagus (food pipe). Carries food from the mouth to the stomach by peristalsis.

Stomach. A large organ that expands as food enters; its muscular walls mix food with more digestive juice. Gastric glands in its wall release:

  • hydrochloric acid — makes the medium acidic, which the enzyme pepsin needs (it also kills many germs that come in with food);
  • pepsin — an enzyme that digests proteins;
  • mucus — protects the inner lining of the stomach from the acid. (Too much acid, or too little protection, is felt as "acidity".)

A sphincter muscle at the exit of the stomach lets food into the small intestine a little at a time.

Small intestine. The longest part of the canal, tightly coiled to fit. Here carbohydrates, proteins and fats are completely digested, and it receives juices from the liver and pancreas.

  • Bile juice (made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder) does two jobs: it makes the acidic food alkaline so that the pancreatic enzymes can act, and its bile salts break large fat globules into smaller globules (emulsification), so enzymes can act on them more efficiently. Bile contains no enzyme.
  • Pancreatic juice (from the pancreas) contains trypsin, which digests proteins, and lipase, which breaks down emulsified fats.
  • Intestinal juice (from glands in the intestine wall) finishes the job: proteins → amino acids; complex carbohydrates → glucose; fats → fatty acids and glycerol.

(The length of the small intestine depends on diet: grass-eaters need a longer one to digest cellulose; meat is easier to digest, so tigers have a shorter one.)

Absorption. The small intestine's inner lining is covered with thousands of finger-like projections called villi. They increase the surface area for absorption, and they are richly supplied with blood vessels, which carry the absorbed food to every cell — for energy, for building new tissue and for repairing old tissue.

Large intestine. Unabsorbed food passes here; its wall absorbs more water. The remaining waste leaves through the anus, controlled by the anal sphincter.

NCERT information box

Dental caries (tooth decay) starts when bacteria feeding on sugars make acids, which soften the enamel. Bacteria and food particles stick to the teeth as plaque, which stops saliva reaching the tooth to neutralise the acid. Brushing after eating removes plaque before the bacteria can make acid.

Lab — saliva and starch (NCERT Activity 5.3)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Take 1 mL of 1% starch solution in each of two test tubes, A and B. Add 1 mL of saliva to tube A only. Leave both for 20–30 minutes, then add a few drops of dilute iodine solution to each.
See
Tube B (no saliva) turns blue-black. Tube A (with saliva) shows no blue-black colour (or only a faint one).
Shows
The starch in tube A has been broken down → saliva contains an enzyme (salivary amylase) that digests starch into sugar. Tube B is the control.
Note
Safety: use your own saliva in your own clean test tube; iodine stains skin and clothes.

Worked example 1

Follow a plate of rice, dal and ghee

Question. Ravi eats rice (mainly starch), dal (mainly protein) and a little ghee (fat). For each food, state where its digestion begins, the enzyme(s) that act on it, and the final product absorbed.

Given: three foods — starch, protein, fat. Asked: start of digestion, enzymes, end products.

Concept used: the enzyme table (this topic). Why it applies: each food type is digested by its own enzymes at particular sites.

  1. 1

    Rice — starch

    Digestion begins in the mouth: salivary amylase breaks starch into simple sugar.

  2. 2

    It is completed in the small intestine by the intestinal juice → glucose.

  3. 3

    Dal — protein

    Digestion begins in the stomach: pepsin, working in the acidic medium made by hydrochloric acid.

  4. 4

    It continues in the small intestine with trypsin from the pancreas.

  5. 5

    The intestinal juice finishes it → amino acids.

  6. 6

    Ghee — fat

    Your textbook describes no fat digestion in the mouth or stomach. In the small intestine, bile salts first break the large fat globules into small droplets (emulsification — not digestion; bile has no enzyme).

  7. 7

    Lipase from the pancreas then breaks down the emulsified fat.

  8. 8

    The intestinal juice finishes it → fatty acids and glycerol.

  9. Check: each food has been matched to an enzyme that acts only on that food (amylase–starch, pepsin/trypsin–protein, lipase–fat), and all three end in the small intestine, the site of complete digestion ✔.

  10. Answer Starch: mouth, salivary amylase → glucose. Protein: stomach, pepsin, then trypsin → amino acids. Fat: small intestine, bile emulsifies, lipase digests → fatty acids + glycerol.

    Common trap: Calling bile "an enzyme that digests fat". Bile only emulsifies fat and makes the medium alkaline; lipase digests it.

Worked example 2

Why a carpet is more like a gut than a floor mat

Question. A thin cotton floor mat and a thick, shaggy carpet of the same size are dipped in water and lifted out. The shaggy carpet holds far more water. Use this idea to explain how the villi help the small intestine, and why each villus has a rich blood supply.

Given: two surfaces of the same size; the shaggy one holds more water. Asked: link to villi; role of blood vessels.

Concept used: villi are finger-like projections that increase surface area; they are richly supplied with blood vessels (this topic). Why it applies: absorption happens across a surface, so more surface means more absorption.

  1. 1

    The shaggy carpet's threads give it much more surface than a flat mat of the same outline, so more water clings to it.

  2. 2

    In the same way, the villi fold the intestine's lining into thousands of tiny fingers, giving a far larger surface area in the same length of tube.

  3. 3

    More surface in contact with digested food means more food is absorbed in the time it spends in the intestine.

  4. 4

    Each villus contains many blood vessels. Absorbed food enters the blood at once and is carried away to every cell for energy, growth and repair.

  5. 5

    Carrying food away keeps the concentration in the villi low, so absorption from the gut can keep going.

  6. Check: both features answer the two halves of the job — villi give the surface, blood vessels give the removal of absorbed food ✔.

  7. Answer Villi are finger-like projections that greatly increase the surface area for absorption; their rich blood supply carries the absorbed food to all cells of the body.

    Common trap: Writing "villi digest the food". Digestion is done by enzymes; villi absorb the digested food.

Worked example 3

Identify the parts from their jobs

Question. On a diagram of the alimentary canal, six parts are marked P to U. Identify each from its description: P — stores bile; Q — gastric glands in its wall release acid, pepsin and mucus; R — the longest part; complete digestion and absorption happen here; S — secretes juice containing trypsin and lipase; T — absorbs water from undigested food; U — carries food from the mouth to the stomach by peristalsis.

Given: six descriptions. Asked: the name of each part.

Concept used: the job of each organ (this topic). Why it applies: in a labelling question, a part is identified by what it does as well as where it is.

  1. 1

    P stores bile → gall bladder (bile is made in the liver).

  2. 2

    Q has gastric glands → stomach.

  3. 3

    R longest, complete digestion and absorption → small intestine.

  4. 4

    S juice with trypsin and lipase → pancreas.

  5. 5

    T absorbs water → large intestine.

  6. 6

    U food pipe → oesophagus.

  7. Check: the six answers follow the path of food: U → Q → R (with P and S emptying into it) → T ✔.

  8. Answer P gall bladder · Q stomach · R small intestine · S pancreas · T large intestine · U oesophagus

    Common trap: Labelling P as "liver". The liver makes bile; the gall bladder, tucked under it, stores it — label the small sac, not the large organ.

Quick check · 3 of 5Short answer2 marks

How does Paramoecium take in food differently from Amoeba?

Answer: Amoeba has no fixed shape: it surrounds food with pseudopodia (temporary finger-like extensions) anywhere on its surface, forming a food vacuole. Paramoecium has a definite shape: food is taken in at one specific spot, and its cilia move food to that spot.

The key contrast is any point + pseudopodia vs one fixed spot + cilia.

Common mistakes!

  • "Pepsin acts in the small intestine."

    Pepsin needs the acidic stomach.

    Pepsin — stomach (gastric glands).

  • "Trypsin is made in the stomach."

    Trypsin is in pancreatic juice.

    Trypsin — pancreas → small intestine.

  • "Bile is an enzyme that digests fats."

    Bile has no enzyme.

    Bile emulsifies fats and makes the medium alkaline; lipase digests fats.

  • "Bile is made in the gall bladder."

    It is made in the liver.

    Liver makes, gall bladder stores.

  • "Lipase digests fat globules directly."

    Large globules are hard for enzymes to act on.

    Bile breaks them up first; lipase acts on emulsified fats.

  • "Most digestion happens in the stomach."

    Only protein digestion begins there.

    Complete digestion and absorption — small intestine.

  • "Villi digest food."

    Villi absorb.

    Enzymes digest; villi increase the surface area for absorption.

  • Labels without leader lines, or the stomach drawn on the body's right

    Marks are lost for unclear or misplaced labels.

    Use a ruler line to each part; stomach on the body's left (your right as you look at the page).

Key points

  • Heterotrophs: break food down outside and absorb (fungi); take whole food inside (Amoeba, animals); parasites (Cuscuta, ticks, lice, leeches, tapeworms).
  • Amoeba: pseudopodia → food vacuole → digested food diffuses into cytoplasm. Paramoecium: cilia move food to one fixed spot.
  • Mouth: salivary amylase, starch → sugar. Stomach: HCl (acidic medium), pepsin (proteins), mucus (protects lining).
  • Small intestine: bile from liver (alkaline; emulsifies fats — no enzyme); pancreatic juice — trypsin (proteins), lipase (fats); intestinal juice → amino acids, glucose, fatty acids + glycerol.
  • Villi increase surface area and are rich in blood vessels → absorption. Large intestine absorbs water; anal sphincter controls the exit.
