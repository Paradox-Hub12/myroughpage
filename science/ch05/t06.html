Topic 6 of 7

Transport in plants

About 50 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe how water and minerals are transported in xylem, by root pressure and by transpiration pull.
  • Describe how food and other substances are transported in phloem (translocation), including the use of energy from ATP.
  • Compare transport in xylem and phloem, and explain transpiration and its roles.

A tall coconut palm lifts water from the soil to leaves 20 metres up, every day, with no heart and no pump you can see. In spring a mango tree pushes stored sugar from its trunk to thousands of new buds. A plant has two separate plumbing systems for these two jobs — and one of them runs on nothing but evaporation from its leaves.

  1. Why plants need a transport system

Plants make food in their leaves and take raw materials — water, nitrogen, phosphorus and other minerals — from the soil through their roots. If the distance between roots and leaves is small, materials can simply diffuse. But in a large plant the distances are far too great for diffusion, so raw materials must be carried up to the leaves, and food carried down to the roots.

Plants manage with relatively slow transport systems because they have low energy needs: they do not move, and many of their tissues are made of dead cells. The challenge is distance — in a very tall tree it is huge.

Plants have two independent sets of conducting tubes (you studied their structure in Class 9):

  • Xylem moves water and minerals from the soil.
  • Phloem moves the products of photosynthesis from the leaves to other parts.

  1. Water and minerals in xylem

In xylem, the vessels and tracheids of roots, stems and leaves are joined end to end into a continuous system of water-conducting channels that runs to every part of the plant. Water is moved up in two ways.

(a) Root pressure — the push from below.

  1. Root cells touching the soil take up ions (minerals) actively, using energy.
  2. This makes the concentration of ions higher in the root than in the soil.
  3. Water follows: it moves from the soil into the root, which evens out the difference.
  4. As water keeps entering the root xylem, the column of water is steadily pushed upwards.

This push alone is not enough to lift water to the tops of most plants.

(b) Transpiration pull — the pull from above.

  1. Water evaporates from the cells of the leaf and escapes as vapour through the stomata.
  2. Water drawn from the xylem of the leaf takes its place.
  3. This evaporation creates a suction that pulls water up from the xylem of the roots, all the way through the stem.

Transpiration is the loss of water in the form of vapour from the aerial parts of a plant. It has two roles:

  • it helps in the absorption and upward movement of water and the minerals dissolved in it, from roots to leaves;
  • it helps in temperature regulation (evaporation cools the leaf).

Night and day: root pressure matters more at night. By day, with the stomata open, transpiration pull becomes the major driving force for water movement in the xylem.

  1. Food in phloem — translocation

The movement of the soluble products of photosynthesis from the leaves to other parts of the plant is called translocation. It takes place in the phloem, which also carries amino acids and other substances.

  • It happens in the sieve tubes, helped by the neighbouring companion cells.
  • It goes both upwards and downwards — to storage organs (roots, fruits, seeds) and to growing parts.

Water moves up the xylem mainly by physical forces — no ATP is used to move the water itself (only the roots' uptake of ions uses energy). Translocation is different: it uses energy to move the food.

  1. Material such as sucrose is loaded into the phloem using energy from ATP.
  2. This raises the osmotic pressure of the phloem tissue, so water moves into it (osmosis).
  3. This pressure pushes the dissolved food along the phloem towards tissues where the pressure is lower.

This lets the plant send food wherever it is needed. In spring, for example, sugar stored over winter in the roots or stem is sent to the opening buds, which need energy to grow.

  1. Xylem and phloem compared

Point Xylem Phloem
Carries water and minerals food (products of photosynthesis, e.g. sucrose), amino acids and other substances
Direction one way — upwards, from roots to leaves both ways — up and down
Driving force physical forces: root pressure and transpiration pull energy from ATP, creating osmotic pressure
Tubes vessels and tracheids sieve tubes with companion cells
Name of the process transport of water (upward movement in xylem) translocation

Lab — does the plant give out water? (NCERT Activity 5.8)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Take two pots of the same size with the same amount of soil — one with a plant, one with a stick of the same height. Cover the soil in both with plastic sheet so no water evaporates from it. Cover each whole set with a plastic sheet and leave in bright sunlight for half an hour.
See
Droplets of water collect inside the sheet over the plant; the sheet over the stick stays dry.
Shows
The water came from the plant's aerial parts — the plant loses water vapour by transpiration. (The soil was sealed, and the stick gave nothing — it is the control.)

Worked examples

Worked example 1

The potted plant in a plastic bag

Question. Kabir tied a clear plastic bag over the leafy shoot of a potted tulsi plant and kept it in sunshine. By noon the inside of the bag was misty with droplets. (a) Name the process. (b) Through which openings did the water leave? (c) Kabir then kept an identical bagged plant in a cool, dark room. Would you expect more or fewer droplets, and why?

Given: bagged shoot in sunshine → droplets; second plant in a cool, dark room. Asked: process; openings; prediction.

Concept used: transpiration — loss of water vapour from the aerial parts, mainly through stomata (this topic and Topic 2). Why it applies: only the shoot was enclosed, so the water came from the aerial parts.

  1. 1

    (a) Water vapour lost from the aerial parts of the plant → transpiration.

  2. 2

    (b) Mainly through the stomata of the leaves.

  3. 3

    (c) In a cool, dark room, less water evaporates, and in the dark the plant has no use for carbon dioxide, so many stomata close.

  4. 4

    So there will be fewer droplets.

  5. Check: the bag covered only the shoot, not the soil, so evaporation from the soil cannot explain the droplets — and the prediction in (c) follows the same rule as Topic 2 (stomata close when carbon dioxide is not needed) ✔.

  6. Answer (a) Transpiration (b) stomata of the leaves (c) fewer droplets — less evaporation, and the stomata close in the dark.

    Common trap: Saying the droplets are "the plant breathing out". This is water vapour lost by transpiration; gas exchange for respiration is a separate process.

Worked example 2

How water reaches the top of a tall tree

Question. A teak tree is 25 m tall. Explain why root pressure alone cannot supply its top leaves on a sunny day, and how the leaves themselves help to lift the water. When would root pressure matter more?

Given: a 25 m tree; sunny day. Asked: limitation of root pressure; role of transpiration; when root pressure matters.

Concept used: root pressure and transpiration pull (this topic). Why it applies: both act on the same continuous column of water in the xylem.

  1. 1

    Root pressure arises because root cells take up ions actively, and water follows into the root xylem, pushing the column up.

  2. 2

    This push is not strong enough to raise water to the heights of tall trees.

  3. 3

    On a sunny day the stomata are open and water evaporates fast from the leaf cells.

  4. 4

    Water lost from the leaf cells is replaced from the xylem in the leaf; this creates a suction that pulls water up through the stem from the roots — the transpiration pull.

  5. 5

    Because the xylem forms an unbroken column from root to leaf, the pull at the top is passed all the way down.

  6. 6

    At night, when the stomata are closed and transpiration is low, root pressure is the more important force.

  7. Check: the answer gives both forces and assigns each to the right time — transpiration pull by day, root pressure at night ✔.

  8. Answer Root pressure is too weak to lift water 25 m; by day, evaporation from the leaves creates a suction (transpiration pull) that draws water up the continuous xylem column. Root pressure matters more at night.

    Common trap: Writing "phloem pulls water up". Water travels in xylem; phloem carries food.

Common mistakes!

  • "Xylem carries food and water."

    Food travels in phloem.

    Xylem: water + minerals. Phloem: food.

  • "Phloem moves only downwards."

    It carries food up and down, to wherever it is needed.

    Phloem: both directions; xylem: upwards only.

  • "Xylem transport needs a lot of ATP."

    It is driven mainly by physical forces.

    Phloem uses ATP; xylem uses root pressure and transpiration pull.

  • "Root pressure is the main force by day."

    By day the stomata are open and transpiration is high.

    Day: transpiration pull. Night: root pressure more important.

  • "Transpiration is just a waste of water."

    It drives water and mineral uptake and cools the plant.

    Give both roles.

  • "Translocation is the movement of water."

    Translocation is the movement of food in phloem.

    Water: transport in xylem. Food: translocation in phloem.

Key points

  • Plants need transport because roots and leaves are far apart; low energy needs (no movement, many dead cells) allow slow systems.
  • Xylem (vessels, tracheids) carries water and minerals upwards: root pressure (ions taken up actively → water follows; important at night) + transpiration pull (evaporation from leaves → suction; main force by day).
  • Transpiration = loss of water vapour from aerial parts; helps absorption and upward movement of water and minerals, and temperature regulation.
  • Phloem (sieve tubes + companion cells) carries food both up and down = translocation; uses ATP to load sucrose → osmotic pressure → flow to low-pressure tissues (e.g. buds in spring).
