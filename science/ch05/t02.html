Topic 2 of 7

Life processes and autotrophic nutrition

About 70 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain how we decide that something is alive, why living things need life processes, and why diffusion is not enough in multicellular organisms.
  • Describe photosynthesis — raw materials, conditions, the three events, products and the balanced equation — and explain how guard cells open and close stomata.
  • Describe the experiments showing that chlorophyll and carbon dioxide are needed for photosynthesis, and prepare a temporary mount of a leaf peel to show stomata.

A cat curled up asleep on a wall is alive. So is a tulsi plant that has not grown a millimetre since yesterday. A pebble beside them is not. You cannot see either living thing move — so how do you know? The answer lies in movements far too small to see, and in the energy a living body must keep finding to pay for them.

  1. What makes something "alive"?

We usually take movement as a sign of life — running, chewing, breathing, growing. But a plant that shows no growth from one day to the next is still alive, and some animals breathe with no movement you can see. So visible movement is not enough to decide.

Biologists look deeper: at movement of molecules. A living body is a highly organised structure — tissues made of cells, cells made of smaller parts. The environment keeps breaking this order down, and if the order breaks down the organism dies. So living things must constantly repair and maintain their structures; and because these structures are built of molecules, they must move molecules around all the time. (Viruses show no molecular movement until they infect a cell — part of the reason scientists argue about whether they are truly alive.)

  1. Life processes

The processes that together carry out this maintenance job — even while we sit still or sleep — are called life processes.

Life process Why it is needed
Nutrition Maintenance needs energy, and growth needs raw materials. Both come from outside, as food. Getting food into the body is nutrition.
Respiration Food must be broken down to release usable energy. Many organisms use oxygen from outside for this. Taking in oxygen and using it to break down food is respiration.
Transport In a large body, food and oxygen are taken in at one place but needed everywhere, so they must be carried round.
Excretion Breaking down food makes wastes that are useless or harmful; they must be removed from the body.

Why is diffusion not enough? In a single-celled organism, the whole surface touches the environment, so food, oxygen and wastes can simply diffuse in and out. In a multicellular organism, most cells are not in direct contact with the surroundings, and diffusion over long distances is far too slow (Topic 1). So large organisms have specialised tissues for taking in food and oxygen and for removing wastes — and a transport system to link them to every cell.

  1. Two ways to get food

Autotrophs (green plants and some bacteria) make their own food from simple inorganic materials — carbon dioxide and water. Heterotrophs (animals and fungi) take in complex food made by other organisms and break it down using enzymes. Every heterotroph depends, directly or indirectly, on autotrophs.

  1. Photosynthesis

Autotrophs meet their carbon and energy needs by photosynthesis: they take in carbon dioxide and water and, using sunlight trapped by chlorophyll, turn them into carbohydrates — a stored form of energy. Oxygen is given out.

Here CX6HX12OX6\ce{C6H12O6}CX6​HX12​OX6​ is glucose. Carbohydrates not needed at once are stored as starch, the plant's internal energy reserve. (Our bodies store some energy from food in a similar way, as glycogen.)

The three events of photosynthesis:

  1. Absorption of light energy by chlorophyll.
  2. Conversion of light energy to chemical energy, and splitting of water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen.
  3. Reduction of carbon dioxide to carbohydrates.

These steps need not happen one straight after the other. Desert plants take in carbon dioxide at night and make an intermediate compound; during the day, the energy absorbed by chlorophyll acts on it.

Chlorophyll is found inside chloroplasts — the green dots you see in leaf cells under a microscope.

  1. Stomata: the leaf's doors

Stomata (singular stoma) are tiny pores on the surface of leaves. Huge amounts of gas exchange happen through them for photosynthesis (some gas also passes through the general surface of stems, roots and leaves). But a lot of water is lost through them too, so the plant closes them when it does not need carbon dioxide.

Each pore is bordered by two guard cells:

  • Water flows into the guard cells → they swell → the pore opens.
  • Water leaves the guard cells → they shrink → the pore closes.

  1. Other raw materials

Land plants draw the water they need for photosynthesis out of the soil through their roots. The soil also gives nitrogen, phosphorus, iron and magnesium. Nitrogen, needed to make proteins, is taken up as inorganic nitrates or nitrites, or as organic compounds made by bacteria from atmospheric nitrogen.

Labs — the photosynthesis experiments

Lab 1: Is chlorophyll needed? (NCERT Activity 5.1)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Keep a potted plant with variegated leaves (green and white patches — e.g. money plant or croton) in the dark for three days to use up its starch (destarching). Put it in sunlight for about six hours. Pluck a leaf and trace its green areas on paper. Dip it in boiling water for a few minutes, then put it in a beaker of alcohol placed in a water bath; heat until the alcohol boils. Finally dip the leaf in dilute iodine solution, rinse and compare with your tracing.
See
In the alcohol the leaf turns pale and the alcohol turns green (chlorophyll dissolves out). With iodine, only the parts that were green turn blue-black; the white parts do not.
Shows
Starch (blue-black with iodine) formed only where chlorophyll was present → chlorophyll is necessary for photosynthesis.
Note
Safety: alcohol catches fire easily — heat it only in a water bath, never over a direct flame; handle boiling water with tongs.

Lab 2: Is carbon dioxide needed? (NCERT Activity 5.2)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Destarch two similar potted plants (three days in the dark). Stand each on a glass plate and cover with a bell jar, sealing the bottom with Vaseline so it is air-tight. Beside one plant, place a watch-glass of potassium hydroxide (KOH), which absorbs carbon dioxide. Keep both in sunlight for about two hours, then test a leaf of each for starch.
See
The leaf from the jar without KOH turns blue-black. The leaf from the jar with KOH does not (or only very little).
Shows
Without carbon dioxide, no starch was made → carbon dioxide is necessary for photosynthesis. (The plant without KOH is the control.)
Note
Safety: potassium hydroxide is corrosive — your teacher handles it; avoid skin and eyes.

Can you design a test for sunlight? Destarch a plant, cover part of one leaf with a strip of black paper, put it in sunlight, then test for starch — see Worked example 1.

Virtual lab — CBSE Practical 6: a leaf peel to show stomata

What you do:

  1. Fold a fresh leaf (for example Tradescantia, Bryophyllum or petunia) and gently pull off a thin, transparent strip of its lower epidermis with forceps.
  2. Put the peel in a watch-glass of water so it does not dry out.
  3. Stain it with a few drops of dilute safranin for 2–3 minutes; wash off the extra stain with water.
  4. Lift the peel onto a clean slide with a brush, add a drop of glycerine, and lower a coverslip slowly using a needle, so that no air bubbles are trapped. Blot off extra glycerine.
  5. Look under the low power of the microscope first, then high power.

What you observe:

Feature Observation
Epidermal cells Many irregular, closely fitting cells; no chloroplasts
Stomata Small pores scattered among the epidermal cells
Guard cells Two kidney- (bean-) shaped cells around each pore, each with a nucleus and chloroplasts

What it shows: The epidermis of a leaf has stomata, each bounded by a pair of guard cells. The lower epidermis is used because it usually has more stomata.

Why each step: water keeps the peel from drying; safranin stains the cells so they are easy to see; glycerine stops the mount drying out under the lights; lowering the coverslip at an angle keeps out air bubbles, which look like black-edged circles and hide the cells.

Safety

use forceps and the needle carefully; handle slides and coverslips by their edges — they break easily.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

The black-paper strip

Question. Riya kept a potted hibiscus in a dark cupboard for three days. She then clipped a strip of black paper across the middle of one leaf, covering both surfaces, and left the plant in bright sunlight for six hours. She tested that leaf for starch with iodine. (a) What will she see? (b) What does the experiment show? (c) Why did she keep the plant in the dark first?

Given: a destarched plant; one leaf partly covered; sunlight; iodine test. Asked: result, conclusion, reason for destarching.

Concept used: starch is made only where photosynthesis happens, and iodine turns starch blue-black (this topic). Why it applies: the only difference between the two parts of the leaf is light.

  1. 1

    (a) The uncovered parts received light → photosynthesis → starch → they turn blue-black.

  2. 2

    The covered strip received no light → no photosynthesis → no starch → it does not turn blue-black (it stays pale brown).

  3. 3

    (b) Everything else (chlorophyll, carbon dioxide, water) was the same in both parts; only light differed → sunlight is necessary for photosynthesis.

  4. 4

    (c) Three days in the dark use up the starch already in the leaf. Any starch found afterwards must have been made during the experiment.

  5. Check: the uncovered part acts as the control — it proves the leaf could make starch, so the absence of starch under the strip can only be due to the missing light ✔.

  6. Answer (a) Blue-black everywhere except the covered strip (b) sunlight is needed for photosynthesis (c) to remove old starch, so that any starch found was made during the experiment.

    Common trap: Forgetting to destarch. Without it, the covered strip would still contain yesterday's starch and turn blue-black, and the experiment would prove nothing.

Worked example 2

Stomata at noon in May

Question. On a hot, dry afternoon in May, a farmer's maize plants have most of their stomata closed. Early the next morning, with dew on the ground, the stomata are open. Explain both observations, including what happens to the guard cells.

Given: hot, dry noon → stomata closed; cool, moist morning → open. Asked: explanation in terms of guard cells.

Concept used: guard cells swell when water flows in (pore opens) and shrink when they lose water (pore closes); stomata lose water (this topic). Why it applies: the plant's water supply is very different at the two times.

  1. 1

    At hot, dry noon the plant is losing water fast and is short of it. Water leaves the guard cells.

  2. 2

    The guard cells shrink, so the stomatal pore closes.

  3. 3

    Closing the stomata cuts down water loss, which protects the plant from wilting — but it also lets in less carbon dioxide, so photosynthesis slows.

  4. 4

    In the cool, moist morning the plant has plenty of water. Water flows into the guard cells; they swell, and the pore opens.

  5. 5

    Open stomata let in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis now that light is available.

  6. Check: the direction matches the rule both times — water in → swell → open; water out → shrink → close ✔.

  7. Answer At noon the guard cells lose water and shrink, closing the pores and saving water; in the morning they take in water and swell, opening the pores so carbon dioxide can enter.

    Common trap: Reversing the mechanism ("guard cells swell to close the pore"). Swollen guard cells bow apart — the pore opens.

Reading the photosynthesis equation

Question. Use the equation for photosynthesis to answer: (a) Show that it is balanced. (b) Which raw material is split during the second event, and which product comes from it? (c) What is "reduced" in the third event, and to what?

    Concept used: atoms are conserved (Chapter 1); the three events of photosynthesis (this topic). Why it applies: the equation summarises the three events.

  2. 1

    (a) Carbon: left 6 × 1 = 6; right 6 (in glucose) = 6 ✔.

  3. 2

    Hydrogen: left 12 × 2 = 24; right 12 (glucose) + 6 × 2 (water) = 24 ✔.

  4. 3

    Oxygen: left 6 × 2 + 12 × 1 = 24; right 6 (glucose) + 6 × 2 (oxygen gas) + 6 × 1 (water) = 24 ✔.

  5. 4

    (b) In the second event water is split into hydrogen and oxygen; the oxygen gas given out comes from this.

  6. 5

    (c) Carbon dioxide is reduced — hydrogen from water is added to it, and oxygen is removed — to carbohydrate (glucose).

  7. Check: C 6 | 6, H 24 | 24, O 24 | 24 ✔. Water appears on both sides: 12 molecules used, 6 formed — that is why 12 are written on the left.

  8. Answer (a) C 6, H 24, O 24 on each side (b) water is split; it gives the oxygen released (c) carbon dioxide is reduced to carbohydrate (glucose).

    Common trap: Writing "chlorophyll + sunlight" as reactants with a plus sign. They are conditions, written over and under the arrow; they are not used up.

A student says, "A tree is alive because it moves its branches in the wind." Why is this a poor reason? Give a better one.

Common mistakes!

  • "Guard cells swell to close the stoma."

    The mechanism is reversed.

    Water in → guard cells swell → pore opens; water out → shrink → closes.

  • "Plants photosynthesise instead of respiring."

    Plants respire all the time; photosynthesis happens only in light.

    Photosynthesis (in light) and respiration (day and night).

  • "The oxygen released comes from carbon dioxide."

    Water is split in the second event.

    The oxygen comes from water.

  • Writing "+ chlorophyll + sunlight" as reactants

    They are not used up.

    Write them over and under the arrow.

  • "Iodine turns starch red/green."

    Iodine with starch gives blue-black.

    Blue-black = starch present.

  • Skipping destarching in the plan of an experiment

    Old starch gives a false result.

    Always keep the plant in the dark for about three days first.

  • "Heat the alcohol directly over the flame."

    Alcohol catches fire.

    Heat alcohol only in a water bath.

Key points

  • Visible movement is not enough to show life; living things show constant molecular movement to maintain their structure.
  • Life processes — nutrition, respiration, transport, excretion — do this maintenance job. Diffusion is not enough in multicellular organisms because most cells are far from the surface.
  • Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (+ water), in sunlight with chlorophyll (balanced equation in section 4); events: absorb light → convert to chemical energy and split water → reduce carbon dioxide; extra carbohydrate stored as starch.
  • Stomata: guard cells swell (water in) → open; shrink (water out) → close.
  • Experiments: variegated leaf → chlorophyll needed; KOH → carbon dioxide needed; black-paper strip → light needed. Always destarch first.
  • Practical 6: lower epidermis, safranin, glycerine, no air bubbles → kidney-shaped guard cells with chloroplasts round each pore.
