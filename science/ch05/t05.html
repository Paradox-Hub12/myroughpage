Transport in human beings

About 70 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe what blood carries (plasma, red blood corpuscles, platelets) and what lymph is and does.
  • Describe the structure of the heart and the flow of blood through its four chambers; explain double circulation and why oxygenated and deoxygenated blood are kept separate.
  • Compare arteries, veins and capillaries, and explain how platelets prevent blood loss.
  • Draw and label a sectional view of the human heart.

Your heart beats about 70 times a minute, every minute, for your whole life — more than two and a half billion beats by the age of seventy — without a single day off. Each beat pushes blood into two separate loops at once: a short one to the lungs and a long one to the rest of the body. Why two loops, and how does one fist-sized pump keep them from mixing?

  1. Blood — what it carries

Blood is a fluid connective tissue (Class 9). It has a fluid part, plasma, in which cells float.

Part What it carries or does
Plasma Carries food, carbon dioxide and nitrogenous wastes dissolved in it; also salts and many other substances
Red blood corpuscles (RBCs) Carry oxygen, using the pigment haemoglobin
Platelets Help blood clot at a wound
White blood cells (Class 9 reminder) Fight infection

To move blood round the body we need a pump (the heart), a network of tubes (blood vessels) to reach every tissue, and a way to repair the network if it leaks (platelets).

  1. The heart — a double pump

The heart is a muscular organ about the size of your fist. Blood must carry both oxygen and carbon dioxide, so the heart has separate chambers that keep oxygen-rich blood from mixing with carbon dioxide-rich blood.

It has four chambers: two thin-walled upper chambers, the atria (singular atrium), which receive blood; and two thick-walled lower chambers, the ventricles, which pump blood out. A wall, the septum, separates the right side from the left.

Follow the blood, step by step:

  1. Oxygen-rich blood from the lungs enters the left atrium through the pulmonary veins. The left atrium relaxes while it collects this blood.
  2. The left atrium contracts while the left ventricle relaxes, so blood passes into the left ventricle.
  3. The muscular left ventricle contracts and pumps the blood out through the aorta to the body.
  4. Deoxygenated blood from the body returns through the vena cava (from the upper and lower body) to the right atrium, which relaxes as it fills.
  5. The right atrium contracts while the right ventricle dilates, so blood passes into the right ventricle.
  6. The right ventricle pumps it through the pulmonary artery to the lungs, where it picks up oxygen — and the cycle repeats.

Ventricles have thicker muscular walls than atria, because they must pump blood out to organs. Valves between the chambers (and at the exits) stop blood from flowing backwards whenever a chamber contracts.

Drawing tip: a sectional view shows the heart as if it were in someone facing you. So the heart's right side is on the left of your page.

  1. The vessel check — artery or vein?

Vessel From To Blood
Aorta (artery) left ventricle body oxygenated
Vena cava (vein) body right atrium deoxygenated
Pulmonary artery right ventricle lungs deoxygenated
Pulmonary vein lungs left atrium oxygenated

An artery is any vessel that carries blood away from the heart; a vein carries it towards the heart. It is the direction that decides the name, not the oxygen. That is why the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood and the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood.

  1. Double circulation — and why it matters

Keeping the two sides apart gives a highly efficient supply of oxygen to the body. This matters most in animals with high energy needs — birds and mammals, which constantly use energy to keep their body temperature steady.

Animal group Heart chambers What happens
Birds, mammals 4 Right and left sides fully separate; no mixing
Amphibians, many reptiles 3 Some mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood is tolerated; their body temperature depends on the surroundings
Fishes 2 Blood is pumped to the gills, oxygenated there, and goes straight on to the body — it passes through the heart only once per cycle

In humans (and other vertebrates except fishes), blood passes through the heart twice in each complete cycle of the body: once through the right side (body → heart → lungs) and once through the left side (lungs → heart → body). This is double circulation — two separate circuits, the pulmonary circuit and the body circuit, with the heart pumping into both.

  1. The tubes — blood vessels

Arteries Veins Capillaries
Carry blood away from the heart to organs from organs back to the heart between arteries and veins, inside organs
Pressure high low low
Walls thick, elastic thin one cell thick
Valves none (except where they leave the heart) valves keep blood flowing one way none
Job withstand high pressure return blood exchange of materials with cells

Inside an organ, an artery branches again and again into finer vessels, ending in capillaries, whose thin walls let materials pass between blood and cells. Capillaries then join up to form veins.

  1. Repairs — platelets

If a vessel is cut, blood is lost, and the loss of pressure would make the pump less efficient. Platelets circulate in the blood and plug the leak by helping the blood clot at the injury.

  1. Lymph — the second transport fluid

Some plasma, proteins and blood cells escape through pores in the capillary walls into the spaces between cells, forming tissue fluid or lymph. Lymph is like plasma but colourless, with less protein. It drains into lymphatic capillaries, which join into larger lymph vessels that open into large veins. Lymph carries digested and absorbed fat from the intestine and drains excess fluid from the spaces between cells back into the blood.

NCERT information box

Blood pressure is the push of blood against the wall of a vessel — far higher in arteries than in veins. Pressure during ventricular contraction (systolic) is normally about 120 mm of Hg, and during relaxation (diastolic) about 80 mm of Hg. It is measured with a sphygmomanometer. High blood pressure (hypertension) is caused by narrowed arterioles and can lead to a burst artery and internal bleeding.

Worked example 1

One red blood cell's journey

Question. A red blood cell has just given up its oxygen in a capillary of the big toe. Trace its path until it gives up oxygen in the toe again, naming every chamber and main vessel in order and saying where it becomes oxygenated.

Given: start — deoxygenated, in a toe capillary. Asked: the full path, in order.

Concept used: blood flow through the heart and double circulation (this topic). Why it applies: one complete cycle must pass through both circuits.

  1. 1

    Toe capillaries → small veins → vena cava (from the lower body).

  2. 2

    Right atrium → (atrium contracts) → right ventricle.

  3. 3

    Right ventricle contracts → pulmonary artery → lung capillaries round the alveoli.

  4. 4

    In the lungs the cell picks up oxygen (and the blood gives up carbon dioxide) → it is now oxygenated.

  5. 5

    Pulmonary vein → left atrium → (atrium contracts) → left ventricle.

  6. 6

    Left ventricle contracts → aorta → smaller arteries → capillaries of the big toe, where it gives up its oxygen.

  7. Check: the cell passed through the heart twice — right side (steps 2–3) and left side (step 5) — which is exactly what double circulation means ✔. Every vessel taking blood away from the heart was an artery; every vessel bringing it back was a vein ✔.

  8. Answer Toe → vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs (oxygenated) → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → arteries → toe capillaries.

    Common trap: Sending blood from the right ventricle straight to the body. The right side always pumps to the lungs; only the left ventricle pumps to the body.

Worked example 2

A leaky valve

Question. A doctor tells Mr Thomas that the valve between his left atrium and left ventricle does not close properly. (a) What normally happens at this valve when the left ventricle contracts? (b) What goes wrong now? (c) Why might he feel tired and short of breath on climbing stairs?

Given: a faulty valve between the left atrium and left ventricle. Asked: normal role; fault; effect.

Concept used: valves stop blood flowing backwards when chambers contract; the left ventricle pumps oxygenated blood to the body (this topic). Why it applies: this valve sits on the path of oxygen-rich blood.

  1. 1

    (a) When the left ventricle contracts, the valve closes, so all the blood is forced forwards into the aorta and none flows back into the left atrium.

  2. 2

    (b) With a leaky valve, some blood is pushed backwards into the left atrium at every beat.

  3. 3

    So less oxygenated blood goes out through the aorta to the body with each beat, and the heart has to work harder to make up.

  4. 4

    (c) On the stairs his muscles need extra oxygen; the heart cannot deliver enough, so he tires quickly. Blood also backs up towards the lungs, which can make breathing harder.

  5. Check: the effect follows from the valve's single job — one-way flow; nothing else in the heart has changed ✔.

  6. Answer (a) It closes to stop backflow into the left atrium (b) blood leaks back at each beat, so less oxygenated blood reaches the body (c) his muscles get too little oxygen during effort.

    Common trap: Saying "oxygenated and deoxygenated blood will mix". This valve lies between two left-side chambers, which both hold oxygenated blood — the problem is backflow, not mixing.

Worked example 3

Why a scratch stops bleeding

Question. Shruti scrapes her knee in a fall. It bleeds for a few minutes, then stops on its own. (a) Which blood component stopped the bleeding, and how? (b) Give two reasons why stopping the leak matters to the body.

Given: a small wound that stops bleeding by itself. Asked: component and action; two reasons.

Concept used: maintenance by platelets (this topic). Why it applies: the wound is a leak in the system of tubes.

  1. 1

    (a) Platelets in the circulating blood gather at the injury and help the blood clot, which plugs the leak.

  2. 2

    (b) Reason 1: it keeps the loss of blood to a minimum.

  3. 3

    Reason 2: a leak would cause a loss of pressure in the system, reducing the efficiency of the heart's pumping.

  4. Check: both reasons come from what the circulation needs to work — enough blood and enough pressure ✔.

  5. Answer (a) Platelets help the blood clot and plug the leak (b) to minimise blood loss and to prevent a fall in pressure that would make pumping less efficient.

    Common trap: Crediting the red blood cells or white blood cells. RBCs carry oxygen and WBCs fight germs; platelets stop the bleeding.

Why are the walls of the ventricles thicker than the walls of the atria?

No chamber stores blood; each passes it on with every beat.
Ventricles must pump blood to the lungs or the whole body, which needs strong, thick muscle.
It is the atria that receive blood from the veins.
The right ventricle holds deoxygenated blood.
Quick check · 3 of 4Short answer2 marks

Give one structural difference between arteries and veins, and explain how it suits each one's job.

Answer: Arteries have thick, elastic walls because blood leaves the heart under high pressure; veins have thin walls because their blood is no longer under pressure, and they have valves so that blood flows only in one direction (towards the heart).

Always link the structure to the pressure (or to one-way flow) — that link earns the explanation mark.

Common mistakes!

  • "The pulmonary vein carries deoxygenated blood."

    It carries blood from the lungs, just oxygenated.

    Pulmonary vein → oxygenated; pulmonary artery → deoxygenated.

  • "Arteries always carry oxygenated blood."

    Arteries are defined by direction, not oxygen.

    Artery = away from the heart; vein = towards it.

  • Right and left sides swapped in the drawing

    The textbook view faces you.

    Heart's right side on the left of the page; the thickest wall (left ventricle) on the right of the page.

  • "The right ventricle pumps blood to the body."

    It pumps to the lungs.

    Right side → lungs; left side → body.

  • "Double circulation means blood goes round the body twice."

    It means blood passes through the heart twice in one cycle.

    Two separate circuits — lungs and body — each passing through one side of the heart.

  • "Fish have double circulation."

    Fish blood passes through the heart once per cycle.

    Fish: 2 chambers, single circulation (heart → gills → body).

  • "Valves are only in the heart."

    Veins also have valves.

    Valves stop backflow in the heart and in veins.

  • "Lymph is red / is the same as plasma."

    Lymph is colourless and has less protein than plasma.

    Lymph: colourless, less protein than plasma.

Key points

  • Plasma carries food, COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ and nitrogenous wastes dissolved; RBCs (haemoglobin) carry oxygen; platelets clot blood.
  • Heart: 4 chambers; atria receive, ventricles pump; ventricle walls thicker; valves prevent backflow; septum keeps the sides apart.
  • Right side: vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs. Left side: pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body.
  • Double circulation: blood passes through the heart twice per cycle; no mixing → efficient oxygen supply for warm-blooded birds and mammals. Amphibians/many reptiles: 3 chambers; fishes: 2 chambers, single circulation.
  • Arteries (away; thick, elastic; high pressure) · veins (towards; thin; valves) · capillaries (one cell thick; exchange).
  • Lymph: colourless, less protein; carries absorbed fat from the intestine; returns excess tissue fluid to the blood.
