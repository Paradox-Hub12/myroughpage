Intext p.81, Q1Why diffusion is not enough2 marks

Why is diffusion insufficient to meet the oxygen requirements of multi-cellular organisms like humans?

Given: a large, multicellular organism (a human). Asked: why diffusion alone cannot supply its oxygen.

Concept used: diffusion is fast over tiny distances but very slow over long ones; in multicellular organisms most cells are not in contact with the surroundings (Topics 1 and 2). Why it applies: oxygen would have to reach cells deep inside the body.

  1. 1

    Most cells are not in direct contact with the air → in a human, cells lie deep inside the body, far from the skin and lungs.

  2. 2

    Diffusion is too slow over such distances → it works over the width of a cell, not across a body many centimetres thick. (It has been estimated that diffusion would take years to move oxygen from the lungs to the toes.)

  3. 3

    The body's oxygen demand is large → humans are big and active, so many cells need oxygen at the same time.

  4. 4

    So humans need lungs with a large surface to take in oxygen, haemoglobin to carry it and a heart and blood vessels to deliver it to every cell.

  5. Check: compare with Amoeba — every part of a single cell is close to the water outside, so diffusion is enough there ✔.

  6. Answer In humans most cells are far from the body surface and not in contact with air; diffusion over these distances is far too slow to supply enough oxygen to the large, active body. So a respiratory system and a circulatory system are needed.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • In multicellular organisms like humans, all the cells are not in direct contact with the surrounding environment/air. (1)
    • Diffusion is very slow over large distances and cannot meet the oxygen demand of the large body, so special organs (lungs) and a transport system (blood) are needed. (1)
    Common trap: Writing only "the body is big". Say why size matters: cells are far from the surface, and diffusion is slow over long distances.
Intext p.81, Q2How do we decide something is alive?2 marks

What criteria do we use to decide whether something is alive?

Given: the question of what counts as "alive". Asked: the criteria.

Concept used: visible movement is not a reliable sign of life; molecular movement is (Topic 2). Why it applies: this is the textbook's argument in §5.1.

  1. 1

    Visible movement — running, breathing, growing — is the everyday sign we use first.

  2. 2

    But it is not enough → a plant that is not visibly growing is alive, and some animals breathe with no visible movement.

  3. 3

    Molecular movement is the real criterion → living structures are highly organised and keep breaking down; to stay alive, organisms must constantly repair and maintain them, which means moving molecules all the time.

  4. 4

    Life processes — nutrition, respiration, transport, excretion — carry out this maintenance; their presence shows that something is alive. (Viruses show no molecular movement until they infect a cell, which is why their status is debated.)

  5. Check: a sleeping dog shows no running but is still alive because its life processes continue — consistent with point 4 ✔.

  6. Answer We first look for movement (growth, breathing), but visible movement is not enough. The deciding criterion is molecular movement — the constant maintenance and repair of the body's organised structure through life processes such as nutrition, respiration, transport and excretion.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • Visible movement (growth-related or not) is commonly used, but it is not enough, since some living things show no visible movement. (1)
    • The criterion is molecular movement: living organisms continuously carry out life processes (nutrition, respiration, etc.) to maintain and repair their structures. (1)
    Common trap: Stopping at "it moves and grows". The textbook's point is that visible movement is not a sufficient test.
Intext p.81, Q3Uses of outside raw materials2 marks

What are outside raw materials used for by an organism?

Given: raw materials taken in from outside. Asked: what they are used for.

Concept used: nutrition supplies energy and materials; respiration uses oxygen to break down food (Topic 2). Why it applies: all raw materials taken in serve these purposes.

  1. 1

    Energy → food is broken down (often using oxygen from outside) to release energy for maintenance and all activities.

  2. 2

    Growth → raw materials are used to build the body as it grows (new cells, proteins and other substances).

  3. 3

    Repair and maintenance → worn-out structures are rebuilt.

  4. 4

    Examples: plants take in carbon dioxide, water and minerals (e.g. nitrogen to make proteins); animals take in food, water and oxygen.

  5. Check: each use matches a life process — energy (nutrition + respiration), growth and repair (nutrition) ✔.

  6. Answer Outside raw materials (food, water, oxygen, carbon dioxide, minerals) are used to obtain energy (breaking down food, usually with oxygen) and to provide materials for growth, repair and maintenance of the body, e.g. making proteins.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • Source of energy: food is broken down (using oxygen) to release energy for life processes. (1)
    • Building and maintenance: materials for growth, synthesis of proteins and repair of tissues. (1)
    Common trap: Giving only "for energy". Growth and repair need materials too.
Intext p.81, Q4Processes essential for life2 marks

What processes would you consider essential for maintaining life?

Given: the maintenance of life. Asked: the essential processes.

Concept used: life processes (Topic 2). Why it applies: these are the processes that together do the maintenance job.

  1. 1

    Nutrition → brings in energy and raw materials as food.

  2. 2

    Respiration → breaks down food (using oxygen) to release energy.

  3. 3

    Transport → carries food, oxygen and wastes to and from every cell.

  4. 4

    Excretion → removes harmful waste by-products.

  5. Check: the four match the four rows of the wrap-up's Card 1 ✔.

  6. Answer Nutrition, respiration, transport and excretion.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • Nutrition, respiration, transportation and excretion — ½ mark each.
    Common trap: Listing "breathing" instead of respiration. Breathing only moves air; respiration releases energy in cells.

What are the differences between autotrophic nutrition and heterotrophic nutrition?

Given: two modes of nutrition. Asked: the differences.

Concept used: autotrophs make food from simple inorganic substances; heterotrophs take in complex food and break it down with enzymes (Topics 2 and 3). Why it applies: each row compares the same point for both modes.

  1. Point Autotrophic nutrition Heterotrophic nutrition
    Food source makes its own food takes in food made by other organisms
    Raw materials simple inorganic substances — carbon dioxide and water complex organic substances
    Chlorophyll and sunlight needed not needed
    Breaking down food not needed for intake complex food broken down by enzymes
    Examples green plants, some bacteria animals, fungi

  2. Check: each row compares the same point for both modes — this is what earns the marks ✔.

  3. Answer Autotrophs make their own food from simple inorganic materials (COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ and water) using sunlight and chlorophyll (green plants, some bacteria); heterotrophs take in complex food made by others and break it down with enzymes (animals, fungi).

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    Any three matched differences from the table — 1 mark each (½ for each side).

    Common trap: Writing points that do not match ("autotrophs make food; heterotrophs have teeth"). Each row must compare the same feature.

Intext p.87, Q3The acid in the stomach2 marks

What is the role of the acid in our stomach?

Given: hydrochloric acid made by the gastric glands. Asked: its role.

Concept used: digestion in the stomach (Topic 3). Why it applies: the acid is one of three secretions of the gastric glands.

  1. 1

    Creates an acidic medium → the protein-digesting enzyme pepsin acts only in an acidic medium, so the acid lets pepsin digest proteins.

  2. 2

    Kills germs → the acid destroys many harmful bacteria that enter the stomach along with food (the "other function" the textbook asks you to think about).

  3. Check: the stomach's lining is protected from this acid by mucus — which shows the acid is strong enough to need protection against ✔.

  4. Answer Hydrochloric acid makes the medium in the stomach acidic, which is needed for the enzyme pepsin to digest proteins; it also kills many germs that come in with food.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • Creates an acidic medium that facilitates the action of the enzyme pepsin (protein digestion). (1)
    • Kills harmful bacteria/germs present in food. (1)
    Common trap: "The acid digests food." Hydrochloric acid is not an enzyme; it provides the medium in which pepsin works.
Intext p.87, Q4Function of digestive enzymes2 marks

What is the function of digestive enzymes?

Given: digestive enzymes. Asked: their function.

Concept used: enzymes are biological catalysts; food must be broken into small molecules to be absorbed (Topics 1 and 3). Why it applies: digestion is the breaking down of food.

  1. 1

    They break down complex food molecules into simple ones → large molecules cannot pass through the wall of the intestine.

  2. 2

    They speed up digestion → as biological catalysts, they make reactions that would otherwise take far too long happen quickly.

  3. 3

    Examples: salivary amylase — starch → sugar; pepsin and trypsin — proteins; lipase — fats; intestinal juice — proteins → amino acids, carbohydrates → glucose, fats → fatty acids + glycerol.

  4. 4

    The simple products are then absorbed by the villi into the blood.

  5. Check against the enzyme table (Topic 3): every enzyme listed turns a large food molecule into smaller ones ✔.

  6. Answer Digestive enzymes are biological catalysts that break down complex food molecules (starch, proteins, fats) into simple, soluble molecules (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol) that can be absorbed into the blood.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • Enzymes (biocatalysts) break down complex food into simpler, absorbable molecules. (1)
    • Any one example, e.g. salivary amylase converts starch into sugar. (1)
    Common trap: "Enzymes absorb food." Enzymes digest; villi absorb.
Intext p.87, Q5How the small intestine absorbs food3 marks

How is the small intestine designed to absorb digested food?

Given: the small intestine. Asked: its design for absorption.

Concept used: villi and blood supply (Topic 3). Why it applies: absorption depends on surface area and on carrying food away.

  1. 1

    Very long and coiled → it is the longest part of the canal, packed into a small space by extensive coiling, so food spends a long time in contact with its wall.

  2. 2

    Villi → its inner lining has numerous finger-like projections that greatly increase the surface area for absorption.

  3. 3

    Rich blood supply → the villi are richly supplied with blood vessels, which take the absorbed food to every cell of the body. (Absorbed fat is carried by lymph.)

  4. 4

    Digestion is completed here → the food is already broken into small, absorbable molecules when it reaches the villi.

  5. Check: compare with the villus in the visual for Topic 3 — finger-like fold, blood vessels inside ✔.

  6. Answer The small intestine is very long and coiled, and its inner lining has numerous finger-like villi that increase the surface area for absorption; the villi are richly supplied with blood vessels that carry the absorbed food to all body cells.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    • Inner lining has numerous finger-like projections called villi. (1)
    • Villi increase the surface area for absorption. (1)
    • Villi are richly supplied with blood vessels that carry absorbed food to every cell. (1)
    Common trap: Writing "villi digest food". They absorb it.

Intext p.91, Q1Land vs water for getting oxygen2 marks

What advantage over an aquatic organism does a terrestrial organism have with regard to obtaining oxygen for respiration?

Given: a land organism and a water organism. Asked: the land organism's advantage in getting oxygen.

Concept used: dissolved oxygen in water is much lower than oxygen in air (Topic 4). Why it applies: the two organisms take oxygen from these different sources.

  1. 1

    Air is rich in oxygen → a terrestrial organism breathes atmospheric oxygen, which is far more plentiful than the oxygen dissolved in water.

  2. 2

    Water has little dissolved oxygen → an aquatic organism must pass large volumes of water over its gills.

  3. 3

    So aquatic organisms must breathe much faster → spending more energy on breathing.

  4. 4

    Advantage of the land organism: it gets the oxygen it needs with a slower breathing rate and less effort.

  5. Check: NCERT Activity 5.6 — a fish opens and closes its mouth and gills far more often per minute than we breathe ✔.

  6. Answer A terrestrial organism takes oxygen from air, which contains much more oxygen than the dissolved oxygen in water. So it can obtain enough oxygen with a much slower breathing rate, whereas aquatic organisms must breathe much faster.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • Terrestrial organisms use atmospheric oxygen, which is far more than the oxygen dissolved in water. (1)
    • Hence they need a slower rate of breathing; aquatic organisms must breathe much faster to get enough oxygen. (1)
    Common trap: Writing "land animals breathe faster". It is the aquatic animals that breathe faster.
Intext p.91, Q2Ways of oxidising glucose3 marks

What are the different ways in which glucose is oxidised to provide energy in various organisms?

Given: glucose broken down in different organisms. Asked: the different pathways.

Concept used: breakdown of glucose by various pathways (Topic 4, NCERT Fig. 5.8). Why it applies: the three pathways share the first step and differ after pyruvate.

  1. 1

    First step, common to all: glucose (6-carbon) → pyruvate (3-carbon) + energy, in the cytoplasm.

  2. 2

    Absence of oxygen, in yeast → pyruvate → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy. This is anaerobic respiration (fermentation).

  3. 3

    Lack of oxygen, in our muscle cells → pyruvate → lactic acid + energy (its build-up causes cramps).

  4. 4

    Presence of oxygen, in mitochondria → pyruvate → carbon dioxide + water + energy. This is aerobic respiration, which releases much more energy.

Intext p.91, Q3Transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide2 marks

How is oxygen and carbon dioxide transported in human beings?

Given: the two respiratory gases. Asked: how each is carried.

Concept used: respiratory pigment haemoglobin; solubility of carbon dioxide (Topic 4). Why it applies: the two gases travel in different ways.

  1. 1

    Oxygen → carried by haemoglobin, a respiratory pigment in the red blood corpuscles. It has a very high affinity for oxygen: it takes up oxygen in the lungs and releases it in tissues that are short of oxygen.

  2. 2

    Carbon dioxide → more soluble in water than oxygen, so it is carried mostly dissolved in the blood (in the plasma), from the tissues to the lungs, where it is released into the alveoli.

  3. Check: in the double-circulation schematic (Topic 5), oxygen is picked up in the lung capillaries and given up in the body capillaries; carbon dioxide does the reverse ✔.

  4. Answer Oxygen is carried by haemoglobin in red blood corpuscles, which has a high affinity for oxygen. Carbon dioxide, being more soluble in water, is carried mostly dissolved in the blood plasma.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • Oxygen: bound to haemoglobin (high affinity for OX2\ce{O2}OX2​) present in RBCs, carried from lungs to tissues. (1)
    • Carbon dioxide: more soluble in water, so transported mainly in dissolved form in blood (plasma). (1)
    Common trap: Saying "haemoglobin carries both gases equally". In this chapter: haemoglobin → oxygen; carbon dioxide → mostly dissolved.
Intext p.91, Q4How lungs maximise surface area3 marks

How are the lungs designed in human beings to maximise the area for exchange of gases?

Given: human lungs. Asked: design features that give a large exchange area.

Concept used: the respiratory tree and alveoli (Topic 4). Why it applies: gas exchange happens across a surface, so more area means faster exchange.

  1. 1

    Branching air passages → inside the lungs, the passage divides into smaller and smaller tubes (bronchi → bronchioles), reaching every part of the lungs.

  2. 2

    Alveoli → the tubes end in millions of balloon-like alveoli, which provide a very large surface for gas exchange (spread out, about 80 m²).

  3. 3

    Rich blood supply → the walls of the alveoli contain an extensive network of blood vessels, so blood is in contact with almost the whole surface.

  4. 4

    Thin walls → gases diffuse across only a very short distance.

  5. Check against the respiratory-system diagram (Topic 4): trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → clusters of alveoli ✔.

  6. Answer The air passages branch into smaller and smaller tubes ending in millions of balloon-like alveoli, which give a very large surface area; the alveolar walls are thin and contain an extensive network of blood vessels for exchange of gases.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    • Air tubes divide into smaller tubes (bronchi, bronchioles) ending in balloon-like alveoli. (1)
    • Large number of alveoli provide a large surface area for gaseous exchange. (1)
    • Walls of alveoli are thin and have an extensive network of blood capillaries. (1)
    Common trap: Describing breathing movements (ribs, diaphragm). The question is about surface area, so focus on alveoli and blood vessels.

What are the components of the transport system in human beings? What are the functions of these components?

  2. Check: the components cover the three needs named in the textbook — a pump, a network of tubes, and a way to repair leaks ✔.

  3. Answer The heart (pump), blood vessels — arteries, veins, capillaries (tubes), blood — plasma, RBCs, platelets (the carrying fluid), and lymph. Functions as in the table.

    • Heart — pumps blood. (½) · Blood vessels — arteries (away from heart), veins (to heart), capillaries (exchange). (1)
    • Blood — plasma carries food, COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​, nitrogenous wastes; RBCs carry OX2\ce{O2}OX2​; platelets help clotting. (1)

Intext p.96, Q2Why keep the two kinds of blood apart?2 marks

Why is it necessary to separate oxygenated and deoxygenated blood in mammals and birds?

Given: mammals and birds. Asked: why the two kinds of blood are kept separate.

Concept used: double circulation and high energy needs (Topic 5). Why it applies: the separation makes oxygen supply efficient.

  1. 1

    Separation prevents mixing → the right and left sides of the heart are separated, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix.

  2. 2

    This gives a highly efficient supply of oxygen → blood reaching the body carries the maximum amount of oxygen.

  3. 3

    Mammals and birds have high energy needs → they constantly use energy to maintain their body temperature, so they need a lot of oxygen for respiration.

  4. Check: contrast — amphibians and many reptiles, whose body temperature depends on the surroundings, have three-chambered hearts and tolerate some mixing ✔.

  5. Answer Mammals and birds have high energy needs because they constantly use energy to keep their body temperature constant. Keeping oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate gives a highly efficient supply of oxygen to the body to meet this need.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • Separation of the right and left sides of the heart prevents mixing, allowing a highly efficient supply of oxygen to the body. (1)
    • Birds and mammals have high energy needs as they constantly use energy to maintain body temperature. (1)
    Common trap: Writing "so that blood does not flow backwards". That is the job of valves, not of the separation.
Intext p.96, Q3Transport system in plants2 marks

What are the components of the transport system in highly organised plants?

Given: highly organised (vascular) plants. Asked: the components of the transport system.

Concept used: vascular tissue — xylem and phloem (Topic 6). Why it applies: these are the plant's two independent conducting systems.

  1. 1

    Xylem → made of vessels and tracheids, joined into continuous channels; transports water and minerals from the roots upwards.

  2. 2

    Phloem → made of sieve tubes with companion cells; transports food (products of photosynthesis) and other substances, upwards and downwards.

  3. Check against the xylem–phloem table (Topic 6): two tissues, two jobs ✔.

  4. Answer The vascular tissue: xylem (vessels and tracheids — water and minerals) and phloem (sieve tubes and companion cells — food).

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    • Xylem — conducts water and minerals from roots to other parts. (1)
    • Phloem — transports food (products of photosynthesis) from leaves to other parts. (1)
    Common trap: Adding "veins and arteries" — plants have no blood vessels.
Intext p.96, Q4Transport of water and minerals3 marks

How are water and minerals transported in plants?

Given: water and minerals in plants. Asked: how they are transported.

Concept used: xylem; root pressure; transpiration pull (Topic 6). Why it applies: both forces act on the water column in xylem.

  1. 1

    Xylem channels → vessels and tracheids of roots, stems and leaves form a continuous system of water-conducting channels.

  2. 2

    Root pressure → root cells in contact with the soil actively take up ions; this creates a concentration difference between root and soil, so water moves into the root, creating a column of water that is pushed upwards. This matters more at night.

  3. 3

    Transpiration pull → water evaporates from leaf cells through the stomata; it is replaced from the leaf xylem, creating a suction that pulls water up from the roots. This is the main force by day.

  4. 4

    Minerals travel dissolved in the water.

  5. Check against the tree diagram (Topic 6): push at the roots, pull at the leaves, one continuous column in xylem ✔.

  6. Answer Water and dissolved minerals move upwards through the continuous xylem channels, pushed by root pressure (roots take up ions actively, water follows) — important at night — and pulled by transpiration pull (evaporation from leaves creates suction) — the main force during the day.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    • Xylem vessels and tracheids form a continuous channel from roots to leaves. (1)
    • Root cells actively take up ions → water enters roots → column of water pushed up (root pressure). (1)
    • Evaporation of water from leaves (transpiration) creates suction that pulls water up — major force during the day. (1)
    Common trap: Mentioning only transpiration. The root-pressure push is part of the textbook's answer too.
Intext p.96, Q5Transport of food3 marks

How is food transported in plants?

Given: food made in leaves. Asked: how it is transported.

Concept used: translocation in phloem (Topic 6). Why it applies: soluble products of photosynthesis move through phloem.

  1. 1

    Translocation → the transport of soluble products of photosynthesis (and amino acids and other substances) is called translocation; it occurs in the phloem.

  2. 2

    Sieve tubes and companion cells → translocation takes place in the sieve tubes with the help of adjacent companion cells.

  3. 3

    Uses energy → material such as sucrose is loaded into the phloem using energy from ATP; this raises the osmotic pressure, so water moves in.

  4. 4

    Pressure moves the food → the pressure pushes material to tissues with lower pressure — in both upward and downward directions, according to the plant's needs (e.g. to buds in spring, to storage organs such as roots, fruits and seeds).

  5. Check: compare with xylem — xylem works by physical forces, phloem by ATP energy ✔.

  6. Answer Food is transported by translocation in the phloem (sieve tubes with companion cells). Sucrose is loaded into the phloem using ATP; water enters by osmosis, raising the pressure, which moves food to tissues of lower pressure — both upwards and downwards, as the plant needs.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    • Translocation of food occurs through phloem (sieve tubes with companion cells). (1)
    • Sucrose transferred into phloem using energy from ATP → osmotic pressure increases → water enters. (1)
    • Pressure moves material to tissues with lower pressure, in both directions, according to need. (1)
    Common trap: Saying "food moves down by gravity". It moves both ways, driven by pressure created using ATP.

Intext p.98, Q1Structure and function of nephrons5 marks

Describe the structure and functioning of nephrons.

Given: the nephron. Asked: its structure and how it works.

Concept used: the nephron as the filtration unit; filtration and selective reabsorption (Topic 7). Why it applies: each structure has a job in making urine.

  1. 1

    Structure

    Glomerulus → a cluster of very thin-walled blood capillaries, supplied by a branch of the renal artery.

  2. 2

    Bowman's capsule → the cup-shaped end of a coiled tube, surrounding the glomerulus; it collects the filtrate.

  3. 3

    Tubular part → a long, coiled tube leading from the capsule, surrounded by capillaries that join into a branch of the renal vein.

  4. 4

    Collecting duct → the tubule opens into it; it carries urine towards the ureter.

  5. 5

    Functioning

    Filtration → blood is filtered in the glomerulus; water, urea and other small substances (glucose, amino acids, salts) pass into Bowman's capsule as the initial filtrate.

  6. 6

    Selective reabsorption → as the filtrate flows along the tubule, glucose, amino acids, salts and a major amount of water are reabsorbed into the surrounding capillaries.

  7. 7

    Urine → what remains, containing nitrogenous wastes such as urea, flows into the collecting duct and on to the ureter.

  8. Check against the nephron diagram (Topic 7): filtration happens where the glomerulus sits in the capsule; reabsorption happens along the tubule with its capillaries; urine leaves by the collecting duct ✔.

  9. Answer A nephron consists of a glomerulus (thin-walled capillary cluster) inside a cup-shaped Bowman's capsule, leading into a long coiled tubule surrounded by capillaries and opening into a collecting duct. Blood is filtered from the glomerulus into the capsule; useful substances (glucose, amino acids, salts, much water) are selectively reabsorbed in the tubule; the rest forms urine.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    • Labelled diagram of a nephron (glomerulus, Bowman's capsule, tubule, collecting duct, renal artery and vein branches). (2)
    • Structure: glomerulus = cluster of thin-walled capillaries in cup-shaped Bowman's capsule; long coiled tubule. (1)
    • Filtration of blood in glomerulus/Bowman's capsule — filtrate collected. (1)
    • Selective reabsorption of glucose, amino acids, salts and water in the tubule; remaining fluid is urine. (1)
    Common trap: Placing filtration in the tubule. Filtration — glomerulus/Bowman's capsule; reabsorption — tubule.

What are the methods used by plants to get rid of excretory products?

Concept used: excretion in plants (Topic 7). Why it applies: plants use strategies quite different from animals.

    Any six methods — ½ mark each.

Intext p.98, Q3Regulating the amount of urine2 marks

How is the amount of urine produced regulated?

Given: urine formation in the nephrons. Asked: how its amount is controlled.

Concept used: selective reabsorption of water in the tubule (Topic 7). Why it applies: the volume of urine is set by how much water is taken back.

  1. 1

    Reabsorption decides the volume → the amount of urine depends on how much water is reabsorbed in the tubules.

  2. 2

    Factor 1: excess water in the body → if there is plenty of excess water, less is reabsorbed and more, dilute urine is formed; if the body is short of water (e.g. after sweating), more is reabsorbed and less, concentrated urine is formed.

  3. 3

    Factor 2: dissolved waste → the amount of dissolved waste to be excreted also affects how much water is used to carry it out.

  4. Check: consistent with the hot-day example in Topic 7 — sweating → more reabsorption → less urine ✔.

  5. Answer The amount of urine is regulated by the amount of water reabsorbed in the kidney tubules, which depends on how much excess water there is in the body and how much dissolved waste has to be excreted.

    • It depends on the excess water in the body and the amount of dissolved waste to be excreted. (1)
    Common trap: "By changing the amount of filtration." It is the reabsorption of water that is adjusted.

Exercise Q5Digestion of fats3 marks

How are fats digested in our bodies? Where does this process take place?

Given: fats in food. Asked: how they are digested, and where.

Concept used: bile, pancreatic lipase and intestinal juice (Topic 3). Why it applies: fat digestion uses all three in the small intestine.

  1. 1

    Site → fats are digested in the small intestine (your textbook describes no fat digestion before this).

  2. 2

    Bile makes the medium alkaline → bile juice from the liver makes the acidic food from the stomach alkaline, so that pancreatic enzymes can act.

  3. 3

    Bile salts emulsify fats → fats are present as large globules, which are hard for enzymes to act on; bile salts break them into smaller globules, increasing the efficiency of enzyme action (like soap acting on dirt).

  4. 4

    Lipase digests them → lipase in pancreatic juice breaks down the emulsified fats.

  5. 5

    Intestinal juice finishes → enzymes in the intestinal juice convert fats into fatty acids and glycerol, which are absorbed.

  6. Check against the enzyme table (Topic 3): bile (no enzyme) → lipase → intestinal juice → fatty acids + glycerol, all in the small intestine ✔.

  7. Answer Fats are digested in the small intestine. Bile from the liver makes the medium alkaline and its bile salts emulsify large fat globules into small ones; lipase from the pancreas breaks down the emulsified fats; the intestinal juice finally converts them into fatty acids and glycerol.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    • Site: small intestine. (½)
    • Bile salts (from liver) break large fat globules into smaller globules — emulsification. (1)
    • Pancreatic lipase breaks down emulsified fats. (1)
    • Intestinal juice converts fats into fatty acids and glycerol. (½)
    Common trap: "Bile digests fats." Bile only emulsifies; lipase digests.

Exercise Q11Double circulation5 marks

Describe double circulation of blood in human beings. Why is it necessary?

Given: human circulation. Asked: description of double circulation; why it is needed.

Concept used: flow through the four chambers; two circuits (Topic 5). Why it applies: blood passes through the heart twice in each cycle.

  1. 1

    Description

    Deoxygenated blood from the body enters the right atrium through the vena cava.

  2. 2

    The right atrium contracts → blood goes into the right ventricle.

  3. 3

    The right ventricle pumps it through the pulmonary artery to the lungs, where it gives up carbon dioxide and takes up oxygen (pulmonary circuit).

  4. 4

    Oxygenated blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium.

  5. 5

    The left atrium contracts → blood goes into the left ventricle.

  6. 6

    The left ventricle pumps it through the aorta to all parts of the body, where it gives up oxygen (body circuit); deoxygenated blood returns to the right atrium.

  7. 7

    So in one complete cycle, blood passes through the heart twice → double circulation.

  8. 8

    Why it is necessary

    The right and left sides are separate, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix → a highly efficient supply of oxygen.

  9. 9

    This is needed by animals with high energy needs, such as mammals and birds, which use energy constantly to maintain body temperature.

  10. Check against the schematic (Topic 5): two loops — heart → lungs → heart, and heart → body → heart ✔.

  11. Answer Deoxygenated blood: body → vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs; oxygenated blood: lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body. Blood passes through the heart twice per cycle. It is necessary because it keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, giving the efficient oxygen supply needed by warm-blooded animals with high energy needs.

    Write it in the exam (5 marks):

    • Body → right atrium → right ventricle → lungs (via pulmonary artery). (1)
    • Lungs → left atrium (via pulmonary veins) → left ventricle → body (via aorta). (1)
    • Blood passes through the heart twice in one cycle — double circulation. (1)
    • Necessary: prevents mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood → efficient oxygen supply. (1)
    • High energy needs of mammals/birds to maintain body temperature. (1)
      (A labelled schematic of the two circuits can replace the first two points.)
    Common trap: "Blood circulates round the body twice." It passes through the heart twice in one cycle.
