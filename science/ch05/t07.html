Excretion

About 55 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the human excretory system and the structure and functioning of a nephron (filtration in the Bowman's capsule, selective reabsorption in the tubule).
  • Explain how the amount of urine produced is regulated (the artificial kidney is a not-for-exam note).
  • Describe the ways plants get rid of excretory products.
  • Draw and label the human excretory system and a nephron.

Every day your two kidneys — each about the size of your fist — filter roughly 180 litres of fluid out of your blood. That is enough to fill a bathtub. Yet you pass only a litre or two of urine. Where does the rest go? The answer is one of the cleverest tricks in your body: filter almost everything out, then take back almost everything useful.

  1. What excretion is

Respiration and photosynthesis make gaseous wastes, which you have already met (carbon dioxide; oxygen in plants). Other metabolic activities make nitrogenous wastes, such as urea and uric acid, which are harmful if they build up. Excretion is the biological process of removing these harmful metabolic wastes from the body.

Many unicellular organisms simply let wastes diffuse from the body surface into the water around them. Multicellular organisms use specialised organs.

  1. The human excretory system

It is made up of:

  • a pair of kidneys — in the abdomen, one on either side of the backbone; they make urine;
  • a pair of ureters — tubes that carry urine from the kidneys to the bladder;
  • a urinary bladder — stores urine;
  • a urethra — releases urine from the body.

Blood reaches each kidney through the renal artery (a branch of the aorta) and leaves through the renal vein (into the vena cava).

The urine path: kidney → ureter → urinary bladder → urethra.

The bladder is muscular and under nervous control. As it fills, the pressure of the expanded bladder gives the urge to pass urine, which we can usually control.

  1. The nephron — the filtering unit

Making urine is about filtering wastes out of the blood, in the same way that the lungs clear carbon dioxide from the blood. So, like the alveoli, the kidney's basic filtration unit is built around a cluster of very thin-walled blood capillaries, the glomerulus.

Each glomerulus sits in the cup-shaped end of a coiled tube, called Bowman's capsule, which collects the filtrate. A capsule with its glomerulus and its long coiled tube makes one nephron. Each kidney has very large numbers of nephrons packed closely together.

How a nephron makes urine:

  1. Filtration — in the glomerulus and Bowman's capsule. Blood arrives through a branch of the renal artery. Water and small dissolved substances — urea, glucose, amino acids, salts — are filtered out of the glomerulus into the capsule. This liquid is the initial filtrate.
  2. Selective reabsorption — in the tubular part of the nephron. As the filtrate flows along the coiled tube, useful substances — glucose, amino acids, salts and a major amount of water — are taken back into the capillaries around the tube.
  3. Urine. What is left — water with nitrogenous wastes such as urea — is urine. It passes into the collecting duct, and from each kidney into the ureter.

Filtering does not choose: everything small enough passes out. Reabsorption chooses: it takes back what the body needs. So filtrate is not urine — urine is what remains after reabsorption.

  1. How the amount of urine is regulated

The amount of water reabsorbed in the tubule depends on:

  • how much excess water there is in the body, and
  • how much dissolved waste there is to be excreted.

On a hot day, when you sweat a lot, there is little excess water, so more water is reabsorbed — you pass a small amount of concentrated urine. After drinking a lot of water, less is reabsorbed — you pass more, dilute urine.

NCERT information box

Artificial kidney (haemodialysis). If the kidneys fail, poisonous wastes build up in the body and can cause death. An artificial kidney removes nitrogenous wastes from the blood by dialysis. The patient's blood flows through tubes lined with a semi-permeable material, which sit in a tank of dialysing fluid — a liquid matched to blood in osmotic pressure but free of nitrogenous wastes. The wastes diffuse out into the fluid, and the purified blood is pumped back into the patient. Unlike a real kidney, there is no reabsorption. In a healthy adult the initial filtrate is about 180 L a day, but only a litre or two is excreted, because the rest is reabsorbed in the kidney tubules.

Think it over

(not for exam — NCERT information box) Organ donation means generously giving an organ to someone whose own organ has stopped working. Anyone, of any age or gender, can become a donor with their own and their family's consent. Corneas, kidneys, heart, liver, pancreas, lungs, intestines and bone marrow are commonly transplanted; most donations happen after death or when a person is declared brain dead, but a kidney, part of a liver, a lung and some tissues can be donated while alive.

  1. Excretion in plants

Plants use completely different strategies from animals:

Waste How the plant gets rid of it
Oxygen (from photosynthesis) Given out through the stomata — oxygen itself can be thought of as a waste product of photosynthesis
Carbon dioxide (from respiration at night) Given out through the stomata
Excess water Transpiration
Other wastes Stored in cellular vacuoles; stored in leaves that fall off; stored as resins and gums, especially in old xylem; some excreted into the soil around the plant

Plants can manage this way because many of their tissues are dead cells, and they can lose parts such as leaves.

Worked example 1

Why is urine darker on a hot day?

Question. After playing cricket for three hours in the May sun, Sameer passes only a small amount of dark yellow urine. On a cool day when he drinks plenty of water, he passes much more pale urine. Explain, using what happens in the nephron.

Given: hot day, heavy sweating → little, concentrated urine; cool day, lots of water → more, dilute urine. Asked: explanation in terms of the nephron.

Concept used: the amount of water reabsorbed in the tubule depends on how much excess water the body has (this topic). Why it applies: sweating changes how much water the body can spare.

  1. 1

    On the hot day Sameer loses a lot of water as sweat, so his body has little excess water.

  2. 2

    As filtrate flows along the tubules, more water is reabsorbed into the blood.

  3. 3

    The same amount of waste (urea) is left in less water → small volume of concentrated (dark) urine.

  4. 4

    On the cool day with plenty of water, the body has excess water, so less water is reabsorbed.

  5. 5

    The waste leaves in more water → larger volume of dilute (pale) urine.

  6. Check: in both cases the wastes are removed; only the water reabsorbed changes — which is exactly the factor NCERT says controls urine volume ✔.

  7. Answer On the hot day more water is reabsorbed in the tubules because the body has little to spare, giving less, concentrated urine; when there is excess water, less is reabsorbed, giving more, dilute urine.

    Common trap: Saying "the kidneys filter less on a hot day". Filtration goes on as usual; it is the reabsorption of water that changes.

Worked example 2

A molecule of urea and a molecule of glucose

Question. A molecule of urea and a molecule of glucose arrive together in the blood of a glomerulus. Follow each one through the nephron and say where each ends up.

Given: urea (waste) and glucose (useful) in glomerular blood. Asked: the path of each.

Concept used: filtration in the Bowman's capsule; selective reabsorption in the tubule (this topic). Why it applies: the two stages treat the two molecules differently.

  1. 1

    Filtration: both are small, dissolved molecules, so both pass out of the glomerulus into Bowman's capsule as part of the filtrate. Filtration does not sort waste from useful substances.

  2. 2

    Glucose flows into the tubular part and is selectively reabsorbed into the capillaries round the tubule → it goes back into the blood, and leaves the kidney in the renal vein.

  3. 3

    Urea is a waste, so it is not taken back like glucose → it stays in the tubule, passes into the collecting duct, and leaves in the urine → ureter → bladder → urethra.

  4. Check: a healthy person has no glucose in the urine, because reabsorption takes it all back — consistent with the path above ✔.

  5. Answer Both are filtered into Bowman's capsule; glucose is reabsorbed into the blood in the tubule; urea, a waste, stays in the tubule and leaves in the urine.

    Common trap: Saying "glucose is not filtered". It is filtered, then reabsorbed. Filtration happens in the capsule; reabsorption happens in the tubule.

Worked example 3

How dialysis cleans blood (based on an NCERT information box; not for exam)

Question. In an artificial kidney, blood flows through tubes with a semi-permeable lining surrounded by dialysing fluid. The fluid is matched to blood in osmotic pressure, but it has no nitrogenous wastes in it. (a) Why does urea leave the blood? (b) Why does glucose not leave? (c) How is this different from a real nephron?

Given: blood and dialysing fluid separated by a semi-permeable lining. Asked: why urea leaves, why glucose stays, the difference from a nephron.

Concept used: diffusion from more to less concentrated (Topic 1). Why it applies: the lining separates two liquids with different amounts of each substance.

  1. 1

    (a) Urea is present in blood and absent in the fluid → it diffuses from blood (more) into the fluid (less).

  2. 2

    (b) The fluid is made with about the same glucose and salts as blood, so there is no concentration difference for them → they do not move out overall. (Equal osmotic pressure also means water is not drawn out of the blood.)

  3. 3

    (c) A nephron filters everything small and then reabsorbs what is useful. The artificial kidney has no reabsorption; it relies on the fluid being made up so that only the wastes diffuse out.

  4. Check: the result matches the box: purified blood is pumped back, wastes stay in the fluid ✔.

  5. Answer (a) Urea diffuses out because the fluid has none (b) the fluid already has blood-level glucose and salts, so there is no difference to drive them out (c) a nephron filters and then reabsorbs; dialysis has no reabsorption.

    Common trap: Thinking dialysis "filters out" wastes the way a sieve does. It works by diffusion across a semi-permeable lining.

Common mistakes!

  • "Filtration happens in the tubule."

    The tubule reabsorbs.

    Filtration: glomerulus → Bowman's capsule. Reabsorption: tubule.

  • "Filtrate and urine are the same."

    Most of the filtrate is taken back.

    Urine = filtrate minus reabsorbed glucose, amino acids, salts and most of the water.

  • "Glucose is not filtered."

    All small dissolved substances are filtered.

    Glucose is filtered, then reabsorbed.

  • "Urine is made in the bladder."

    The bladder only stores it.

    Urine is made in the kidneys (nephrons).

  • Ureter and urethra swapped

    They are different tubes.

    Ureter: kidney → bladder (two of them). Urethra: bladder → outside (one).

  • "Plants have no excretory products."

    Plants make wastes too.

    Oxygen, COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​, excess water, and other wastes stored or shed (table above).

  • Nephron drawn without the capsule round the glomerulus

    The cup is where filtrate collects.

    Draw the cup (Bowman's capsule) with the knot of capillaries inside it.

Key points

  • Excretion = removal of harmful metabolic wastes (e.g. nitrogenous wastes such as urea and uric acid).
  • Human excretory system: 2 kidneys → 2 ureters → urinary bladder (muscular, nervous control) → urethra.
  • Nephron: glomerulus (thin-walled capillary cluster) in Bowman's capsule → filtration; tubule → selective reabsorption of glucose, amino acids, salts and most water; remainder = urine → collecting duct → ureter.
  • Urine volume depends on how much excess water and how much dissolved waste the body has: less excess water → more reabsorbed → less, concentrated urine.
  • Plants: oxygen and COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ via stomata; excess water by transpiration; other wastes in vacuoles, falling leaves, resins and gums in old xylem, or released into the soil.
