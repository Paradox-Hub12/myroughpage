Topic 7 of 7

Corrosion and its prevention; alloys

About 45 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the corrosion of silver, copper and iron (rusting), and the conditions needed for rusting (three-test-tube activity).
  • Describe and choose methods of preventing rusting — painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising, chrome plating, anodising and making alloys — and explain why galvanised iron is protected even when the zinc is scratched.
  • Explain what an alloy is, give examples (steel, stainless steel, brass, bronze, solder, amalgam) with their uses, and say how alloying changes a metal's properties.

Your grandmother's silver anklet has turned black. The copper kalash in the prayer room has a green crust. The iron gate that was painted two monsoons ago is flaking brown where the paint has chipped. And yet a steel spoon from the same kitchen is still bright after years of washing. Metals are slowly attacked by the air around them — but we have learnt many ways to fight back.

  1. Corrosion — a quick recap from Chapter 1

When a metal's surface is slowly attacked by substances in its surroundings, such as moisture, oxygen and acids, we say it corrodes.

Metal What you see What has formed
Silver turns black silver sulphide — silver reacts with sulphur compounds in the air
Copper loses its shiny brown surface, gains a green coat basic copper carbonate — copper reacts with moist carbon dioxide in the air
Iron a brown, flaky coating rust — formed when iron is exposed to moist air for a long time

Rust is a serious problem: it weakens bridges, railings, ships and car bodies, and the flakes fall off, exposing fresh iron underneath to rust further.

  1. What does iron need to rust?

Lab: three nails, three conditions (NCERT Activity 3.14)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do

Put clean iron nails in three test tubes, A, B and C.

  • A: some tap water; cork the tube. → nails get air and water.
  • B: boiled distilled water (boiling removes dissolved air) with about 1 mL of oil on top (the oil floats and stops air dissolving back in); cork the tube. → nails get water but no air.
  • C: some anhydrous calcium chloride (it absorbs moisture from the air); cork the tube. → nails get dry air.
  • Leave the tubes for a few days.
See
The nails rust in tube A only. The nails in B and C stay shiny.
Shows
Rusting needs both air (oxygen) and water. Remove either one and iron does not rust.

  1. Virtual lab — the rusting test

Run the three-tube test in fast-forward, then try one extra tube.

Tube Contents Air? Water? After 3–4 days
A tap water, corked ✔ ✔ brown rust on the nails ✔
B boiled distilled water + layer of oil ✘ ✔ no rust
C dry air + anhydrous calcium chloride ✔ ✘ no rust
D (extra) salt water, corked ✔ ✔ more rust than in A

What it shows: A, B and C prove that both air and water are needed. Tube D shows that dissolved salt makes rusting faster — one reason why iron rusts so quickly near the sea, where the air is both humid and salty, compared with a dry hilly region.

Why each step matters: the water in B is boiled to drive out dissolved air; the oil stops fresh air dissolving; calcium chloride must be anhydrous (water-free) so that it can soak up moisture; every tube is corked so that the conditions do not change.

  1. Preventing rusting

The rusting of iron can be prevented by painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising, chrome plating, anodising or making alloys. Each method works in one of two ways:

Method What is done How it protects Typical use
Painting a coat of paint keeps air and water out gates, railings, bridges, car bodies
Oiling / greasing a film of oil or grease keeps air and water out; also lubricates moving parts bicycle chains, machine parts, tools
Galvanising a thin coating of zinc on iron or steel keeps air and water out and zinc, being more reactive, corrodes first buckets, roofing sheets, water pipes
Chrome plating a layer of chromium keeps air and water out; shiny finish taps, cycle handlebars, car fittings
Anodising a thick oxide layer on aluminium the oxide layer stops further corrosion aluminium window frames, pressure-cooker bodies
Making alloys mixing iron with other elements the alloy itself does not rust stainless steel utensils

Why galvanised iron stays protected even when the zinc is scratched. Zinc is above iron in the reactivity series. Where the coating is broken, the zinc around the scratch reacts with air and water instead of the iron, so the iron is not corroded. Paint, oil and grease give no such protection — once they are scratched, the iron underneath rusts.

Galvanising vs anodising: galvanising puts zinc on iron; anodising thickens the oxide layer on aluminium.

  1. Alloys

Iron is the most used metal, but never pure: pure iron is soft and stretches easily when hot. Mixed with a small amount of carbon (about 0.05%), it becomes hard and strong — this is steel. Mixed with nickel and chromium, it becomes stainless steel, which is hard and does not rust.

An alloy is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or of a metal and a non-metal. It is made by melting the main metal and dissolving the other elements in it in definite proportions, then cooling it to room temperature. If one of the metals is mercury, the alloy is called an amalgam.

Alloying changes properties: an alloy conducts electricity less well and melts at a lower temperature than the pure metals in it.

Alloy Made of Useful property Use
Steel iron + a little carbon hard and strong construction, tools
Stainless steel iron + nickel + chromium hard, does not rust utensils, surgical instruments
Brass copper + zinc harder than copper; poor electrical conductor compared with copper utensils, fittings, musical instruments
Bronze copper + tin hard, does not corrode easily; poor conductor compared with copper statues, medals, bells
Solder lead + tin low melting point welding (joining) electrical wires
Amalgam mercury + another metal — e.g. zinc amalgam

That is why electrical circuits use pure copper, not brass or bronze.

NCERT information box; Indian knowledge link

The Iron Pillar of Delhi. In the Qutb complex at Mehrauli stands an iron pillar more than 1,600 years old — its inscription is generally linked to the Gupta emperor Chandragupta II (about 375–415 CE). It is about 7.2 m long (about 1.1 m of it below the ground) and weighs more than 6 tonnes, and it has stood in the open without rusting away. Metallurgists, notably R. Balasubramaniam of IIT Kanpur (2000), traced the secret to iron with unusually high phosphorus (around 0.25%, against less than 0.05% in modern iron) made by the ancient smiths' process; this helps a very thin, tightly sticking protective film to form on the surface, which has grown only about a twentieth of a millimetre in sixteen centuries. (Your textbook gives the height as 8 m and the mass as 6 tonnes.)

Worked example 1

Design the protection

Question. Suggest the best way to prevent rusting for (a) a bicycle chain, (b) an iron gate, (c) a steel bucket used to carry water. Give a reason for each.

Given: three iron or steel objects with different uses. Asked: a method and a reason for each.

Concept used: each method keeps out air and water, or uses a more reactive metal (this topic). Why it applies: the best method depends on how the object is used.

  1. 1

    (a) The chain has moving parts that rub together. Paint would crack off. → Oiling/greasing: keeps air and water out and lets the links move smoothly.

  2. 2

    (b) The gate stands outside in sun and rain and does not need to move much. → Painting: a cheap, durable layer that keeps air and water out (repainted when it chips).

  3. 3

    (c) The bucket is always in contact with water and gets knocked and scratched. Paint would chip and oil would wash off. → Galvanising: zinc keeps water and air away, and even where it is scratched, zinc corrodes in place of the iron.

  4. Check: test each choice against the other objects — oil on a bucket would wash away, paint on a chain would crack, and galvanising a gate would work but cost more than paint. Each answer suits its own object best ✔.

  5. Answer (a) oiling/greasing (b) painting (c) galvanising — reasons above.

    Common trap: Writing "painting" for everything. Think about how the object is used: moving parts need oil; things in constant contact with water and knocks need galvanising.

Worked example 2

Which alloy?

Question. Name the alloy in each case and give its main elements: (a) used to join electrical wires because it melts easily; (b) used for kitchen sinks because it does not rust; (c) a yellow alloy used for door handles and musical instruments; (d) an alloy that contains mercury.

Given: four descriptions. Asked: alloy and composition.

Concept used: the alloys table (this topic). Why it applies: each description names a property or use from the table.

  1. 1

    (a) Low melting point, joins wires → solder = lead + tin.

  2. 2

    (b) Does not rust → stainless steel = iron + nickel + chromium.

  3. 3

    (c) Yellow, used for handles and instruments → brass = copper + zinc.

  4. 4

    (d) Contains mercury → an amalgam (mercury + another metal, e.g. zinc amalgam).

  5. Check: reverse it — from each composition, recall the use: lead + tin melts easily (wires) ✔; Fe + Ni + Cr resists rust (sinks) ✔; Cu + Zn is brass ✔; Hg → amalgam ✔.

  6. Answer (a) solder (Pb + Sn) (b) stainless steel (Fe + Ni + Cr) (c) brass (Cu + Zn) (d) amalgam (Hg + another metal)

    Common trap: Mixing brass and bronze. Learn them as a fixed pair: brass — copper + zinc; bronze — copper + tin.

Worked example 3

Scratched coatings

Question. Two iron buckets are scratched deeply: bucket P is painted; bucket Q is galvanised. After a month in the rain, P is rusty along the scratch but Q is not. Explain.

Given: a scratch through paint and a scratch through zinc. Asked: why only P rusts.

Concept used: rusting needs air and water; zinc is more reactive than iron (reactivity series, Topic 4). Why it applies: the scratch lets air and water reach the iron in both buckets.

  1. 1

    In both buckets, the scratch exposes iron to air and water.

  2. 2

    In P, paint only works as a barrier. Once broken, nothing stops the exposed iron reacting → P rusts.

  3. 3

    In Q, the zinc around the scratch is more reactive than iron.

  4. 4

    Air and water react with the zinc instead of the iron, so the iron is protected → Q does not rust.

  5. Check: if zinc were less reactive than iron (like tin), the iron would rust first at a scratch — which is exactly what happens to a scratched tin can. So the result depends on zinc being above iron ✔.

  6. Answer Paint is only a barrier; zinc is a barrier and more reactive than iron, so it corrodes in place of the iron at the scratch.

    Common trap: Saying "zinc does not react with air and water". It does — that is precisely how it protects the iron.

Common mistakes!

  • "Rusting needs only water."

    Tube B (water, no air) shows no rust.

    Rusting needs both air (oxygen) and water.

  • "Rusting needs only air."

    Tube C (dry air) shows no rust.

    Both are needed.

  • Calling any corrosion "rusting"

    Only iron rusts. Silver tarnishes black; copper turns green.

    Rust = corrosion of iron.

  • "Galvanising = electroplating with zinc only, so a scratch ruins it."

    Zinc protects iron even at a scratch, because zinc is more reactive.

    Explain with the reactivity series.

  • Mixing galvanising and anodising

    Different metals, different coatings.

    Galvanising: zinc on iron. Anodising: thicker oxide on aluminium.

  • "Stainless steel is pure iron."

    It is an alloy of iron with nickel and chromium.

    Pure iron is soft; it is never used pure.

  • "An alloy is a compound."

    Its parts are mixed, not chemically joined.

    Homogeneous mixture.

  • "Alloys conduct better than pure metals."

    Their conductivity is lower.

    Copper for wires; solder for its low melting point.

Key points

  • Corrosion: silver → black silver sulphide; copper → green basic copper carbonate; iron → brown flaky rust.
  • Rusting needs both air and water (tubes A, B, C); salt speeds it up.
  • Prevention: painting, oiling, greasing (keep air and water out); galvanising (zinc coat — zinc corrodes first, so it protects even when scratched); chrome plating; anodising (aluminium); alloying (stainless steel).
  • Alloy = homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal; amalgam contains mercury.
  • Steel (Fe + C), stainless steel (Fe + Ni + Cr), brass (Cu + Zn), bronze (Cu + Sn), solder (Pb + Sn, low melting point). Alloys have lower conductivity and melting points than pure metals.
