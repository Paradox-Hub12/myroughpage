Topic 6 of 7

Getting metals from their ores

About 55 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Define minerals, ores and gangue, and name metals found in the free state (gold, silver, platinum, copper).
  • Explain enrichment of ores, roasting (sulphide ores) and calcination (carbonate ores), with equations.
  • Explain reduction by heating alone, by carbon and by a more reactive metal (thermit reaction), and electrolytic reduction for Na, Mg, Ca and Al; choose the method from a metal's place in the reactivity series.
  • Describe electrolytic refining (anode, cathode, electrolyte, anode mud).

Gold can be picked out of a river bed as shiny flakes. Iron never can — every iron nail was once a reddish rock, and every aluminium can was once a clay-like mineral. Why is gold found ready-made while aluminium has to be pulled out with huge amounts of electricity? Look at the reactivity series: the higher a metal is, the harder it is to get out.

Quick reminder (Chapter 1)

Reduction is the loss of oxygen (or gain of hydrogen). Getting a metal from its oxide is a reduction. A more reactive element can displace a less reactive one from its compound.

  1. Minerals, ores and gangue

The earth's crust is the main source of metals; sea water also contains soluble salts such as sodium chloride and magnesium chloride.

  • Mineral: an element or compound that occurs naturally in the earth's crust.
  • Ore: a mineral so rich in one metal that extracting the metal from it is profitable. (Every ore is a mineral, but not every mineral is an ore.)
  • Gangue: the impurities — soil, sand and so on — mixed with an ore when it is mined.
  • Metallurgy: the whole business of extracting metals from their ores and refining them for use.

  1. Where a metal sits decides how it is found

Position in the series Metals How they occur in nature
Bottom (low reactivity) Au, Ag, (Pt), Cu Often free (native) — gold, silver, platinum and copper are found as the metal. Copper and silver also occur as sulphide or oxide ores.
Middle (medium reactivity) Zn, Fe, Pb Mainly as oxides, sulphides or carbonates.
Top (high reactivity) K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al Never free — always combined in compounds.

Many ores are oxides, because oxygen is very reactive and very plentiful on the earth.

  1. Step 1 for every ore: enrichment

Mined ore contains a lot of gangue. It is removed first. The method chosen depends on some difference between the ore and the gangue — a physical property (such as density) or a chemical one. This is enrichment (concentration) of the ore.

  1. Metals low in the series: heating alone

Their oxides break down on heating. Mercury's ore, cinnabar (HgS\ce{HgS}HgS), is heated in air to give mercuric oxide, which on further heating gives mercury:

  1. Metals at the top: electrolytic reduction

Heating with carbon does not work for sodium, magnesium, calcium or aluminium: these metals hold on to oxygen more strongly — they have more affinity for oxygen than carbon has. They are obtained by electrolytic reduction:

  • Sodium, magnesium and calcium: electrolysis of their molten chlorides. The metal is deposited at the cathode (negative electrode) and chlorine is given off at the anode (positive electrode):

  • Aluminium: electrolytic reduction of aluminium oxide.

  1. The last step: refining

Metals from these processes are not very pure. The most widely used method of purifying them is electrolytic refining — used for copper, zinc, tin, nickel, silver, gold and others.

Part What is used (copper as the example)
Anode (+) the impure metal — a block of impure copper
Cathode (−) a thin strip of pure metal — pure copper
Electrolyte a solution of a salt of the metal — acidified copper sulphate

When current passes, pure metal from the anode dissolves into the electrolyte, and an equal amount of pure metal from the electrolyte is deposited on the cathode. Soluble impurities go into the solution; insoluble impurities settle at the bottom below the anode as anode mud.

  1. The whole route on one page

Ore → enrichment → then, by position in the series:

  • High reactivity (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al): electrolysis of the molten ore (chloride or oxide) → pure metal.
  • Medium reactivity (Zn, Fe, Pb): carbonate ore → calcination, sulphide ore → roasting → metal oxide → reduction (carbon, or a more reactive metal) → refining.
  • Low reactivity (Cu, Hg): sulphide ore → roasting (heating in air) → metal → refining. (Copper oxide can also be reduced with carbon — see the note below.) Ag, Au: often found free.

(Your textbook puts copper in two groups. Its chart, and the section on middle metals, place copper with zinc, iron and lead — metals whose oxides can be reduced with carbon. The section on low-reactivity metals obtains copper from its sulphide ore, CuX2S\ce{Cu2S}CuX2​S, just by heating in air. Both are right: copper oxide can be reduced by carbon, but copper's sulphide ore does not need it. In the exam, if you are asked how copper is obtained from CuX2S\ce{Cu2S}CuX2​S, write the two heating equations above. If you are asked which metals' oxides are reduced by carbon, as in the chart, copper may be included.)

Worked example 1

Lead from two different ores

Question. Lead occurs as galena (PbS\ce{PbS}PbS) and as cerussite (PbCOX3\ce{PbCO3}PbCOX3​). Name the process used to convert each ore into lead(II) oxide, write the equations, and write the equation for reducing the oxide with carbon.

Given: a sulphide ore and a carbonate ore of lead. Asked: process names; three equations.

Concept used: sulphide → roasting (excess air); carbonate → calcination (limited air); oxide + carbon → metal + carbon monoxide. Why it applies: lead is in the middle of the series, so it is reduced with carbon.

  1. 1

    Galena — roasting

    Heat PbS\ce{PbS}PbS strongly in excess air. Products: PbO\ce{PbO}PbO and SOX2\ce{SO2}SOX2​.

  2. 2

    Skeletal: PbS+OX2→PbO+SOX2\ce{PbS + O2 -> PbO + SO2}PbS+OX2​​PbO+SOX2​.

  3. 3

    Oxygen on the right: 1 + 2 = 3 — an odd number, but OX2\ce{O2}OX2​ always brings an even number → double the products: 2 PbO+2 SOX2\ce{2PbO + 2SO2}2PbO+2SOX2​.

  4. 4

    Lead and sulphur on the right are now 2 each → put 2 in front of PbS\ce{PbS}PbS.

  5. 5

    Oxygen on the right: 2 + 4 = 6 → put 3 in front of OX2\ce{O2}OX2​.

    2 PbS(s)+3 OX2(g)→heat2 PbO(s)+2 SOX2(g)\ce{2PbS(s) + 3O2(g) ->[\text{heat}] 2PbO(s) + 2SO2(g)}2PbS(s)+3OX2​(g)heat​2PbO(s)+2SOX2​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    Cerussite — calcination

    Heat PbCOX3\ce{PbCO3}PbCOX3​ strongly in limited air. It breaks down into PbO\ce{PbO}PbO and COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ — already balanced.

    PbCOX3(s)→heatPbO(s)+COX2(g)\ce{PbCO3(s) ->[\text{heat}] PbO(s) + CO2(g)}PbCOX3​(s)heat​PbO(s)+COX2​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  7. 7

    Reduction

    Carbon removes the oxygen: PbO+C→Pb+CO\ce{PbO + C -> Pb + CO}PbO+C​Pb+CO — already balanced.

    PbO(s)+C(s)→Pb(s)+CO(g)\ce{PbO(s) + C(s) -> Pb(s) + CO(g)}PbO(s)+C(s)​Pb(s)+CO(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →

  8. Check: roasting — Pb 2 | 2, S 2 | 2, O 6 | 2 + 4 = 6 ✔; calcination — Pb 1, C 1, O 3 | 1 + 2 = 3 ✔; reduction — Pb, O, C one each side ✔. The gas from each process matches its ore: sulphide → SOX2\ce{SO2}SOX2​, carbonate → COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ ✔.

  9. Answer Galena: roasting, 2 PbS+3 OX2→2 PbO+2 SOX2\ce{2PbS + 3O2 -> 2PbO + 2SO2}2PbS+3OX2​​2PbO+2SOX2​ · Cerussite: calcination, PbCOX3→PbO+COX2\ce{PbCO3 -> PbO + CO2}PbCOX3​​PbO+COX2​ · Reduction: PbO+C→Pb+CO\ce{PbO + C -> Pb + CO}PbO+C​Pb+CO

    Common trap: Swapping the names. Roasting = sulphide in plenty of air; calcination = carbonate in limited air.

Worked example 2

Choosing the method

Question. Suggest how each metal is obtained, giving a reason from the reactivity series: (a) potassium, (b) iron (from FeX2OX3\ce{Fe2O3}FeX2​OX3​), (c) silver.

Given: three metals at different heights in the series. Asked: method and reason; one equation for (b).

Concept used: top → electrolysis; middle → reduction with carbon; bottom → found free or heating alone. Why it applies: the method depends on how strongly the metal holds on to oxygen.

  1. 1

    (a) Potassium

    Potassium is at the top of the series.

  2. 2

    Its affinity for oxygen is greater than carbon's, so carbon cannot reduce its compounds.

  3. 3

    Method: electrolytic reduction of molten potassium chloride — potassium forms at the cathode.

  4. 4

    (b) Iron

    Iron is in the middle of the series.

  5. 5

    Carbon can remove oxygen from its oxide. Skeletal: FeX2OX3+C→Fe+CO\ce{Fe2O3 + C -> Fe + CO}FeX2​OX3​+C​Fe+CO.

  6. 6

    Iron: left 2, right 1 → put 2 in front of Fe.

  7. 7

    Oxygen: left 3, right 1 → put 3 in front of CO.

  8. 8

    Carbon: right now 3 → put 3 in front of C.

    FeX2OX3(s)+3 C(s)→2 Fe(s)+3 CO(g)\ce{Fe2O3(s) + 3C(s) -> 2Fe(s) + 3CO(g)}FeX2​OX3​(s)+3C(s)​2Fe(s)+3CO(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →
  9. 9

    (c) Silver

    Silver is near the bottom of the series. It is very unreactive.

  10. 10

    Method: often found free in nature; its compounds give the metal easily (by heating). It is then purified by electrolytic refining.

  11. Check: (b) Fe 2 | 2, O 3 | 3, C 3 | 3 ✔. Cross-check the method with the textbook's groups: K is in the "electrolysis" group, Fe in "reduction using carbon", Ag in "found in native state" ✔.

  12. Answer (a) electrolysis of molten KCl (b) reduction with carbon — FeX2OX3+3 C→2 Fe+3 CO\ce{Fe2O3 + 3C -> 2Fe + 3CO}FeX2​OX3​+3C​2Fe+3CO (c) found free / heating alone, then refined.

    Common trap: Suggesting carbon for potassium or aluminium. Carbon works only for metals below aluminium.

Worked example 3

Chromium by the thermit method

Question. Chromium is less reactive than aluminium. Chromium(III) oxide, CrX2OX3\ce{Cr2O3}CrX2​OX3​, is heated with aluminium powder. Write the balanced equation, say which substance is oxidised and which reduced, and explain why the chromium comes out molten.

Given: CrX2OX3\ce{Cr2O3}CrX2​OX3​ + Al. Asked: equation; oxidised/reduced; why molten.

Concept used: a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its oxide; these reactions are highly exothermic (this topic and Chapter 1). Why it applies: aluminium is above chromium.

  1. 1

    Aluminium takes the oxygen: products are Cr and AlX2OX3\ce{Al2O3}AlX2​OX3​. Skeletal: CrX2OX3+Al→Cr+AlX2OX3\ce{Cr2O3 + Al -> Cr + Al2O3}CrX2​OX3​+Al​Cr+AlX2​OX3​.

  2. 2

    Chromium: left 2, right 1 → put 2 in front of Cr.

  3. 3

    Aluminium: right 2 → put 2 in front of Al.

  4. 4

    Oxygen: left 3, right 3 ✔.

    CrX2OX3(s)+2 Al(s)→2 Cr(l)+AlX2OX3(s)+heat\ce{Cr2O3(s) + 2Al(s) -> 2Cr(l) + Al2O3(s)} + \text{heat}CrX2​OX3​(s)+2Al(s)​2Cr(l)+AlX2​OX3​(s)+heatSwipe to see the whole equation →
  5. 5

    Aluminium gains oxygen → aluminium is oxidised (it is the reducing agent).

  6. 6

    Chromium(III) oxide loses oxygen → it is reduced.

  7. 7

    The reaction gives out so much heat that the chromium formed is molten.

  8. Check: Cr 2 | 2, Al 2 | 2, O 3 | 3 ✔. Same pattern as the thermit reaction (FeX2OX3+2 Al→2 Fe+AlX2OX3\ce{Fe2O3 + 2Al -> 2Fe + Al2O3}FeX2​OX3​+2Al​2Fe+AlX2​OX3​), with Cr in place of Fe ✔.

    Common trap: Saying "aluminium is reduced because it is the reducing agent". The reducing agent is itself oxidised.

Quick check · 2 of 4Very short answer1 mark

All ores are minerals, but not all minerals are ores. Explain.

Answer: A mineral is any naturally occurring element or compound in the earth's crust. It is called an ore only if it contains so much of a metal that the metal can be extracted from it profitably.

Magnesium is obtained by:

Quick check · 4 of 4Short answer2 marks

In the electrolytic refining of copper, what is used as the cathode and the electrolyte, and what is anode mud?

Answer: Cathode: a thin strip of pure copper. Electrolyte: acidified copper sulphate solution. Anode mud: the insoluble impurities that settle at the bottom below the anode.

The impure metal is always the anode — it dissolves; pure metal collects on the cathode.

Common mistakes!

  • Roasting and calcination swapped

    Roasting: sulphide, excess air, gives SOX2\ce{SO2}SOX2​. Calcination: carbonate, limited air, gives COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​.

    Use the "R-rich / C-carbonate" hook.

  • "Calcination needs no air at all"

    The textbook says limited air.

    Write "heated strongly in limited air".

  • Reducing Na, Mg, Ca or Al oxides with carbon

    These metals have more affinity for oxygen than carbon.

    Electrolytic reduction.

  • Skipping enrichment, or refining before reduction

    The order matters.

    Ore → enrichment → (roast/calcine) → reduce → refine.

  • Impure metal as the cathode

    The impure metal must dissolve.

    Anode = impure, cathode = pure strip.

  • "Thermit reaction: iron reduces aluminium oxide"

    Aluminium is the more reactive metal.

    FeX2OX3+2 Al→2 Fe+AlX2OX3\ce{Fe2O3 + 2Al -> 2Fe + Al2O3}FeX2​OX3​+2Al​2Fe+AlX2​OX3​.

  • Writing COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ in zinc oxide + carbon

    The textbook equation gives carbon monoxide.

    ZnO+C→Zn+CO\ce{ZnO + C -> Zn + CO}ZnO+C​Zn+CO.

Key points

  • Mineral = naturally occurring element or compound; ore = mineral from which a metal can be extracted profitably; gangue = impurities.
  • Bottom metals (Au, Ag, Pt, Cu) can be found free; top metals (K–Al) never are.
  • Enrichment first. Roasting: sulphide + excess air → oxide + SOX2\ce{SO2}SOX2​. Calcination: carbonate + limited air → oxide + COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​.
  • Reduction: heating alone (Hg; Cu from CuX2S\ce{Cu2S}CuX2​S); carbon (Zn, Fe, Pb — and copper oxide, as in the textbook chart); a more reactive metal (thermit: FeX2OX3+2 Al\ce{Fe2O3 + 2Al}FeX2​OX3​+2Al, joins rails); electrolysis (Na, Mg, Ca from molten chlorides; Al from its oxide).
  • Electrolytic refining: impure anode, pure cathode, salt-solution electrolyte; insoluble impurities = anode mud.
