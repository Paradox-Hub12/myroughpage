Intext p.40, Q1A metal for each description2 marks

Give an example of a metal which
(i) is a liquid at room temperature.
(ii) can be easily cut with a knife.
(iii) is the best conductor of heat.
(iv) is a poor conductor of heat.

Given: four descriptions. Asked: one metal for each.

Concept used: the general properties of metals and their exceptions (Topic 2). Why it applies: (i) and (ii) are exceptions to "metals are hard solids"; (iii) and (iv) compare conductors.

  1. 1

    (i) The only metal that is liquid at room temperature is mercury.

  2. 2

    (ii) The alkali metals are soft enough to cut with a knife → sodium (or lithium, potassium).

  3. 3

    (iii) The best conductors of heat are silver and copper → silver.

  4. 4

    (iv) Comparatively poor conductors of heat are lead and mercury → lead.

  5. Check: (iii) agrees with the sample-paper experiment on the Board questions page (the heat-conduction rods) ✔; (i) and (iv) are consistent — mercury, the liquid metal, is also one of the poor conductors ✔.

  6. Answer (i) mercury (ii) sodium (iii) silver (iv) lead (mercury is also a poor conductor of heat).

    Common trap: Giving bromine for (i). Bromine is a liquid non-metal; the question asks for a metal.
Intext p.40, Q2Malleable and ductile2 marks

Explain the meanings of malleable and ductile.

Given: two terms. Asked: their meanings.

Concept used: physical properties of metals (Topic 2). Why it applies: both describe how a metal changes shape without breaking.

  1. 1

    Malleable: a substance that can be beaten into thin sheets (for example with a hammer) without breaking. Gold and silver are the most malleable metals; aluminium foil is an everyday example.

  2. 2

    Ductile: a substance that can be drawn into thin wires. Gold is the most ductile metal; copper and aluminium wires are everyday examples.

  3. Check: each definition matches its everyday use — foil (a sheet) for malleable, electric wire for ductile ✔.

  4. Answer Malleable — can be beaten into thin sheets. Ductile — can be drawn into thin wires.

    Common trap: Swapping the two. Sheets → malleable; wires → ductile.

Intext p.46, Q1Sodium in kerosene2 marks

Why is sodium kept immersed in kerosene oil?

Given: sodium. Asked: why it is stored in kerosene.

Concept used: sodium is near the top of the reactivity series (Topic 3). Why it applies: very reactive metals react with both oxygen and water.

  1. 1

    Sodium reacts very vigorously with the oxygen in air; kept in the open it can catch fire.

  2. 2

    It also reacts violently with water (even moisture in the air), giving hydrogen, which catches fire.

  3. 3

    Sodium does not react with kerosene.

  4. 4

    Under kerosene, air and moisture cannot reach the metal → this protects it and prevents accidental fires.

  5. Check: the same reason explains why potassium (also at the top) is stored the same way, while magnesium (lower down) is not ✔.

  6. Answer Sodium is so reactive that it catches fire in air and reacts violently with water; kerosene, which does not react with it, keeps air and moisture away and prevents accidental fires.

    Common trap: Writing "kept in kerosene so that it does not melt" or "because it is soft" — the reason is its reactivity.

Intext p.46, Q3Metals A, B, C and D3 marks

Samples of four metals A, B, C and D were taken and added to the following solution one by one. The results obtained have been tabulated as follows.

Metal Iron(II) sulphate Copper(II) sulphate Zinc sulphate Silver nitrate
A No reaction Displacement
B Displacement No reaction
C No reaction No reaction No reaction Displacement
D No reaction No reaction No reaction No reaction

Use the Table above to answer the following questions about metals A, B, C and D.
(i) Which is the most reactive metal?
(ii) What would you observe if B is added to a solution of Copper(II) sulphate?
(iii) Arrange the metals A, B, C and D in the order of decreasing reactivity.

Given: displacement results for four metals. Asked: most reactive; observation for B + copper(II) sulphate; order.

Concept used: a metal displaces another from its salt solution only if it is more reactive; the reactivity series Zn > Fe > Cu > Ag (Topic 4). Why it applies: each result places a metal between two known metals.

  1. 1

    A: cannot displace iron, but displaces copper → A is between iron and copper.

  2. 2

    B: displaces iron, but cannot displace zinc → B is between zinc and iron.

  3. 3

    C: displaces only silver → C is between copper and silver.

  4. 4

    D: displaces none, not even silver → D is below silver.

  5. 5

    Order: B (above Fe) > A (between Fe and Cu) > C (between Cu and Ag) > D (below Ag).

  6. 6

    (i) B is the most reactive.

  7. 7

    (ii) B is above iron, so it is certainly above copper → B displaces copper: the blue colour of the solution fades and a reddish-brown layer of copper forms on B.

  8. Check: every "no reaction" fits the order — A cannot beat Fe, C cannot beat Cu, Fe or Zn, D cannot beat anything ✔.

  9. Answer (i) B (ii) B displaces copper: the blue solution fades and reddish-brown copper is deposited on B. (iii) B > A > C > D

    Common trap: Choosing C as most reactive "because it is the only metal shown displacing silver". The silver nitrate column is blank for A and B — being higher than C, they would displace silver too. Judge each metal by its highest success and its lowest failure.

What would you observe when zinc is added to a solution of iron(II) sulphate? Write the chemical reaction that takes place.

Given: zinc + iron(II) sulphate solution. Asked: observation; equation.

Concept used: a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution (Topic 4). Why it applies: zinc is above iron.

  1. 1

    Zinc is more reactive than iron → it displaces iron.

  2. 2

    The pale green colour of iron(II) sulphate fades (zinc sulphate is colourless).

  3. 3

    A dark grey/black deposit of iron forms on the zinc.

    Common trap: Saying "the solution turns blue" — blue is copper sulphate; iron(II) sulphate is pale green.

Intext p.49, Q2High melting points2 marks

Why do ionic compounds have high melting points?

Given: ionic compounds. Asked: why their melting points are high.

Concept used: ionic compounds are made of oppositely charged ions held by strong electrostatic forces (Topic 5). Why it applies: melting means separating the ions.

  1. 1

    An ionic compound is made of positive and negative ions.

  2. 2

    The ions are held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction.

  3. 3

    To melt the solid, these strong forces between the ions must be overcome.

  4. 4

    That needs a large amount of energy (heat) → high melting point.

  5. Check: the data fit — sodium chloride melts at 1074 K, calcium oxide at 2850 K, far above wax or ice ✔.

  6. Answer The ions are held by strong electrostatic forces of attraction, and a considerable amount of energy is needed to break these inter-ionic attractions.

    Common trap: Saying "because ionic compounds are hard". Hardness and high melting point have the same cause — strong attraction between ions; one does not explain the other.

Intext p.53, Q1Mineral, ore, gangue3 marks

Define the following terms.
(i) Mineral (ii) Ore (iii) Gangue

Given: three terms. Asked: definitions.

Concept used: occurrence of metals (Topic 6).

  1. 1

    (i) Mineral: an element or compound that occurs naturally in the earth's crust.

  2. 2

    (ii) Ore: a mineral that contains a very high percentage of a particular metal, from which the metal can be extracted profitably.

  3. 3

    (iii) Gangue: the impurities such as soil and sand that are mixed with an ore when it is mined.

  4. Check: the definitions fit together — every ore is a mineral, and gangue is what is removed from an ore before extraction ✔.

  5. Answer (i) a naturally occurring element or compound in the earth's crust; (ii) a mineral from which a metal can be extracted profitably; (iii) the impurities (soil, sand, etc.) in a mined ore.

    Common trap: Defining an ore as "any rock containing a metal". The key word is profitably.

Intext p.53, Q3From oxide to metal2 marks

What chemical process is used for obtaining a metal from its oxide?

Given: a metal oxide. Asked: the process that gives the metal.

Concept used: removal of oxygen is reduction (Chapter 1, Topic 6). Why it applies: the oxide must lose its oxygen.

  1. 1

    Getting a metal from its oxide means removing oxygen → this is reduction.

  2. 2

    For metals in the middle of the series, the oxide is heated with a reducing agent such as carbon: ZnO+C→Zn+CO\ce{ZnO + C -> Zn + CO}ZnO+C​Zn+CO.

  3. 3

    A more reactive metal can also be used (displacement): FeX2OX3+2 Al→2 Fe+AlX2OX3\ce{Fe2O3 + 2Al -> 2Fe + Al2O3}FeX2​OX3​+2Al​2Fe+AlX2​OX3​.

  4. 4

    Very reactive metals (Na, Mg, Ca, Al) are obtained by electrolytic reduction.

  5. Check: in each equation the metal oxide loses oxygen ✔. ZnO: Zn 1, O 1, C 1 each side; FeX2OX3\ce{Fe2O3}FeX2​OX3​: Fe 2, O 3, Al 2 each side ✔.

  6. Answer Reduction — e.g. heating the oxide with carbon: ZnO+C→Zn+CO\ce{ZnO + C -> Zn + CO}ZnO+C​Zn+CO

    Common trap: Answering "roasting" or "calcination". Those make the oxide; reduction turns the oxide into the metal.

Intext p.55, Q2Metals that do not corrode easily1 mark

Which metals do not corrode easily?

Given: — Asked: metals that resist corrosion.

Concept used: the less reactive a metal, the less easily it corrodes (Topics 4 and 7).

  1. 1

    Corrosion is a reaction of the metal with air, moisture and other substances.

  2. 2

    Metals at the bottom of the reactivity series react least → they corrode least.

  3. 3

    Examples: gold and platinum (silver tarnishes only slowly).

  5. Answer Less reactive metals such as gold and platinum (and, to a lesser extent, silver).

Intext p.55, Q3Alloys1 mark

What are alloys?

Given: — Asked: a definition.

Concept used: alloys (Topic 7).

  1. 1

    An alloy is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or of a metal and a non-metal.

  2. 2

    It is made by melting the main metal and dissolving the other elements in it in definite proportions, then cooling.

  3. 3

    Examples: brass (copper + zinc), bronze (copper + tin), solder (lead + tin), steel (iron + carbon).

  4. Check: steel contains carbon, a non-metal — so the definition must include "metal and a non-metal" ✔.

  5. Answer Alloys are homogeneous mixtures of two or more metals, or of a metal and a non-metal (e.g. brass, bronze, steel).

    Common trap: Calling an alloy a compound. Its parts are mixed, not chemically combined.

Exercise Q5Hammer, battery, bulb, wires and a switch5 marks

You are given a hammer, a battery, a bulb, wires and a switch.
(a) How could you use them to distinguish between samples of metals and non-metals?
(b) Assess the usefulness of these tests in distinguishing between metals and non-metals.

Given: a hammer and the parts of a circuit. Asked: (a) two tests; (b) how reliable they are.

Concept used: malleability and electrical conductivity; the exceptions (Topic 2). Why it applies: the hammer tests malleability; the circuit tests conduction.

  1. 1

    (a)

    Hammer test (malleability): place the sample on a hard surface and strike it with the hammer. A metal flattens into a sheet; a non-metal solid breaks into pieces (it is brittle).

  2. 2

    Conductivity test: connect the battery, bulb and switch in series with wires, leaving a gap between two wire ends. Place the sample across the gap and close the switch.

  3. 3

    If the bulb glows, the sample conducts electricity → likely a metal. If it does not glow → likely a non-metal.

  4. 4

    (b)

    The tests are useful: most metals are malleable and conduct; most non-metals are brittle and do not.

  5. 5

    But they are not conclusive, because of exceptions:

    • graphite (a non-metal) conducts electricity, so the bulb glows;
    • very soft metals such as sodium and potassium cannot be tested safely with a hammer, and they are not tested in this way;
    • the hammer test cannot be done on liquids such as mercury (metal) or bromine (non-metal), or on gases.
  6. 6

    So the tests give a good first guess, which should be confirmed with chemical tests (for example, whether the oxide is basic or acidic).

  7. Check: apply both tests to iron (flattens, bulb glows → metal ✔) and to sulphur (shatters, no glow → non-metal ✔); graphite shows the limit (brittle but glows) ✔.

  8. Answer (a) Hammer: metals flatten, non-metals shatter. Circuit: the bulb glows with a metal, not with a non-metal. (b) Useful, but not foolproof — graphite conducts, soft or liquid metals cannot be hammer-tested, so chemical tests are needed to be sure.

    Common trap: In (b), writing only "the tests are useful". The word assess asks you to say where they fail as well.
