Chemistry toolkit — atoms, electrons and ions

About 15–20 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…

  • Work out the valency of an element and the ion its atom forms (Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Al³⁺, Cl⁻, F⁻, O²⁻, S²⁻, N³⁻).

Why this lesson exists

This chapter explains why sodium bursts into flame in water but gold stays bright for centuries — and the answer is always "count the outer electrons". Topic 5 (ionic compounds) depends on it completely. If you remember electron arrangements from Class 9, prove it below and skip ahead to Topic 2.

Sodium and chlorine are both dangerous on their own: one catches fire in water, the other is a poisonous green gas. Put them together and you get the salt you sprinkle on your raita. What changed? Only where one electron went. Before you can understand metals and non-metals, you need to be able to count electrons.

  1. Electrons live in shells

An atom has a tiny nucleus (protons and neutrons) with electrons arranged around it in shells, named K, L, M, N from the inside out. The atomic number tells you how many protons — and, in a neutral atom, how many electrons — the atom has.

For the first 20 elements, fill the shells in order:

Shell K L M N
Electrons it takes (elements 1–20) 2 8 8 the rest (1 or 2)

So sodium (11 electrons) is 2, 8, 1 and calcium (20 electrons) is 2, 8, 8, 2.

The electrons in the outermost shell are the valence electrons. They decide how an element reacts.

  1. Why atoms react: the full outer shell

The noble gases — helium (2), neon (2, 8), argon (2, 8, 8) — have a completely filled outer shell and hardly react at all. Other atoms react so that they, too, end up with a full outer shell (8 electrons, or 2 if only the K shell is left). This is called reaching a stable octet.

There are two ways to get there by transferring electrons:

  • Lose the few outer electrons, so that the full shell underneath becomes the outer shell. Atoms with 1, 2 or 3 valence electrons do this — these are metals.
  • Gain a few electrons to fill the outer shell. Atoms with 5, 6 or 7 valence electrons do this — these are non-metals.

(Atoms with 4 valence electrons, such as carbon, usually share electrons instead. You will meet sharing in Chapter 4.)

  1. Ions and valency

When an atom loses or gains electrons, the numbers of protons and electrons are no longer equal, so it becomes a charged particle — an ion.

  • Losing electrons gives a positive ion (cation). Sodium: 11 protons, 10 electrons → NaX+\ce{Na+}NaX+.
  • Gaining electrons gives a negative ion (anion). Chlorine: 17 protons, 18 electrons → ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX−.

The number of electrons an atom loses or gains is its valency, and it is also the size of the charge on the ion.

Valence electrons What the atom does Valency Ions in this chapter
1 loses 1 1 NaX+\ce{Na+}NaX+, KX+\ce{K+}KX+
2 loses 2 2 MgX2+\ce{Mg^2+}MgX2+, CaX2+\ce{Ca^2+}CaX2+
3 loses 3 3 AlX3+\ce{Al^3+}AlX3+
5 gains 3 3 NX3−\ce{N^3-}NX3− (nitride)
6 gains 2 2 OX2−\ce{O^2-}OX2− (oxide), SX2−\ce{S^2-}SX2− (sulphide)
7 gains 1 1 ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX− (chloride), FX−\ce{F-}FX− (fluoride)

Notice the name change for a negative ion made from one atom: chlorine → chloride, oxygen → oxide, sulphur → sulphide.

  1. Writing the formula of an ionic compound

A compound of a metal and a non-metal is made of ions, and the whole compound has no overall charge. So the formula must make the positive and negative charges add up to zero.

  1. Write the positive (metal) ion first and the negative ion second.
  2. Write each charge above its ion.
  3. Cross the numbers over to become subscripts (criss-cross).
  4. Simplify if both numbers share a factor; never write a subscript 1.
  5. Check: total positive charge = total negative charge.

You will also need the polyatomic ions from Chapter 2 (sulphate SOX4X2−\ce{SO4^2-}SOX4​X2−, nitrate NOX3X−\ce{NO3-}NOX3​X−, carbonate COX3X2−\ce{CO3^2-}COX3​X2−, hydroxide OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX−) for the salts in Topic 4.

Quick reminder — balancing (Chapter 1)

In a balanced equation every element has the same number of atoms on both sides. Change only the big numbers in front (coefficients), never a formula. In equations with ions or electrons, the total charge must also be the same on both sides.

Worked example 1

Arrangement and ion for four elements

Question. For potassium (Z = 19), calcium (Z = 20), fluorine (Z = 9) and nitrogen (Z = 7), write the electron arrangement and the ion each forms.

Given: four atomic numbers. Asked: arrangement and ion for each.

Concept used: fill shells 2, 8, 8, then the rest; atoms with 1–3 valence electrons lose them, atoms with 5–7 gain enough to reach 8. Why it applies: all four are among elements 1–20.

  1. 1

    Potassium

    19 electrons: 2 in K, 8 in L, 8 in M, 1 left for N → 2, 8, 8, 1.

  2. 2

    One valence electron → it loses 1 → KX+\ce{K+}KX+ (2, 8, 8).

  3. 3

    Calcium

    20 electrons: 2, 8, 8, 2 left for N → 2, 8, 8, 2.

  4. 4

    Two valence electrons → it loses 2 → CaX2+\ce{Ca^2+}CaX2+ (2, 8, 8).

  5. 5

    Fluorine

    9 electrons: 2 in K, 7 in L → 2, 7.

  6. 6

    Seven valence electrons → it gains 1 → FX−\ce{F-}FX− (2, 8).

  7. 7

    Nitrogen

    7 electrons: 2 in K, 5 in L → 2, 5.

  8. 8

    Five valence electrons → it gains 3 → NX3−\ce{N^3-}NX3− (2, 8).

  9. Check: count protons and electrons in each ion — KX+\ce{K+}KX+: 19 p, 18 e → +1 ✔; CaX2+\ce{Ca^2+}CaX2+: 20 p, 18 e → +2 ✔; FX−\ce{F-}FX−: 9 p, 10 e → −1 ✔; NX3−\ce{N^3-}NX3−: 7 p, 10 e → −3 ✔.

  10. Answer K 2, 8, 8, 1 → KX+\ce{K+}KX+ · Ca 2, 8, 8, 2 → CaX2+\ce{Ca^2+}CaX2+ · F 2, 7 → FX−\ce{F-}FX− · N 2, 5 → NX3−\ce{N^3-}NX3−

    Common trap: Writing potassium as 2, 8, 9. For elements 1–20, the M shell stops at 8 and the 19th electron starts the N shell.

Worked example 2

Formulae of three ionic compounds

Question. Write the formulae of (a) potassium sulphide, (b) calcium fluoride, (c) aluminium oxide.

Given: three names, each a metal with a non-metal. Asked: their formulae.

Concept used: criss-cross the ion charges so that the total charge is zero. Why it applies: each compound is made of a metal ion and a non-metal ion.

  1. 1

    (a) Potassium sulphide

    Ions: KX+\ce{K+}KX+ (+1) and SX2−\ce{S^2-}SX2− (−2).

  2. 2

    Cross over: K gets 2, S gets 1 → KX2S\ce{K2S}KX2​S.

  3. 3

    (b) Calcium fluoride

    Ions: CaX2+\ce{Ca^2+}CaX2+ (+2) and FX−\ce{F-}FX− (−1).

  4. 4

    Cross over: Ca gets 1, F gets 2 → CaFX2\ce{CaF2}CaFX2​.

  5. 5

    (c) Aluminium oxide

    Ions: AlX3+\ce{Al^3+}AlX3+ (+3) and OX2−\ce{O^2-}OX2− (−2).

  6. 6

    Cross over: Al gets 2, O gets 3 → AlX2OX3\ce{Al2O3}AlX2​OX3​.

  7. Check: (a) 2 × (+1) + (−2) = 0 ✔ (b) (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✔ (c) 2 × (+3) + 3 × (−2) = +6 − 6 = 0 ✔.

  8. Answer (a) KX2S\ce{K2S}KX2​S (b) CaFX2\ce{CaF2}CaFX2​ (c) AlX2OX3\ce{Al2O3}AlX2​OX3​

    Common trap: Writing AlO\ce{AlO}AlO "because aluminium and oxygen are one of each". The charges +3 and −2 do not cancel — you need two Al and three O.

Magnesium has atomic number 12. Write the electron arrangement of the magnesium atom and of the magnesium ion. How many protons and electrons does the ion have?

Answer: Atom 2, 8, 2; ion MgX2+\ce{Mg^2+}MgX2+ 2, 8; the ion has 12 protons and 10 electrons.

Common mistakes!

  • Potassium = 2, 8, 9

    For elements 1–20, the M shell takes only 8 before N starts.

    2, 8, 8, 1.

  • "Chlorine has valency 7."

    It needs to gain 1 to complete its octet.

    Valency = electrons lost or gained = 1.

  • Thinking the nucleus changes when an ion forms

    Only electrons move in a reaction; protons stay the same.

    NaX+\ce{Na+}NaX+ still has 11 protons, now with 10 electrons.

  • MgX2OX2\ce{Mg2O2}MgX2​OX2​

    Subscripts that share a factor must be simplified.

    MgO\ce{MgO}MgO.

Key points

  • Shells fill 2, 8, 8, then the rest (elements 1–20); outer-shell electrons are valence electrons.
  • Atoms react to reach a full outer shell like a noble gas.
  • Metals (1–3 valence electrons) lose electrons → positive ions; non-metals (5–7) gain electrons → negative ions.
  • Valency = number of electrons lost or gained = size of the ion's charge.
  • Ionic formula: criss-cross the charges, simplify, check that the charges add to zero.
