Topic 5 of 7

Ionic compounds

About 45 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Draw electron-dot structures of sodium, magnesium, oxygen and chlorine, and show the formation of NaCl, MgCl₂, MgO and Na₂O by the transfer of electrons.
  • Explain the properties of ionic compounds — hard, brittle solids; high melting and boiling points; soluble in water but not in kerosene or petrol; conduct electricity when molten or dissolved but not as solids.

Ice melts at 0 °C. Wax melts in a candle flame. But common salt stays solid in a hot frying pan — it melts only at about 800 °C. And yet a pinch of salt in water lets the water carry an electric current, which is why you must never touch a switch with wet hands. Both facts have the same cause: salt is not made of molecules at all, but of ions.

Quick reminder (Topic 1)

Metals have 1–3 valence electrons and lose them; non-metals have 5–7 and gain electrons. Both end up with a full outer shell.

  1. Electron-dot structures

An electron-dot structure shows the symbol of an element with its valence electrons drawn as dots or crosses around it. Inner shells are not drawn. In this chapter we draw the metal's electrons as crosses (×) and the non-metal's electrons as dots (•), so you can follow each electron when it moves.

Count the dots every time: the number around the symbol must equal the number of valence electrons.

  1. Forming sodium chloride: give and take

  • Sodium (2, 8, 1) loses its one outer electron. Its L shell (8) becomes the outer shell. It still has 11 protons but only 10 electrons → a sodium cation, NaX+\ce{Na+}NaX+.
  • Chlorine (2, 8, 7) needs one more electron to complete its octet. It takes the electron sodium lost: 17 protons, 18 electrons → a chloride anion, ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX−.

The oppositely charged ions attract each other and are held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction. Sodium chloride does not exist as molecules: a crystal of salt is a huge, regular stack of NaX+\ce{Na+}NaX+ and ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX− ions.

  1. Forming magnesium chloride

Magnesium (2, 8, 2) must lose two electrons, but one chlorine atom can take only one. So two chlorine atoms are needed:

The ions present in MgClX2\ce{MgCl2}MgClX2​ are the magnesium cation MgX2+\ce{Mg^2+}MgX2+ and the chloride anion ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX−, in the ratio 1 : 2.

Compounds formed like this — by the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal — are called ionic compounds or electrovalent compounds.

  1. How to draw any electron-dot formation — six steps

  1. Write the electron arrangement of each atom.
  2. Draw each atom's valence electrons (× for the metal, • for the non-metal).
  3. Decide how many electrons each atom loses or gains to reach a full shell.
  4. Decide how many atoms of each you need so that electrons lost = electrons gained.
  5. Draw a curved arrow from each moving cross to the non-metal that receives it.
  6. Draw the ions: the metal ion in brackets with no dots (its old outer shell is empty) and the charge outside; the non-metal ion in brackets with eight electrons and the charge outside.

Property What it is Why
Physical nature Solids, somewhat hard; generally brittle — they break into pieces when pressure is applied Strong forces of attraction between positive and negative ions
Melting and boiling points High A lot of energy is needed to break the strong attraction between the ions
Solubility Generally soluble in water; insoluble in kerosene, petrol and similar solvents
Conduction of electricity Do not conduct as solids; do conduct when molten or dissolved in water Current in a liquid is carried by moving ions. In a solid the ions are held in a rigid structure and cannot move. When the compound melts (heat overcomes the attraction) or dissolves, the ions move freely to the opposite electrodes.

Worked example 1

Potassium chloride

Question. Show the formation of potassium chloride from potassium (Z = 19) and chlorine (Z = 17) by the transfer of electrons. Name the ions present.

Given: K (Z = 19), Cl (Z = 17). Asked: electron-dot formation; ions.

Concept used: the six-step method (this topic). Why it applies: potassium is a metal and chlorine a non-metal, so electrons are transferred.

  1. 1

    Arrangements: K 2, 8, 8, 1; Cl 2, 8, 7.

  2. 2

    Valence electrons: K has 1 (draw one ×); Cl has 7 (draw seven •).

  3. 3

    K loses 1 electron; Cl gains 1 electron.

  4. 4

    Electrons lost (1) = electrons gained (1) → one K for one Cl.

  5. 5

    The cross moves from K to Cl:

    K×  +  ∙∙Cl∙∙∙∙∙  ⟶  [K]+[∙∙Cl∙∙∙∙×∙]−\mathrm{K}^{\times} \;+\; {}^{\bullet}_{\bullet}\overset{\bullet\bullet}{\underset{\bullet\bullet}{\mathrm{Cl}}}{}^{\bullet} \;\longrightarrow\; \left[\mathrm{K}\right]^{+}\left[{}^{\bullet}_{\bullet}\overset{\bullet\bullet}{\underset{\bullet\bullet}{\mathrm{Cl}}}{}^{\bullet}_{\times}\right]^{-}K×+∙∙​∙∙Cl​∙∙​∙⟶[K]+[∙∙​∙∙Cl​∙∙​×∙​]−Swipe to see the whole equation →
  6. 6

    New arrangements: KX+\ce{K+}KX+ 2, 8, 8 and ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX− 2, 8, 8 — both full.

  7. Check: total charge +1 − 1 = 0 ✔; electrons drawn on the left 1 + 7 = 8 = electrons drawn on the right 0 + 8 ✔.

  8. Answer Formation shown above; ions KX+\ce{K+}KX+ and ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX−; formula KCl.

    Common trap: Drawing a shared pair between K and Cl. A metal and a non-metal transfer electrons; they do not share.

Worked example 2

Calcium oxide

Question. Show the formation of calcium oxide from calcium (Z = 20) and oxygen (Z = 8) by the transfer of electrons. Write the arrangement of each ion.

Given: Ca (Z = 20), O (Z = 8). Asked: electron-dot formation; ion arrangements.

Concept used: the six-step method. Why it applies: calcium is a metal, oxygen a non-metal.

  1. 1

    Arrangements: Ca 2, 8, 8, 2; O 2, 6.

  2. 2

    Valence electrons: Ca has 2 (two ×); O has 6 (six •).

  3. 3

    Ca loses 2; O gains 2.

  4. 4

    2 lost = 2 gained → one Ca for one O.

  5. 5

    Both crosses move from Ca to O:

  6. 6

    Ion arrangements: CaX2+\ce{Ca^2+}CaX2+ 2, 8, 8; OX2−\ce{O^2-}OX2− 2, 8.

Worked example 3

Explaining two properties

Question. (a) Solid potassium chloride does not conduct electricity, but molten potassium chloride does. Why? (b) Why does calcium oxide have a very high melting point (2850 K)?

Given: two observations about ionic compounds. Asked: reasons.

Concept used: ionic compounds are made of ions held by strong electrostatic forces (this topic). Why it applies: both properties come from the ions and the forces between them.

  1. 1

    (a)

    Electric current through a substance needs moving charged particles.

  2. 2

    KCl is made of KX+\ce{K+}KX+ and ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX− ions.

  3. 3

    In the solid, the ions are held in a rigid structure and cannot move → no current.

  4. 4

    On melting, heat overcomes the attraction, the ions move freely to the electrodes → current flows.

  5. 5

    (b)

    In CaO, CaX2+\ce{Ca^2+}CaX2+ and OX2−\ce{O^2-}OX2− ions are held by strong electrostatic forces of attraction.

  6. 6

    Melting means separating these ions, which needs a large amount of energy → very high melting point.

  7. Check: if (a) were caused by "no ions in the solid", the solution of KCl would not conduct either — but it does. So the ions are there all along; what changes is whether they can move ✔.

  8. Answer (a) Ions in the solid cannot move; in the melt they move freely. (b) Strong attraction between the ions needs a lot of energy to overcome.

    Common trap: Saying "molten KCl conducts because electrons flow through it". In ionic compounds it is the ions that carry the charge.

Quick check · 1 of 3MCQ1 mark

In the electron-dot structure of a chloride ion, how many electrons are drawn around Cl, and what charge is written outside the bracket?

7 is the chlorine atom, before it gains an electron.
Seven of chlorine's own plus one gained = a full octet of 8; one extra electron → charge −1.
Gaining an electron makes the ion negative.
That is the number of electrons gained, not the number drawn.
Quick check · 2 of 3Short answer2 marks

In the formation of magnesium chloride, how many electrons does each magnesium atom lose, how many chlorine atoms are needed, and what are the ions present?

Answer: Magnesium loses 2 electrons; 2 chlorine atoms are needed (each gains 1); ions MgX2+\ce{Mg^2+}MgX2+ and ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX−.

Electrons lost must equal electrons gained: 2 = 1 + 1. Drawing only one chlorine atom is the most common slip.

Common mistakes!

  • Drawing all of an atom's electrons (e.g. 11 for Na)

    Electron-dot structures show valence electrons only.

    Na: one ×; Cl: seven •.

  • Wrong valence count (O with 8 dots, Cl with 8 dots before reacting)

    Count from the arrangement: O is 2, 6; Cl is 2, 8, 7.

    Write the arrangement first, then draw.

  • Missing brackets or charges on the ions

    Ions are charged particles.

    [Na]+\left[\mathrm{Na}\right]^{+}[Na]+, […]−\left[\ldots\right]^{-}[…]− every time.

  • Showing a shared pair in NaCl

    Metal + non-metal → transfer, not sharing.

    Arrows from the metal's crosses to the non-metal.

  • One Cl for one Mg

    Mg loses 2 electrons; each Cl takes only 1.

    Two Cl atoms — MgClX2\ce{MgCl2}MgClX2​.

  • Dots left on the metal ion

    Its outer electrons have gone.

    Metal ion in brackets with no dots.

  • "Ionic solids conduct electricity."

    Ions are fixed in the solid.

    Conduct only when molten or dissolved.

  • "Ionic compounds dissolve in petrol."

    They are insoluble in kerosene and petrol.

    Soluble in water.

Key points

  • Electron-dot structures show valence electrons only (× for the metal, • for the non-metal).
  • Ionic (electrovalent) compounds form by transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal; the ions are held by strong electrostatic forces.
  • NaCl: Na gives 1 electron to Cl. MgClX2\ce{MgCl2}MgClX2​: Mg gives 1 electron to each of two Cl. MgO: Mg gives 2 electrons to O.
  • Ionic compounds: hard, brittle solids; high melting and boiling points; soluble in water, insoluble in kerosene and petrol.
  • They conduct when molten or in solution (ions free to move), not as solids.
