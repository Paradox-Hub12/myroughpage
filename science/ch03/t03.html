Topic 3 of 7

Metals with oxygen and water

About 45 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Write equations for metals burning in oxygen, and classify oxides as basic, acidic, neutral or amphoteric (aluminium oxide, zinc oxide).
  • Describe the reactions of metals with cold water, hot water and steam (Na, K, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe), and name the metals that do not react with water.
  • Explain why sodium and potassium are stored in kerosene and why some metals are protected by an oxide layer.

In the school lab there is a small bottle in which grey lumps of sodium sit under kerosene — never in the open air, never in water. On the same shelf, a roll of magnesium ribbon lies happily in a plastic bag, and a gold-plated medal has kept its shine for thirty years. Three metals, three very different moods. Oxygen and water reveal how reactive a metal is.

Quick reminder (Chapter 2)

Metal oxides react with acids to give a salt and water, so they are basic. Non-metal oxides react with bases, so they are acidic. Balancing: change only coefficients; check every element.

Most metals give basic oxides; most non-metals give acidic oxides (a few non-metal oxides, such as carbon monoxide, CO, are neutral). This chemical difference sorts elements more reliably than the physical tests of Topic 2.

  1. Metal + oxygen → metal oxide

Almost all metals combine with oxygen. Copper heated in air turns into black copper(II) oxide, and aluminium forms aluminium oxide:

4 Al+3 OX2→2 AlX2OX3\ce{4Al + 3O2 -> 2Al2O3}4Al+3OX2​​2AlX2​OX3​Swipe to see the whole equation →

Amphoteric oxides. Most metal oxides are basic. But aluminium oxide and zinc oxide react with both acids and bases to give a salt and water. Oxides like these are called amphoteric oxides:

  1. Not all metals react with oxygen equally

Teacher demonstration — heating metals in a flame (NCERT Activity 3.9). Your teacher holds samples of different metals in a flame with tongs. Safety: eye protection; never look straight at a burning metal; sodium and potassium are not used.

Metal What happens with oxygen
Potassium, sodium React so vigorously that they catch fire if left in the open air.
Magnesium Burns with a dazzling white flame.
Magnesium, aluminium, zinc, lead At room temperature, covered by a thin layer of oxide that protects the metal from further oxidation.
Iron A lump does not burn on heating, but iron filings sprinkled into a flame burn vigorously (sparks).
Copper Does not burn; the hot metal gets a black coating of copper(II) oxide.
Silver, gold Do not react with oxygen even at high temperatures.

This — together with the fact that sodium catches fire in air and is stored under kerosene — tells us sodium is more reactive than magnesium, and both are far more reactive than silver and gold. But burning does not help us decide the order of zinc, iron, copper and lead — for that we need water, acids and displacement (Topic 4).

Why sodium and potassium are kept under kerosene. They react vigorously with the oxygen and moisture in air and can catch fire. Kerosene does not react with them and keeps air and water away. This protects the metal and prevents accidental fires.

Anodising makes aluminium's natural oxide layer thicker. The cleaned aluminium object is connected as the anode while dilute sulphuric acid is electrolysed. Oxygen released at its surface combines with the aluminium and thickens the protective oxide coat, which can then be coloured with dyes. (You only need the name "anodising" as a way of preventing corrosion — Topic 7.)

  1. Metal + water → metal oxide (or hydroxide) + hydrogen

When a metal reacts with water, it forms a metal oxide and hydrogen. If the oxide dissolves in water, it goes on to form the metal hydroxide.

Teacher demonstration — metals in cold water, hot water and steam (NCERT Activity 3.10). Small pieces of metal are put in cold water; those that do not react are tried in hot water, and those that still do not react are heated in steam (see the visual below). Safety: sodium and potassium are shown only by the teacher, as tiny pieces, behind a safety screen.

Two points to notice:

  • Cold water gives a hydroxide; steam gives an oxide. Iron and steam give FeX3OX4\ce{Fe3O4}FeX3​OX4​, a black oxide.
  • Aluminium is quite reactive, yet aluminium pots do not react with boiling water — the protective oxide layer keeps the water away.

Worked example 1

Two metals burning

Question. Write balanced equations for (a) sodium burning in oxygen to form sodium oxide, (b) zinc burning in oxygen. Will each oxide turn red litmus blue?

Given: two metals reacting with oxygen. Asked: balanced equations; the nature of each oxide.

Concept used: metal + oxygen → metal oxide; formula from ions (Topic 1); sodium oxide forms an alkali, zinc oxide is amphoteric. Why it applies: both are metals burning in oxygen.

  1. 1

    (a) Sodium

    Formula of the oxide: NaX+\ce{Na+}NaX+ and OX2−\ce{O^2-}OX2− → NaX2O\ce{Na2O}NaX2​O.

  2. 2

    Skeletal: Na+OX2→NaX2O\ce{Na + O2 -> Na2O}Na+OX2​​NaX2​O.

  3. 3

    Oxygen: left 2, right 1 → put 2 in front of NaX2O\ce{Na2O}NaX2​O.

  4. 4

    Sodium: right is now 2 × 2 = 4 → put 4 in front of Na.

Classifying three oxides

Which water, and what product?

    Magnesium

    Condition: put it in hot water. It slowly gives bubbles and floats.

Quick check · 3 of 4Short answer2 marks

Potassium is stored under kerosene, but magnesium ribbon can be kept in the open. Explain both.

Answer: Potassium reacts so vigorously with oxygen and moisture in air that it can catch fire; kerosene keeps air and water away. Magnesium is less reactive, and at room temperature its surface gets a thin protective oxide layer that stops further reaction.

Both halves are needed: "very reactive → kept away from air and water" and "oxide layer protects magnesium".

A piece of calcium is dropped into water. Write the balanced equation and say why the calcium starts to float.

Common mistakes!

  • "All metal oxides are basic."

    Aluminium oxide and zinc oxide are amphoteric.

    Basic in general; AlX2OX3\ce{Al2O3}AlX2​OX3​ and ZnO react with acids and bases.

  • Steam reactions giving hydroxides

    Steam gives the oxide.

    Fe → FeX3OX4\ce{Fe3O4}FeX3​OX4​; Al → AlX2OX3\ce{Al2O3}AlX2​OX3​; Zn → ZnO.

  • "Magnesium reacts with cold water."

    It reacts with hot water.

    Cold: K, Na, Ca · hot: Mg · steam: Al, Zn, Fe · none: Pb, Cu, Ag, Au.

  • "Sodium is kept in water / in air."

    It reacts violently with both.

    Under kerosene.

  • "Calcium floats because it is light."

    It floats because hydrogen bubbles stick to it.

    Give the bubble reason (Magnesium too).

Key points

  • Metal + oxygen → metal oxide. Metal oxides are mostly basic; AlX2OX3\ce{Al2O3}AlX2​OX3​ and ZnO are amphoteric; NaX2O\ce{Na2O}NaX2​O and KX2O\ce{K2O}KX2​O dissolve to form alkalis.
  • Non-metal oxides are acidic (or neutral, like CO).
  • K, Na catch fire in air → stored under kerosene; Mg, Al, Zn, Pb have a protective oxide layer; Cu gets black CuO; Ag, Au do not react.
  • Water: cold — K, Na (violent, HX2\ce{H2}HX2​ catches fire), Ca (floats); hot — Mg; steam — Al, Zn, Fe (give oxides); no reaction — Pb, Cu, Ag, Au.
  • Metal + water → metal oxide + hydrogen; a soluble oxide then forms the hydroxide.
