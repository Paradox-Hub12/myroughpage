Topic 2 of 7

Physical properties of metals and non-metals

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe lustre, hardness, malleability, ductility, conduction of heat and electricity, sonority and melting point for metals and non-metals.
  • State the important exceptions (mercury, gallium and caesium, sodium and potassium, iodine, diamond, graphite, bromine).
  • Classify an element from a set of observations, including the hammer–battery–bulb test, and judge how reliable the test is.

Look around your kitchen. The kadhai is iron, the pressure cooker is aluminium, the old lota is copper, a strip of foil wraps the roti, and a thin copper wire runs inside every plug. Now look at the coal in a sigri, the yellow sulphur in a firecracker, the oxygen you breathe. Why do we make cooking pots from the first group and never from the second? The answer is in their physical properties — what you can see, feel and measure without changing the substance.

  1. Seven properties of metals

The activities below are NCERT Activities 3.1–3.6. Each one tests one property.

Property What you do What you observe What it shows
Lustre Rub iron, copper, aluminium and magnesium with sandpaper. The dull surface becomes shiny. Pure metals have a shining surface — metallic lustre. (The dullness was a coating of oxide or dirt.)
Hardness Try to cut iron, copper, aluminium and magnesium with a knife. They cannot be cut. Metals are generally hard — but hardness varies (see the exceptions below).
Malleability Hammer pieces of iron, zinc, lead and copper on an iron block. They flatten into sheets instead of breaking. Metals can be beaten into thin sheets — malleability. Gold and silver are the most malleable.
Ductility List the metal wires you see every day. Copper and aluminium wires, iron wire, gold and silver wire in jewellery. Metals can be drawn into thin wires — ductility. Gold is the most ductile: about 2 km of wire can be drawn from just one gram.
Heat conduction Clamp an aluminium or copper wire, fix a pin to its free end with wax, and heat the clamped end. After some time the wax melts and the pin falls; the wire does not melt. Metals are good conductors of heat and have high melting points.
Electrical conduction Put a metal between two terminals in a circuit with a cell and a bulb. The bulb glows. Metals are good conductors of electricity.
Sonority Strike a metal plate or bell with a hard object. It gives a ringing sound. Metals are sonorous — this is why school bells are made of metal.

Best and poorest conductors of heat: silver and copper are the best; lead and mercury are comparatively poor conductors.

Properties explain uses. Aluminium and copper make cooking vessels because they conduct heat well and have high melting points. Copper and aluminium make electric wires because they are ductile and conduct electricity. The wires in your home are coated with PVC or a rubber-like material because these do not conduct — the coating stops you getting a shock.

  1. Non-metals: mostly the opposite

There are far fewer non-metals than metals — for example carbon, sulphur, iodine, oxygen, hydrogen and nitrogen. Try the same tests on coal, sulphur and iodine (NCERT Activity 3.7) and you find that non-metals are generally:

  • solids or gases at room temperature (bromine is the only liquid non-metal);
  • dull (not lustrous);
  • brittle — solid non-metals break into powder when hammered, so they are neither malleable nor ductile;
  • poor conductors of heat and electricity;
  • not sonorous.
Property Metals Non-metals
State at room temperature solids (except mercury) solids or gases (bromine is a liquid)
Lustre lustrous dull (except iodine)
Hardness generally hard (except Li, Na, K) solids are brittle (diamond is the exception — the hardest natural substance)
Malleable and ductile? yes no
Heat and electricity good conductors poor conductors (except graphite, which conducts electricity)
Sonorous? yes no
Melting point generally high (except gallium, caesium) generally low (except diamond and graphite)

  1. The exceptions the board loves

Physical properties alone cannot sort every element, because there are exceptions:

  1. Mercury is a metal but it is a liquid at room temperature.
  2. Gallium and caesium are metals with such low melting points that they melt if you hold them on your palm.
  3. Lithium, sodium and potassium (the alkali metals) are soft enough to slice with a knife, like cold butter. They are also light (low density) and melt at low temperatures.
  4. Iodine is a non-metal but it is lustrous (shiny violet-black crystals).
  5. Carbon exists in different forms called allotropes. Diamond is the hardest natural substance, and it melts and boils only at very high temperatures. Graphite conducts electricity.
  6. Bromine is a non-metal that is a liquid.

Teacher demonstration — cutting sodium (NCERT Activity 3.2). Your teacher holds a piece of sodium with tongs, dries it between folds of filter paper, puts it on a watch-glass and cuts it with a knife. It cuts as easily as cold butter, and the fresh surface is shiny for a moment before it goes dull. Safety: sodium is never touched with bare hands and never brought near water — it reacts violently (Topic 3).

Because of these exceptions, elements are classified more reliably by their chemical properties. The most useful one is the nature of their oxides: metal oxides are generally basic and non-metal oxides are acidic — you will test this at the start of Topic 3.

  1. The hammer–battery–bulb test

With a hammer, a battery, a bulb, wires and a switch you can test two properties:

  • Hammer: a metal flattens (malleable); a non-metal solid breaks into pieces (brittle).
  • Circuit: put the sample between the two wire ends with the battery, bulb and switch in series. If the bulb glows, the sample conducts — it is likely to be a metal.

The test is useful but not foolproof. Graphite would make the bulb glow although it is a non-metal, and very soft metals such as sodium cannot be tested safely this way. The hammer test also cannot be used on liquids, such as mercury and bromine, or on gases. So two matching results make a good guess, not a proof.

Worked example 1

Classifying two unknown elements

Question. A student tests two elements, X and Y.

    • X is a grey solid. Hammered, it flattens into a sheet. A rod of X rings when struck, and its fresh surface is shiny.
    • Y is a yellow solid. Hammered, it breaks into powder. It does not conduct electricity in a circuit.

    Classify X and Y as metal or non-metal, giving two reasons for each.

    Given: observations on X and Y. Asked: metal or non-metal, with reasons.

    Concept used: metals are lustrous, malleable, sonorous and conducting; non-metals are brittle and non-conducting (this topic). Why it applies: every observation is one of these properties.

  2. 1

    X flattens when hammered → malleable → a metal property.

  3. 2

    X rings when struck → sonorous → a metal property.

  4. 3

    X's fresh surface is shiny → lustrous → a metal property.

  5. 4

    So X is a metal.

  6. 5

    Y breaks into powder → brittle → a non-metal property.

  7. 6

    Y does not conduct electricity → a non-metal property.

  8. 7

    So Y is a non-metal (the yellow colour suggests sulphur).

  9. Check: compare with known elements — X behaves like iron (malleable, sonorous, lustrous) and Y like sulphur (yellow, brittle, non-conducting); iron is a metal and sulphur a non-metal ✔.

  10. Answer X is a metal (malleable, sonorous, lustrous); Y is a non-metal (brittle, does not conduct electricity).

    Common trap: Using colour as a reason. "Grey" or "yellow" tells you nothing on its own — use properties such as malleability or conductivity.

Worked example 2

An exceptions question

Question. Which statement is correct?
(a) All metals are solids at room temperature.
(b) Graphite, a non-metal, conducts electricity.
(c) All non-metals are dull.
(d) Sodium is a hard metal that cannot be cut with a knife.

Given: four general statements. Asked: the correct one.

Concept used: the list of exceptions (this topic). Why it applies: each wrong statement is a general rule stated as if it had no exception.

  1. 1

    (a) Mercury is a liquid metal → (a) is wrong.

  2. 2

    (b) Graphite is an allotrope of carbon (a non-metal) and it conducts electricity → (b) is correct.

  3. 3

    (c) Iodine is a lustrous non-metal → (c) is wrong.

  4. 4

    (d) Sodium is soft enough to cut with a knife → (d) is wrong.

  5. Check: test (b) another way — graphite is carbon, and carbon burns to COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​, an acidic oxide, so it is chemically a non-metal even though it conducts ✔.

  6. Answer (b)

    Common trap: Choosing (a) because "metals are solids" is the rule you learnt first. Words like all and never are a signal to look for an exception.

A metal that melts when you hold it in your palm is:

Mercury is already a liquid at room temperature — there is nothing left to melt.
Gallium (and caesium) have melting points just below body temperature.
Sodium is soft and has a low melting point (about 98 °C), but it does not melt in the hand — and it must never be held.

Quick check · 2 of 3Very short answer2 marks

Name the property of metals used in each case: (a) beating aluminium into foil, (b) making copper wires, (c) making a temple bell, (d) making a frying pan.

Answer: (a) malleability (b) ductility (and electrical conductivity) (c) sonority (d) good conduction of heat (and a high melting point).

Malleable = into sheets; ductile = into wires. Mixing these two words is the most common slip.

Common mistakes!

  • "All metals are hard solids with high melting points."

    Mercury is liquid; sodium and potassium are soft; gallium and caesium melt in the hand.

    Learn the rule and its exceptions together.

  • "Non-metals never conduct electricity."

    Graphite conducts electricity.

    Say "non-metals are poor conductors, except graphite."

  • "Non-metals are never lustrous."

    Iodine is lustrous.

    Iodine is the lustrous non-metal.

  • "Diamond is a metal because it is so hard."

    Diamond is carbon — a non-metal.

    Diamond: hardest natural substance, still a non-metal.

  • Mixing up malleable and ductile

    They are different shapes.

    Malleable → sheets (think mallet); ductile → wires.

  • "The only liquid element is mercury."

    Bromine is a liquid too.

    Mercury = liquid metal; bromine = liquid non-metal.

Key points

  • Metals are generally lustrous, hard, malleable, ductile, sonorous, good conductors of heat and electricity, with high melting points.
  • Non-metals are generally dull, brittle, non-conducting solids or gases.
  • Exceptions: mercury (liquid metal), gallium and caesium (melt in the palm), Li, Na, K (soft), iodine (lustrous), diamond (hardest natural substance), graphite (conducts), bromine (liquid non-metal).
  • Silver and copper are the best conductors of heat; lead and mercury are poor ones.
  • Physical tests (hammer, circuit) help, but chemical properties classify elements more reliably.
