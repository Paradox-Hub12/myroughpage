Chapter 3 · Unit I

Metals and Non-metals

7 topicsAbout 7½ hoursBoard exam
Why this chapter matters

The copper wire in your phone charger, the aluminium foil around your tiffin, the iron in a railway bridge, the gold in a wedding necklace, the chlorine that cleans the town's water and the carbon in your pencil — your day runs on metals and non-metals. Why does iron rust while gold stays bright for centuries? Why is sodium kept under kerosene? Why are railway tracks joined with a burning mixture of iron oxide and aluminium?

This chapter answers all of these with two big ideas. The first is the reactivity series — one list of metals, from the most reactive (potassium) to the least (gold), that predicts how a metal behaves with oxygen, water, acids and other metals' salts, and even how it must be extracted from its ore. The second is ionic bonding — how metals give electrons to non-metals to form compounds like common salt, and why those compounds have such high melting points. It is part of Unit I, Chemical Substances – Nature and Behaviour, and its electron-dot diagrams, reactivity questions and extraction equations come up in the board exam every year.

What you'll learn

  • Write the electron arrangement of elements 1–20; work out the ion an atom forms; write formulae of ionic compounds.
  • Describe lustre, hardness, malleability, ductility, conduction, sonority and melting point; learn the exceptions; classify an element from observations.
  • Write equations for metals burning; tell basic, acidic and amphoteric oxides apart; describe how metals react with cold water, hot water and steam; explain why sodium is kept in kerosene.
  • Write metal + acid equations; explain why copper and nitric acid give no hydrogen; predict displacement reactions; use the reactivity series.
  • Draw electron-dot structures and show how NaCl\ce{NaCl}NaCl, MgClX2\ce{MgCl2}MgClX2​ and MgO\ce{MgO}MgO form; explain the properties of ionic compounds.
  • Define mineral, ore and gangue; explain roasting, calcination, reduction (carbon, thermit, electrolysis) and electrolytic refining; choose the method from a metal's position.
  • Describe corrosion and the conditions for rusting; choose a way to prevent it; explain what alloys are and why we use them.
  • Use the reactivity ladder and the extraction flowchart to solve "identify the element X" problems.
