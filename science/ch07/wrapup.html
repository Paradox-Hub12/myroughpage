Wrap-up

One parent or two

About 35 minutes · 10 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why variation helps the species.
  • Tell the asexual methods apart.
  • Compare reproduction in unicellular and multicellular organisms.
  • Explain why sexual reproduction gives more variation.
  • Draw and label the longitudinal section of a flower.
  • Distinguish pollination from fertilisation.
  • Trace the sequence from pollination to seed and fruit.
  • Trace fertilisation, implantation and the placenta.
  • Label the human reproductive systems.
  • Compare the methods of contraception.

Every topic in this chapter answers one question: how is the DNA blueprint passed on to a new individual? With one parent, the copy is made by a single individual — fast, and the offspring are very alike. With two parents, DNA from two individuals is combined — slower, but every offspring is a new combination. Many questions on this chapter ask you to compare — for example "What are the advantages of sexual reproduction over asexual reproduction?" (NCERT Exercise Q4). This page puts the chapter on three cards and then works through five mixed questions.

Card 1 — Asexual and sexual reproduction

Point Asexual reproduction Sexual reproduction
Number of parents one two (male and female)
Gametes (germ-cells) not formed male and female gametes, each with half the chromosomes
Fertilisation and zygote none gametes fuse → zygote
Variation in offspring little — only from small errors in DNA copying more — DNA of two individuals is combined in new ways
Examples fission (Amoeba, Plasmodium), budding (Hydra, yeast), fragmentation (Spirogyra), vegetative propagation (potato, Bryophyllum), spores (Rhizopus) flowering plants (mustard, papaya), humans

Unicellular vs multicellular (NCERT Exercise Q9 in brief): a unicellular organism reproduces by dividing its one cell (fission; budding in yeast). Multicellular organisms have tissues and organs, so they need more complex methods: fragmentation if the body is very simple, regeneration or budding using specialised cells, vegetative propagation, spores from special reproductive parts, or sexual reproduction with gametes made in reproductive organs.

Card 2 — Two sequence cards

The flower — from pollen to seedling

Step Event Where
1 Pollination — pollen moves from anther to stigma stigma
2 Pollen tube grows down through the style style → ovary
3 Fertilisation — male germ-cell fuses with the egg ovule
4 Zygote divides → embryo ovule
5 Ovule → seed; ovary → fruit; other parts may fall off flower
6 Germination — embryo grows into a seedling soil

Humans — from gametes to birth

Step Event Where
1 Sperms made; egg released (one a month) testes; ovary
2 Sperms enter and travel upwards vagina → uterus → oviduct
3 Fertilisation → zygote oviduct (fallopian tube)
4 Zygote divides → embryo; implantation lining of the uterus
5 Placenta — glucose and oxygen in, wastes out uterine wall
6 About nine months; birth by rhythmic contractions uterus
— If not fertilised: lining breaks down → menstruation uterus → vagina

Card 3 — Draw it in the exam

Rules for every biology diagram

  1. Use a sharp pencil; draw neat outlines, no shading, about a third to half a page in size.
  2. Draw every label line with a ruler; lines must touch the part and must not cross.
  3. Write labels horizontally, lined up on one or both sides, in neat print.
  4. Write a title under the diagram (e.g. "Longitudinal section of a flower").
  5. Use brackets where your textbook groups parts (Pistil, Stamen), and arrows for stages or movement.
  6. Learn the label set — your textbook's figure labels are listed below exactly as printed.

Five mixed worked examples

Mixed example 1

Seeds or cuttings for a mango orchard?

Question. A farmer wants a new orchard of mango trees that all give the same sweet fruit as one old tree. Should she grow them from seeds or by grafting? Use the ideas of DNA copying and variation in your answer.

Given: one prized tree; a uniform orchard wanted. Asked: seeds or grafting, with reasons.

Concept used: asexual vs sexual reproduction (Card 1); variation (Topic 1); vegetative propagation (Topic 2). Why it applies: seeds come from sexual reproduction; grafting is vegetative.

  1. 1

    Seeds come from sexual reproduction: each seed carries a new combination of DNA from two parents → the trees would vary, and many might not give the same fruit.

  2. 2

    Grafting is vegetative propagation: the new plants come from one parent and are genetically similar enough to have all its characteristics.

  3. 3

    Grafted plants also bear fruit earlier than plants grown from seed.

  4. 4

    So she should use grafting.

  5. Check: grafting is on your textbook's list of vegetative methods used in agriculture (with layering) ✔.

  6. Answer Grafting — the trees get the parent's characteristics (little variation) and fruit earlier; seeds would give variable trees.

    Common trap: "Seeds, because sexual reproduction is better." More variation is good for a species in a changing niche, but a farmer who wants uniform fruit chooses a vegetative method.

Mixed example 2

Same word, different place

Question. The word ovary is used for a plant and for a human. For each, say (a) what it makes, (b) what happens to it, or what it does, after fertilisation, and (c) where fertilisation happens in that organism.

Given: "ovary" in a flower and in a woman. Asked: product; fate or job after fertilisation; site of fertilisation.

Concept used: flower parts (Topic 3); the female reproductive system (Topic 4). Why it applies: the same word names organs with similar jobs but different fates.

  1. 1

    Flower: (a) the ovary contains ovules, each with an egg cell; (b) after fertilisation it grows and ripens into the fruit; (c) fertilisation happens inside the ovule, within the ovary.

  2. 2

    Human: (a) the ovary makes eggs (one a month after puberty) and some hormones; (b) it does not turn into another structure (unlike the plant ovary, which becomes the fruit); (c) fertilisation happens in the oviduct (fallopian tube), not in the ovary.

  3. Check: in the plant, fertilisation is inside the ovary (in an ovule); in a human, it is outside the ovary, in the oviduct ✔.

  4. Answer Plant ovary: holds ovules with eggs, and becomes the fruit; fertilisation in the ovule. Human ovary: eggs and hormones; fertilisation in the oviduct.

    Common trap: "Human fertilisation happens in the ovary, as in plants." In humans it is in the oviduct.

Mixed example 3

Spot the errors in a flower drawing

Question. A student's drawing of the longitudinal section of a flower shows: the label "Pistil" pointing to a stalk with a head at its tip, at the side of the flower; the ovary drawn at the top of the central part, with the stigma at the bottom; "Sepal" pointing to a pink petal; and no lines from the labels to the parts. List the errors and correct each.

Given: a faulty diagram. Asked: errors and corrections.

Concept used: the flower diagram and its labels (Topic 3; Card 3). Why it applies: each error breaks the textbook's figure.

  1. 1

    "Pistil" on a side stalk → a stalk with a head is a stamen (filament + anther). The pistil is the central part.

  2. 2

    Ovary at the top → the ovary is the swollen part at the base; the stigma is at the top, joined by the style.

  3. 3

    "Sepal" on a petal → sepals are the green leaf-like parts below the petals.

  4. 4

    No label lines → every label needs a ruler line touching its part.

  5. Check: after correction the labels match the textbook set: Stigma, Style, Ovary, Pistil, Anther, Filament, Stamen, Petal, Sepal ✔.

  6. Answer Relabel the side stalk "Stamen" (with Anther and Filament); put the ovary at the base and the stigma at the top of the pistil; point "Sepal" to the green parts below the petals; draw ruler lines to every part.

    Common trap: fixing the labels but leaving the ovary at the top — the position is marked too.

Mixed example 4

The same problem, solved twice

Question. Both flowering plants and humans face the same problem: if a zygote received all the DNA of both parents, the DNA would double each generation. (a) How is this solved? (b) Use maize (male gamete: 10 chromosomes) to show the numbers.

Given: the problem of double DNA. Asked: the solution; numbers for maize.

Concept used: gametes have half the chromosomes (Topic 3). Why it applies: the same solution works in plants and animals.

  1. 1

    (a) Gametes are made in reproductive organs by meiosis, a division that gives them half the number of chromosomes and half the DNA of body cells.

  2. 2

    When the two gametes fuse, the full number is re-established in the zygote.

  3. 3

    (b) Male gamete 10 → female gamete also 10 (half the full number) → zygote 10 + 10 = 20.

  4. Check: 20 is the full number, the same as in the parent's body cells — so it does not double ✔.

  5. Answer (a) Gametes carry half the chromosomes; fusion restores the full number. (b) Female gamete 10; zygote 20.

    Common trap: "Female gamete 20, because the egg is bigger." Size comes from food stores, not from more chromosomes.

Mixed example 5

Barrier or not?

Question. For each person, say whether the method avoids pregnancy and whether it helps prevent sexually transmitted infections: (a) a man uses a condom; (b) a woman has a copper-T fitted; (c) a woman takes oral pills; (d) a man has had his vas deferens blocked.

Given: four methods. Asked: pregnancy prevented? STIs prevented?

Concept used: the contraception card (Topic 5). Why it applies: all four prevent pregnancy, but they work differently.

  1. 1

    (a) Condom → barrier; avoids pregnancy and helps prevent STIs (to some extent).

  2. 2

    (b) Copper-T → in the uterus; avoids pregnancy; no STI protection.

  3. 3

    (c) Pills → eggs not released; avoids pregnancy; no STI protection.

  4. 4

    (d) Vas deferens blocked → no sperm transfer; avoids pregnancy; no STI protection.

  5. Check: only (a) is a barrier — the one kind of method that also helps prevent STIs ✔.

  6. Answer All four avoid pregnancy; only (a), the condom, also helps prevent STIs.

    Common trap: thinking a woman with a copper-T is protected from infections — she is not (NCERT intext p.126 Q5).
