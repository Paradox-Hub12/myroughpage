Topic 5 of 5

Reproductive health

About 40 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why sexual maturity is not the same as readiness, and the pressures described in the textbook.
  • Name sexually transmitted infections (bacterial — gonorrhoea, syphilis; viral — warts, HIV-AIDS) and explain how a condom helps prevent them.
  • Describe the methods of contraception — barrier, hormonal pills, loop or copper-T, surgical — how each works, and their side-effects.
  • Explain the reasons for contraception, and why female foeticide is wrong and illegal (sex ratio; prenatal sex determination banned).

Your height reached its adult level, or will soon — but does that mean you are ready to run a house, earn a living or bring up a child? Being able to do something and being ready for it are not the same. This topic looks at reproduction as a matter of health — of the body, of the mind and of society.

Quick reminder (Class 9)

Some diseases are infectious: they spread from person to person, caused by microbes such as bacteria and viruses.

As in Topic 4, this topic stays at your textbook's level. For personal questions about health, talk to a parent, a teacher you trust or a doctor.

  1. Maturity is not the same as readiness

Sexual maturation is gradual, and it happens while the body is still growing. So a body that has begun to mature is not necessarily ready — in body or in mind — for sexual activity, or for having and raising children.

Deciding is made harder by pressures from many sides:

  • from friends, to take part in activities whether or not we really want to;
  • from families, to marry and have children;
  • from government agencies, not to have children.

Making a choice in such a situation can be very difficult.

  1. Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)

Diseases can pass from person to person in many ways. Because the sexual act is a very close contact between bodies, many diseases can be sexually transmitted:

Kind of microbe Sexually transmitted infections (your textbook)
Bacteria gonorrhoea, syphilis
Viruses warts, HIV-AIDS

Using a covering for the penis during sex, called a condom, reduces the chance of passing on many of these infections — though not completely.

  1. Why contraception? — the health of the woman

The sexual act always can lead to pregnancy. Pregnancy puts a heavy load on a woman's body and mind; if she is not ready for it, her health suffers. So many methods have been developed to avoid pregnancy — contraceptive methods.

  1. Methods of contraception

Method How it works Helps prevent STIs? Side-effects
Barrier — condom, or a vaginal covering sperm cannot reach the egg condom: yes — helps, to some extent —
Hormonal — oral pills change hormonal balance: eggs are not released no side-effects (hormone changes)
Loop or copper-T placed in the uterus no side-effects from irritation of the uterus
Surgical — vas deferens or fallopian tube blocked sperm and egg cannot meet no safe long-term; poor surgery may cause infections

Your textbook gives this protection to the condom.

The surgical methods in detail: blocking the vas deferens in the male (vasectomy) means sperms cannot be transferred; blocking the fallopian tube in the female (tubectomy) means the egg cannot reach the uterus. Either way, fertilisation cannot take place.

One rule to remember: of all these methods, only a barrier method (the condom) also helps protect against STIs. Pills, the copper-T and surgery prevent pregnancy but do not stop the spread of infections.

  1. Misuse of surgery — female foeticide and the sex ratio

Surgery can also be used to remove an unwanted pregnancy. This can be misused by people who do not want a particular child — for example, the illegal abortion of a foetus because it is female (female foeticide).

  • For a healthy society, the female-to-male sex ratio must be maintained.
  • Because of reckless female foeticide, the child sex ratio is falling worryingly fast in some parts of our society.
  • This is in spite of the fact that prenatal sex determination — finding out the sex of a baby before birth — is prohibited by law.

  1. Population size

The rates of birth and death decide the size of a population. Many people worry that a growing population makes it harder to raise everyone's standard of living. Your textbook also asks you to think: if inequality in society is the main reason for poor living standards, the size of the population matters relatively little. What do you see as the main reasons for poor living standards around you?

Worked example 1

Sort the methods

Question. Sort these into barrier, hormonal, device in the uterus and surgical, and say for each whether it protects against sexually transmitted infections: oral pills; copper-T; condom; blocking the vas deferens; loop; blocking the fallopian tubes.

Given: six methods. Asked: the category of each; STI protection.

Concept used: the contraception card (this topic). Why it applies: each method belongs to one category with one way of working.

  1. 1

    Condom → barrier — stops sperm reaching the egg → protects against STIs (helps, to some extent).

  2. 2

    Oral pills → hormonal — eggs not released → no STI protection.

  3. 3

    Copper-T and loop → devices placed in the uterus → no STI protection.

  4. 4

    Blocking the vas deferens and blocking the fallopian tubes → surgical → no STI protection.

  5. Check: only one of the six — the condom — is a covering (a barrier) during the sexual act, so only it can reduce the passing of infection ✔.

  6. Answer Barrier: condom (protects). Hormonal: pills (no). Device in uterus: copper-T, loop (no). Surgical: vas deferens and fallopian tube blocked (no).

    Common trap: "Every contraceptive protects against STIs." Only the barrier method does.

Worked example 2

What do the two surgical methods have in common?

Question. In one surgical method the vas deferens is blocked; in another the fallopian tubes are blocked. What does each block, and what common event does both prevent?

Given: two surgical methods. Asked: what each blocks; the common event prevented.

Concept used: surgical contraception (this topic); the path of gametes (Topic 4). Why it applies: both block a tube that a gamete must travel through.

  1. 1

    Vas deferens blocked → sperms cannot be transferred from the testes.

  2. 2

    Fallopian tubes blocked → the egg cannot travel along the tube (and sperms cannot reach it).

  3. 3

    Common event prevented → the meeting and fusion of the male and female gametes — fertilisation.

  4. 4

    Gametes are still made in the testes and ovaries; only their path is blocked.

  5. Check: fertilisation needs sperm and egg to meet in the oviduct; each block stops that meeting ✔.

  6. Answer Vas deferens blocked → no sperm transfer; fallopian tube blocked → egg cannot reach the uterus. Both prevent the meeting of sperm and egg (fertilisation).

    Common trap: "Surgery stops gametes being made." The gametes are still made; their route is blocked.

Worked example 3

Why the sex ratio matters

Question. Using your textbook's points, explain why prenatal sex determination is banned, and why maintaining the sex ratio matters.

Given: the ban on prenatal sex determination. Asked: the reason; why the sex ratio matters.

Concept used: misuse of surgery; the sex ratio (this topic). Why it applies: knowing the sex before birth has been misused.

  1. 1

    Misuse → surgery for removing pregnancies has been misused for illegal sex-selective abortion of female foetuses.

  2. 2

    Effect → because of reckless female foeticide, the child sex ratio is declining at an alarming rate in some sections of society.

  3. 3

    Why it matters → for a healthy society, the female-to-male sex ratio must be maintained.

  4. 4

    The law → to stop this, prenatal sex determination is prohibited by law.

  5. Check: each step follows from the one before: misuse → falling ratio → need to protect the ratio → the ban ✔.

  6. Answer Prenatal sex determination is banned because it has been misused for female foeticide, which is lowering the child sex ratio; a healthy society needs the female-to-male ratio to be maintained.

    Common trap: saying "it is banned because the test is unsafe". The reason is its misuse for female foeticide.

Common mistakes!

  • "Every contraceptive protects against STIs."

    Pills, copper-T and surgery do not stop infections passing.

    Only barrier methods (condom) help prevent STIs.

  • "A woman using a copper-T is protected from STIs."

    The copper-T works inside the uterus; it is not a barrier.

    Copper-T: prevents pregnancy, no STI protection; it may irritate the uterus.

  • "Pills kill sperms."

    Pills change hormonal balance.

    Pills → eggs not released → no fertilisation.

  • "Surgical methods stop sperms or eggs being made."

    They block a tube.

    Vas deferens / fallopian tube blocked → gametes cannot meet.

  • "Gonorrhoea and HIV-AIDS are both viral."

    Gonorrhoea is bacterial.

    Bacterial: gonorrhoea, syphilis. Viral: warts, HIV-AIDS.

  • "Prenatal sex determination is allowed if parents ask."

    It is prohibited by law.

    Banned because of its misuse for female foeticide; the sex ratio must be maintained.

Key points

  • Sexual maturity is gradual, while the body is still growing; it does not mean the body or mind is ready. Pressures come from friends, families and government agencies.
  • STIs: bacterial — gonorrhoea, syphilis; viral — warts, HIV-AIDS. A condom helps prevent many of them, to some extent.
  • Pregnancy makes major demands on the woman's body and mind; if she is not ready, her health suffers — hence contraception.
  • Methods: barrier (condom; sperm cannot reach egg), hormonal pills (eggs not released; side-effects), loop or copper-T in the uterus (side-effects from irritation), surgical (vas deferens or fallopian tube blocked; safe long-term, but surgery can cause infections if not done properly).
  • Only barrier methods also help protect against STIs.
  • Female foeticide is illegal; the sex ratio must be maintained for a healthy society; prenatal sex determination is prohibited by law.
