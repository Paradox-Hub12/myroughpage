Chapter 7 · Unit II

How do Organisms Reproduce?

5 topicsAbout 7–8 hoursBoard exam
Why this chapter matters

A field of identical banana plants grows without a single seed. A slice of bread turns black with mould in three days. A pea pod holds six seeds, each with a tiny plant inside. And every one of us began as a single cell. Living things do not need to reproduce to stay alive — so why do they spend so much energy on it, and how do they do it?

This chapter follows reproduction from the DNA copy inside a cell to the many methods living things use. You will see why copying is never perfect and why that variation helps a species survive; how single parents make new individuals by fission, budding, fragmentation, regeneration, vegetative propagation and spores; how a flower makes seeds and fruit after pollination and fertilisation; how the human reproductive systems work; and why reproductive health — readiness, safe practices, contraception and a balanced sex ratio — matters. It belongs to Unit II, World of Living. In the two CBSE sample papers it supplies three MCQs (a flower bud with its anthers removed, the surgical methods of contraception, and chromosome numbers in gametes) and a 2-mark question on the uterus after implantation (2026-27), and a 5-mark question with a choice between banana plants and watermelon flowers (2025-26).

What you'll learn

  • Explain why DNA copying is the basic event of reproduction, why copies vary, and how variation and consistency each help a species.
  • Describe fission, budding, fragmentation, regeneration, vegetative propagation and spore formation, with the textbook's organisms; choose the right method for an organism.
  • Explain why sexual reproduction gives more variation; name and label the parts of a flower; tell pollination from fertilisation; trace the path from pollen to seed and fruit; name the parts of a seed.
  • Describe the changes at puberty; describe and label the male and female reproductive systems; explain fertilisation, implantation, the placenta and menstruation.
  • Explain why maturity is not readiness; name sexually transmitted infections; describe the methods of contraception and which one also protects against infections; explain why the sex ratio must be maintained.
  • Compare asexual and sexual reproduction; use the method chooser, the flower and human sequence cards, the "draw it in the exam" checklist and the label-the-diagram trainer.

Most of the marks in this chapter come from four skills, and every topic practises them:

  1. Explain "why" — why DNA copying matters, why variation helps the species, why farmers use vegetative propagation, why menstruation happens, why contraception is used.
  2. Match method → organism → what happens — using the method chooser card.
  3. Trace a sequence in order — pollination → pollen tube → fertilisation → zygote → embryo → seed and fruit; sperm path; fertilisation → implantation → placenta.
  4. Draw and label — the flower, the seed, and the male and female systems, each with a "Draw it in the exam" checklist and a plain line version to copy.
