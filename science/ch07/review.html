Week 1

Set 2 — Week 1

5 questions, about 8 minutes

Focus: applying the ideas — slightly harder.

W1.1 · 1 of 5●● Standard

Jasmine plants are grown from cuttings, not seeds. Give two reasons from your textbook.

Answer: Jasmine has lost the capacity to produce seeds; plants from vegetative propagation flower earlier and have all the characteristics of the parent (any two). (Topic 2)

Did you get it?

W1.2 · 2 of 5●● Standard

A Hydra cut into three pieces becomes three Hydra. An uncut Hydra grows a small outgrowth that later drops off and lives on its own. Name the two processes.

Answer: Cut pieces growing into whole animals — regeneration; the outgrowth that detaches — budding. (Topic 2)

Did you get it?

W1.3 · 3 of 5●● Standard

Pollen from a papaya flower on one tree lands on the stigma of a papaya flower on another tree. Name the process, and the process that must follow before a seed forms.

Answer: Cross-pollination; then fertilisation — a pollen tube grows through the style and the male germ-cell fuses with the egg in the ovule. (Topic 3)

Did you get it?

W1.4 · 4 of 5●● Standard

Why does the lining of the uterus become thick every month?

Answer: One egg is released every month, so the uterus prepares every month to receive and nourish an embryo: its lining becomes thick, spongy and rich in blood. (Topic 4)

Did you get it?

W1.5 · 5 of 5●● Standard

How does blocking the fallopian tubes prevent pregnancy?

Answer: The egg cannot travel along the tube to meet sperms (or reach the uterus), so fertilisation cannot take place. (Topic 5)

Month 1

Set 3 — Month 1

5 questions, about 12 minutes

Focus: linking topics — the kind of thinking the board exam rewards.

M1.1 · 1 of 5●●● Advanced

Both asexual and sexual reproduction produce variation. Explain where the variation comes from in each, and why sexual reproduction gives more.

Answer: In asexual reproduction, variation comes only from small inaccuracies in DNA copying, which build up slowly. In sexual reproduction, DNA from two individuals — each carrying different variations — is combined, so every offspring is a new combination; this gives more variation, faster. (Topics 1 and 3)

Did you get it?

M1.2 · 2 of 5●●● Advanced

Compare where fertilisation takes place, and where the young embryo develops, in a flowering plant and in a human.

Answer: Plant: fertilisation in the ovule (inside the ovary); the embryo develops within the ovule, which becomes the seed. Human: fertilisation in the oviduct (fallopian tube); the embryo implants and develops in the uterus. (Topics 3 and 4)

Did you get it?

M1.3 · 3 of 5●● Standard

Without looking, list the labels on (a) your textbook's longitudinal section of a flower and (b) its diagram of the human female reproductive system. Then check against the wrap-up's "Draw it in the exam" card.

Answer: (a) Stigma, Style, Ovary, Pistil, Anther, Filament, Stamen, Petal, Sepal. (b) Oviduct or Fallopian tube, Ovary, Uterus, Cervix, Vagina. (Topics 3 and 4)

Did you get it?

M1.4 · 4 of 5●● Standard

A seed contains cotyledons; a human embryo has a placenta. What job do both do for the young embryo?

Answer: Both supply food to the developing embryo: the cotyledons are the seed's food store; the placenta passes glucose and oxygen from the mother's blood to the embryo (and removes its wastes). (Topics 3 and 4)

Did you get it?

M1.5 · 5 of 5●●● Advanced

"All contraceptive methods are the same — they all just prevent pregnancy." Use your textbook to show two ways in which this is wrong.

Answer: The methods work differently: a barrier stops sperm reaching the egg, pills stop eggs being released, a copper-T works inside the uterus, surgery blocks the vas deferens or fallopian tubes. And they differ in protection: only the barrier method also helps prevent sexually transmitted infections. (They also differ in side-effects: pills — hormonal side-effects; copper-T — irritation of the uterus; surgery — risk of infection if not done properly.) (Topic 5)

Did you get it?
