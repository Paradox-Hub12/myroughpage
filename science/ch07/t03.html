Topic 3 of 5

Sexual reproduction in flowering plants

About 65 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why sexual reproduction gives more variation, and why gametes have half the number of chromosomes.
  • Name the parts of a flower, tell unisexual from bisexual flowers, and draw and label the longitudinal section of a flower.
  • Describe self- and cross-pollination, and explain how pollination differs from fertilisation.
  • Describe fertilisation (pollen tube) and the changes that follow — seed, fruit, falling of parts — and germination and the parts of a seed's embryo.

Cut open a pea pod and count the peas. Each pea is a seed with a tiny plant inside it, and every one of them began when a speck of yellow powder landed on a flower. Bees, butterflies and the wind carry that powder from flower to flower. But landing on the flower is only the beginning — something much smaller has to happen deep inside before a single seed can form.

Quick reminder (Class 7)

A flower has four kinds of parts: sepals (green, outermost), petals (often coloured), stamens and the pistil (in the centre).

  1. Why the sexual mode? More variation

In Topic 1 you saw that DNA copying makes small variations, but only slowly — copying is precise enough that variation builds up gradually. Copying less accurately would not help: many copies would then fail to work and the cells would die.

So how can variation be made faster? Two individuals of a species carry different sets of variations, built up over many generations — and since both are alive, their variations are known to be harmless. Sexual reproduction combines the DNA of two different individuals, so every offspring gets a new combination of variations. That is why sexual reproduction gives more variation than asexual reproduction.

  1. The problem of double DNA — and meiosis

If each new individual simply received all the DNA of both parents, every generation would have twice the DNA of the one before. This would upset the control of the cell by its DNA.

The solution: special cells in reproductive organs (the germ-cells, or gametes) carry only half as many chromosomes (and half as much DNA) as the other cells of the body. They are made by a special cell division called meiosis. When two gametes fuse, the full number is re-established.

Cell Chromosome number (example: pea)
Male gamete half — 7
Female gamete half — 7
Zygote and body cells full — 7 + 7 = 14

Two kinds of gametes. The fertilised cell (zygote) needs stored energy to grow. So in complex organisms one gamete is large with food stores — the female gamete — and the other is small and likely to be motile (able to move) — the male gamete.

  1. The flower — where the gametes are made

The reproductive parts of flowering plants are in the flower.

  • Stamen — the male reproductive part. It has a stalk (filament) and a head (anther), which makes pollen grains — the yellowish powder that sticks to your fingers.
  • Pistil — the female reproductive part, in the centre of the flower. It has three parts: the ovary (the swollen base), the style (the long neck) and the stigma (the tip, often sticky). Inside the ovary are ovules; each ovule holds one egg cell (the female gamete).
  • Sepals and petals are not reproductive; sepals protect the bud, and colourful petals attract insects that carry pollen.
Flower type Has Examples
Unisexual either stamens or pistil papaya, watermelon
Bisexual both stamens and pistil Hibiscus, mustard

  1. Pollination — getting pollen to the stigma

The male germ-cell is made inside the pollen grain, but the egg is in the ovule. So pollen must first be carried from the anther (stamen) to the stigma — pollination.

  • Self-pollination — pollen reaches the stigma of the same flower.
  • Cross-pollination — pollen is carried from one flower to another, by wind, water or animals (bees, butterflies, birds).

A unisexual flower such as a watermelon flower cannot pollinate itself: its pollen must come from another flower.

  1. Fertilisation — the gametes fuse inside the ovule

Once pollen has landed on the stigma of the right kind of flower:

  1. The pollen grain sends out a thin pollen tube.
  2. The tube grows down through the style into the ovary, taking the male germ-cell with it.
  3. Inside the ovule, the male germ-cell fuses with the female germ-cell (egg) — this fusion is fertilisation.
  4. Fertilisation gives a zygote, which can grow into a new plant.

Pollination vs fertilisation — the matched card

Point Pollination Fertilisation
What happens transfer of pollen from the anther to the stigma fusion of the male and female germ-cells (gametes)
Where on the stigma (outside the ovary) inside the ovule, in the ovary
What moves the pollen grain, carried by wind, water or animals the male germ-cell, down the pollen tube
What it gives pollen on the stigma — no zygote yet a zygote
Order first second — only after pollination

Memory aid (just a memory aid): pollination is the journey; fertilisation is the meeting. The journey always comes first.

  1. After fertilisation — seed, fruit and the parts that fall

Flower part After fertilisation
Zygote divides several times → embryo, inside the ovule
Ovule develops a tough coat → seed
Ovary grows rapidly and ripens → fruit
Petals, sepals, stamens, style and stigma may shrivel and fall off

Sometimes a flower part stays on the fruit — look at the green cap at the stalk end of a brinjal or tomato, or the dry crown at the far end of a guava.

The flower sequence card — learn it in order:

pollination → pollen tube grows through the style → fertilisation in the ovule → zygote → embryo → ovule becomes seed, ovary becomes fruit; then the seed germinates → seedling

  1. The seed and germination

A seed contains the future plant, or embryo. Under suitable conditions (water, air, warmth) the embryo grows into a seedling — germination. The parts of a dicot seed's embryo (NCERT Fig. 7.9):

  • Plumule — the future shoot;
  • Radicle — the future root;
  • Cotyledon — the food store (a dicot such as gram, pea or bean has two cotyledons).

Virtual lab — CBSE Practical 14: parts of the embryo of a dicot seed (NCERT Activity 7.7)

Designed from NCERT Activity 7.7 and usual school practice; your teacher will confirm the steps against the CBSE practical list and the lab manual.

What you do:

  1. Soak a few seeds of Bengal gram (chana) — or pea, or red kidney bean (rajma) — in water overnight.
  2. Pour off the extra water, wrap the seeds in a damp cloth and wait one more day. Do not let them dry out.
  3. Gently remove the seed coat (the thin outer skin) with your fingers.
  4. Split the seed carefully into its two halves along the line where they meet.
  5. Look at the halves with a hand lens; compare what you see with the textbook's figure of germination and name the parts.

What you observe:

Part What it looks like Its job
Seed coat thin, wrinkled outer skin, easy to peel after soaking protects the seed
Cotyledons two large, thick, fleshy halves food store for the embryo
Plumule a tiny shoot with small folded leaves, between the cotyledons future shoot
Radicle a small pointed tip, sticking out at one end future root

What it shows:

  1. A dicot seed has two cotyledons that store food.
  2. The seed contains a tiny embryo — plumule (future shoot) and radicle (future root) — the "future plant" that grows during germination.
  3. Soaking softens the seed coat and lets the seed start germinating, which makes the parts larger and easier to see.
Safety

do not use a sharp blade — the soaked seed splits with your fingers. Wash your hands afterwards; do not eat seeds treated for sowing.

Worked example 1

The bagged bud

Question. In a Hibiscus bud, a gardener carefully removes all the anthers before they open, and ties a paper bag over the bud. (a) Will the flower form seeds? (b) What would happen if, after a day, she dusted pollen from another Hibiscus flower on its stigma and bagged it again? Explain.

Given: a bisexual flower with its anthers removed, then bagged. Asked: seeds or not, in two cases.

Concept used: pollination must happen before fertilisation (this topic). Why it applies: removing anthers removes the flower's own pollen; the bag keeps other pollen out.

  1. 1

    (a) With the anthers gone, the flower has no pollen of its own → no self-pollination.

  2. 2

    The bag stops wind and insects bringing pollen from other flowers → no cross-pollination.

  3. 3

    No pollen on the stigma → no pollen tube → no fertilisation → no zygote → no seed (and no fruit).

  4. 4

    (b) Dusting pollen from another flower is cross-pollination done by hand. A pollen tube can now grow to the ovule → fertilisation → seeds and fruit can form.

  5. Check: the pistil was never removed, so once pollen reaches the stigma the flower can be fertilised ✔.

  6. Answer (a) No — no pollination, so no fertilisation. (b) Yes — hand cross-pollination allows fertilisation, so seeds and fruit can form.

    Common trap: "The flower will still make seeds because it has an ovary." The ovules can become seeds only after fertilisation, which needs pollen.

Worked example 2

Papaya and Hibiscus

Question. A papaya flower has only a pistil; a Hibiscus flower has both stamens and a pistil. Name the type of each flower. Which of them must be cross-pollinated? Why?

Given: two flowers. Asked: types; which must be cross-pollinated.

Concept used: unisexual and bisexual flowers; self- and cross-pollination (this topic). Why it applies: self-pollination needs pollen and stigma in the same flower.

  1. 1

    Papaya flower → only a pistil → unisexual.

  2. 2

    Hibiscus → stamens and pistil → bisexual.

  3. 3

    A papaya flower with only a pistil has no pollen of its own, so pollen must come from another flower → it must be cross-pollinated.

  4. 4

    A bisexual flower has both, so it can be self-pollinated (it may also be cross-pollinated).

  5. Check: your textbook's examples — papaya and watermelon unisexual; Hibiscus and mustard bisexual ✔.

  6. Answer Papaya — unisexual; Hibiscus — bisexual. The papaya flower must be cross-pollinated, because it has no stamens of its own.

    Common trap: "Unisexual flowers cannot make fruit." A female (pistil-only) flower can — once pollen arrives from another flower.

Worked example 3

The journey of one pollen grain

Question. Trace the journey of one pollen grain from the anther of a mustard flower until a seed is formed. Write each event in order and say where it happens.

Given: one pollen grain. Asked: the events in order, with places.

Concept used: the flower sequence card (this topic). Why it applies: each event must happen before the next.

  1. 1

    Pollination → the pollen grain is carried from the anther to the stigma (of the same or another flower).

  2. 2

    Pollen tube → a tube grows out of the pollen grain, down through the style to the ovary.

  3. 3

    Fertilisation → in the ovule, the male germ-cell fuses with the egg cell → zygote.

  4. 4

    Embryo → the zygote divides several times → embryo, within the ovule.

  5. 5

    Seed → the ovule develops a tough coat and becomes a seed (the ovary becomes the fruit).

  6. Check: the events follow the flower sequence card, and fertilisation comes only after pollination ✔.

  7. Answer anther → (pollination) → stigma → pollen tube through the style → ovary → fertilisation in the ovule → zygote → embryo → ovule becomes a seed.

    Common trap: writing "fertilisation" for the pollen landing on the stigma. Landing is pollination; fertilisation is the fusion in the ovule.

Worked example 4

Which part of the pea pod came from where?

Question. A pea pod contains six peas. (a) Which part of the flower became the pod? (b) Which became the peas? (c) What does each pea contain that can grow into a new plant? (d) How many ovules must have been fertilised?

Given: a pod with six peas. Asked: origins of pod and peas; contents of a pea; number of fertilised ovules.

Concept used: changes after fertilisation (this topic). Why it applies: the fruit and seeds come from definite flower parts.

  1. 1

    (a) The pod is the fruit → it came from the ovary.

  2. 2

    (b) Each pea is a seed → it came from an ovule.

  3. 3

    (c) Each pea contains an embryo (plumule, radicle) and cotyledons (food store).

  4. 4

    (d) One seed forms from each fertilised ovule → six ovules were fertilised.

  5. Check: the ovary contains the ovules, just as the pod contains the peas ✔.

  6. Answer (a) ovary (b) ovules (c) an embryo (future plant) with cotyledons (d) six

    Common trap: "The ovary becomes the seed." It is the ovule → seed; the ovary → fruit.

Common mistakes!

  • "Pollination and fertilisation are the same."

    One is a transfer, the other a fusion.

    Pollination: pollen anther → stigma. Fertilisation: gametes fuse in the ovule.

  • "Fertilisation happens on the stigma."

    Gametes fuse inside the ovule.

    Pollen lands on stigma → tube through style → fusion in the ovule.

  • "Fertilisation, then pollination."

    Pollen must reach the stigma first.

    Pollination → pollen tube → fertilisation → zygote → embryo → seed.

  • "The ovary becomes the seed; the ovule becomes the fruit."

    The two are reversed.

    Ovule → seed; ovary → fruit.

  • "Gametes have the full number of chromosomes."

    Then the number would double each generation.

    Gametes have half; the zygote restores the full number.

  • Flower diagram: stamens labelled "Pistil", or the ovary drawn at the top

    The pistil is in the centre, ovary at the base.

    Pistil = stigma (top) + style + ovary (base); stamen = anther + filament.

  • Labels written with no leader lines, or one line for "Sepal" pointing to a petal

    Marks need a clear line to the part.

    Ruler line from each label to its part; sepals are the green parts below the petals.

Key points

  • Sexual reproduction combines DNA from two individuals → new combinations → more variation.
  • Gametes have half the chromosomes (made by meiosis); fusion re-establishes the full number (pea: 7 + 7 = 14). The female gamete is large with food stores; the male is small and motile.
  • Flower: stamen (anther + filament; pollen) — male; pistil (stigma, style, ovary with ovules) — female. Unisexual: papaya, watermelon; bisexual: Hibiscus, mustard.
  • Pollination = pollen from anther to stigma (self or cross; wind, water, animals). Fertilisation = fusion of gametes in the ovule, after a pollen tube grows through the style.
  • After fertilisation: zygote → embryo; ovule → seed; ovary → fruit; petals, sepals, stamens, style and stigma may fall off.
  • Germination: the embryo grows into a seedling — plumule (future shoot), radicle (future root), cotyledon (food store).
