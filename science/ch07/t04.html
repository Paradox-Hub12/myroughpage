Reproduction in human beings

About 60 minutes · 5 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe the changes at puberty in boys and girls.
  • Describe the male reproductive system and the function of each part (testes, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, prostate gland, urethra).
  • Describe the female reproductive system (ovaries, oviducts, uterus, vagina), fertilisation, implantation and the placenta.
  • Explain what happens when the egg is not fertilised — menstruation.
  • Draw and label the human male and female reproductive systems.

Think back to your Class 5 photo. You have grown taller since then, and you lost your milk teeth long ago. But in the early teenage years a different kind of change begins — not just more body, but a different body. Why does this happen at this age, and what is it preparing the body for?

Quick reminder (Chapter 6)

The testes secrete testosterone and the ovaries secrete oestrogen; these hormones bring about the changes at puberty.

This topic is taught at the level of your textbook, in the same matter-of-fact way as any other body system. If you have personal questions about your own body or health, talk to a parent, a teacher you trust or a doctor.

Humans reproduce sexually: a male gamete (sperm) and a female gamete (egg) from two individuals fuse to form a zygote.

  1. Growth and puberty

From early childhood, the body grows — it becomes taller and larger. In the early teenage years a new set of changes begins: the appearance of the body changes, its proportions change and new features appear. These changes are part of sexual maturation.

Why at this age? Making germ-cells is a specialised function, done by special tissues. While the body is still growing fast, its resources go mainly into growth. When overall growth starts to slow down, the body turns to maturing its reproductive tissues. This stage of the teenage years is puberty.

Changes common to boys and girls Changes in girls Changes in boys
thick hair grows in new places — the armpits and the genital area between the thighs — and may become darker breasts begin to grow larger, and the skin at the tips of the breasts (nipples) becomes darker thick hair begins to grow on the face
thinner hair may appear on the legs, arms and face girls begin to menstruate the voice begins to crack
the skin often becomes oily, and pimples may appear the penis occasionally becomes enlarged and erect
we become aware of our own bodies and others' in new ways

These changes happen slowly, over months and years. They do not all happen at the same time, nor at an exact age — early in some people, later in others — and they differ from person to person, just as noses and fingers differ. Changes such as new hair growth are signs that the body is maturing sexually.

  1. The male reproductive system

The male system has parts that make the germ-cells (sperms) and parts that deliver them.

Part What it does
Testes (in the scrotum) make sperms; secrete testosterone
Scrotum holds the testes outside the abdominal cavity, because sperm formation needs a lower temperature than normal body temperature
Vas deferens carries sperms from the testis; it joins the tube that leads out of the urinary bladder
Seminal vesicles and prostate gland pour secretions into the passage, so the sperms float in a fluid that makes their transport easier and gives them nutrition
Urethra a common passage for both sperms and urine
Penis the organ that transfers the sperms

Testosterone does two jobs: it regulates the formation of sperms, and it brings about the changes in appearance in boys at puberty.

Sperms are tiny cells made mainly of genetic material, with a long tail that helps them move towards the female germ-cell.

Path of sperms: testis → vas deferens → urethra → out through the penis (the seminal vesicles and prostate gland add fluid on the way).

  1. The female reproductive system

Part What it does
Ovaries (two) make the eggs (female germ-cells); also produce some hormones
Oviduct (fallopian tube) a thin tube that carries the egg from the ovary towards the uterus; site of fertilisation
Uterus (womb) an elastic, bag-like organ into which the two oviducts open; the embryo implants and grows here
Cervix the opening of the uterus into the vagina
Vagina the passage through which sperms enter

A baby girl is born with thousands of immature eggs in her ovaries. After puberty they begin to mature, and each month one of the two ovaries releases one egg.

  1. Fertilisation, implantation and the placenta

  1. During sexual intercourse, sperms enter the vagina and move upwards.
  2. In the oviduct (fallopian tube) they may meet the egg → fertilisation → zygote.
  3. The zygote divides and forms a ball of cells, the embryo.
  4. The embryo is implanted in the lining of the uterus, where it grows and develops organs to become a foetus.
  5. The uterus gets ready for this every month: its lining becomes thick and gets a rich supply of blood, ready to feed an embryo.
  6. The embryo is fed from the mother's blood by way of a special tissue, the placenta.
  7. After about nine months of development, the baby is pushed out by regular, rhythmic contractions of the uterus muscles.

The placenta is a disc embedded in the wall of the uterus.

  • On the embryo's side it has finger-like villi.
  • The villi dip into blood spaces on the mother's side.
  • So there is a large surface area across which glucose and oxygen move from the mother to the embryo.
  • The embryo's wastes cross the other way, into the mother's blood, to be removed.

The placenta does not pour the mother's blood into the embryo: substances pass across it between the villi and the blood spaces.

  1. When the egg is not fertilised — menstruation

  • An egg that is not fertilised lives for about one day.
  • But the uterus has already prepared, as it does every month: its lining has become thick and spongy, ready to nourish an embryo.
  • This lining is not needed now. So it gradually breaks down, and it leaves the body through the vagina as blood and mucus.
  • This happens roughly every month and is called menstruation. It usually lasts about two to eight days.

Each month: the uterine lining thickens and an egg is released → (no fertilisation) the egg lives about a day → the lining breaks down → menstruation → the cycle begins again.

Worked example 1

Put the sperm's path in order

Question. Arrange these in the order in which sperms pass through them, starting from where they are made: urethra, testis, vas deferens. Name the two glands that add fluid on the way, and say what the fluid does.

Given: three parts in mixed order. Asked: the correct order; the glands; the fluid's job.

Concept used: the male reproductive system (this topic). Why it applies: sperms are made in one place and delivered through tubes.

  1. 1

    Sperms are made in the testis.

  2. 2

    They are delivered through the vas deferens.

  3. 3

    The vas deferens unites with the tube from the bladder; the urethra is the common passage out.

  4. 4

    Glands: the seminal vesicles and the prostate gland add their secretions along the vas deferens.

  5. 5

    Fluid: it makes the transport of sperms easier and gives them nutrition.

  6. Check: the order matches the male-system diagram — testis at the bottom, vas deferens rising to join the urethra ✔.

  7. Answer Testis → vas deferens → urethra. Glands: seminal vesicles and prostate gland; their fluid eases transport and nourishes the sperms.

    Common trap: putting the ureter in the path. The ureter carries urine from the kidney to the bladder; sperms never pass through it.

Worked example 2

Where does each event happen?

Question. Name the organ where each happens: (a) eggs are made; (b) fertilisation; (c) implantation of the embryo; (d) the embryo develops into a foetus; (e) sperms enter.

Given: five events. Asked: the organ for each.

Concept used: the female reproductive system and the human sequence card (this topic). Why it applies: each event has one site.

  1. 1

    (a) Eggs are made in the ovary.

  2. 2

    (b) Sperms meet the egg in the oviduct (fallopian tube) → fertilisation.

  3. 3

    (c) The embryo implants in the lining of the uterus.

  4. 4

    (d) It grows into a foetus in the uterus.

  5. 5

    (e) Sperms enter through the vagina.

  6. Check: on the human sequence card the oviduct comes first and the uterus second — fertilisation before implantation ✔.

  7. Answer (a) ovary (b) oviduct / fallopian tube (c) uterus (lining) (d) uterus (e) vagina

    Common trap: (b) "uterus". Fertilisation happens in the oviduct; the embryo only implants in the uterus afterwards.

Worked example 3

What the uterine lining does each month

Question. Explain why the lining of the uterus becomes thick every month, and what happens to it (a) if the egg is fertilised and (b) if it is not.

Given: the monthly thickening. Asked: the reason; the two outcomes.

Concept used: implantation, the placenta and menstruation (this topic). Why it applies: the lining is prepared for an embryo every month because an egg is released every month.

  1. 1

    Reason → one egg is released every month, so the uterus prepares every month to receive and nourish an embryo: its lining becomes thick, spongy and richly supplied with blood.

  2. 2

    (a) Fertilised → the embryo implants in this lining; the placenta forms and the embryo is nourished there for about nine months.

  3. 3

    (b) Not fertilised → the egg lives about one day; the thick lining is not needed, so it breaks down and leaves through the vagina as blood and mucus — menstruation — lasting about two to eight days.

  4. Check: both outcomes start from the same thickened lining — only fertilisation decides which follows ✔.

  5. Answer The lining thickens every month to be ready to nourish an embryo. (a) If fertilised, the embryo implants and the placenta forms. (b) If not, the lining breaks down and is shed as menstruation.

    Common trap: "Menstruation happens because the egg is fertilised." It happens because the egg is not fertilised.

Common mistakes!

  • "Fertilisation happens in the uterus."

    Sperms meet the egg before it reaches the uterus.

    Fertilisation — oviduct; implantation and growth — uterus.

  • "The seminal vesicles (or prostate) make sperms."

    They only add fluid.

    Testes make sperms; seminal vesicles and prostate add fluid for transport and nutrition.

  • "Testes are outside the body to keep sperms warm."

    Sperm formation needs a lower temperature.

    Scrotum outside the abdomen → lower temperature for sperm formation.

  • "Sperms travel through the ureter."

    The ureter carries urine from kidney to bladder.

    Testis → vas deferens → urethra.

  • "The placenta passes the mother's blood into the baby."

    Substances pass across it.

    Villi + blood spaces → glucose and oxygen to the embryo; wastes to the mother's blood.

  • "Menstruation means the egg was fertilised."

    It happens when it is not.

    No fertilisation → thick lining breaks down → menstruation (2–8 days).

  • Diagram: prostate and seminal vesicle swapped, or oviduct drawn ending in nothing

    Labels and positions are marked.

    Seminal vesicle behind the bladder; prostate at its base, round the urethra. Each oviduct runs from the uterus towards an ovary; its open end lies close to the ovary.

Key points

  • Puberty: as general growth slows, reproductive tissues mature. Common changes: new thick hair (armpits, genital area), oily skin, pimples. Girls: breasts grow, menstruation begins. Boys: facial hair, voice cracks.
  • Male: testes (in the scrotum — lower temperature) make sperms and testosterone → vas deferens → urethra (common passage for sperms and urine); seminal vesicles and prostate add fluid for transport and nutrition.
  • Female: ovaries (eggs, hormones; one egg a month) → oviduct/fallopian tube → uterus → cervix → vagina.
  • Fertilisation in the oviduct → zygote → embryo → implantation in the uterine lining → placenta (villi + blood spaces; glucose and oxygen in, wastes out) → about nine months → birth by uterine contractions.
  • No fertilisation → egg lives about one day → thick, spongy lining breaks down → menstruation (about 2–8 days), roughly every month.
