Topic 1 of 5

Why organisms reproduce — DNA copying and variation

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain that reproduction makes copies of DNA together with extra cell apparatus, and why DNA copying is essential.
  • Explain why DNA copying is never perfect, and why variation helps a species survive but not necessarily an individual.
  • Explain how reproduction keeps the populations of a species stable in their niche.

A mango tree does not need to make mangoes to stay alive. Making and using food, respiration and getting rid of wastes keep it alive — but making flowers, fruits and seeds only uses up its food and energy. So why does every living thing spend so much effort on reproducing? And why do children look like their parents, but never exactly like them?

Quick reminder (Class 9)

The nucleus of a cell holds chromosomes. Chromosomes are made of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), which holds the instructions children inherit from their parents. A cell uses this information to make proteins. A cell makes new cells by dividing.

  1. Reproduction is not needed for an individual to stay alive

Nutrition, respiration and excretion are life processes — without them an organism dies. Reproduction is different: an organism can live its whole life without reproducing, and reproducing actually costs it a lot of energy.

So why does it happen? Look at it from the side of the kind of organism (the species). We notice mango trees, sparrows or ants because there are large numbers of them. If a kind of organism did not reproduce, it would disappear when its last member died. Reproduction gives rise to young ones that closely resemble their parents, so the species carries on.

  1. Reproduction means copying the DNA "blueprint"

Members of a species look alike because their body designs are alike. A body design is built from a set of instructions — like the blueprint (plan) of a house. In living things this blueprint is the DNA in the nucleus:

  • DNA holds the instructions the cell uses to build proteins.
  • If the DNA information changes, different proteins are made.
  • Different proteins finally lead to a changed body design.

So, at its most basic level, reproduction involves making a copy of the DNA.

  1. DNA copy + extra cell apparatus → two cells

A cell uses chemical reactions to build a copy of its DNA. Now it has two copies. Could it simply keep one copy and push the other out? No — a DNA copy on its own, pushed out of the cell, would have no organised cell structure to carry out life processes.

So the cell does two things:

  1. it copies its DNA, and
  2. it makes additional cellular apparatus (the rest of the cell's working parts).

Then the two copies move apart, and each one takes a share of the cell's machinery with it. In effect, one cell divides to give two cells.

  1. Copies are never perfect — variation

Think of a photocopy of a photocopy. Each new copy is very like the one before it, but small smudges creep in, and the smudges of earlier copies are passed on too.

DNA copying is similar. The chemical reactions that copy DNA are never one hundred per cent reliable, so each copy of the DNA can have small changes. The new DNA copies are similar, but may not be identical to the original.

  • A few changes are drastic: the changed DNA cannot run the cell it ends up in, and that new cell dies.
  • Many other changes are not so drastic — those cells survive. So the cells that live on are alike, but each a little different.

These small differences are called variations. Because every round of copying can add variation, variation is the raw material of evolution (you will meet this in Chapter 8).

  1. Why variation matters — the species, not the individual

Every species lives in its own niche — its particular place and way of life in an ecosystem (a fish in a pond, a cactus in a desert).

  • Consistent DNA copying keeps the body design that lets the organism use its niche. So reproduction keeps the population of a species stable: the new individuals fit the niche just as their parents did, and they replace those that die.
  • But niches can change for reasons the organisms cannot control: temperatures rise or fall, water levels change, a meteorite may strike.

If every member of a population were exactly the same and the niche changed drastically, the whole population could be wiped out. If a few individuals have variations that suit the new conditions, they survive and reproduce.

Example: bacteria living in temperate (moderately cool) waters. If global warming heats the water, most of the bacteria die, but the few variants that can stand the heat survive and multiply.

So variation is useful for the survival of the species over time. It is not necessarily useful for a particular individual: a variation that would help in hot water does nothing for a bacterium while the water stays cool — and some variations are harmful enough to kill the new cell.

Worked example 1

Two photocopy chains

Question. Rani photocopies a page, then photocopies the copy, and so on, ten times. Arjun makes ten copies, each straight from the original. Whose tenth copy will differ more from the original? How does this help you understand variation in reproduction?

Given: two ways of copying. Asked: which tenth copy differs more; the link to DNA copying.

Concept used: DNA copying is not absolutely accurate; changes are passed on (this topic). Why it applies: each new DNA copy is made from a copy that already carries earlier changes.

  1. 1

    In Rani's chain, each copy carries the smudges of the copy before it and adds new ones → changes add up.

  2. 2

    In Arjun's set, each copy has only its own few smudges.

  3. 3

    So Rani's tenth copy differs more from the original.

  4. 4

    In living things, each generation's DNA is copied from the parent's copy, which already carries variations from earlier generations → small variations build up over generations, like Rani's chain.

  5. Check: the textbook's point — "each new variation is made in a DNA copy that already has variations accumulated from previous generations" — matches Rani's chain ✔.

  6. Answer Rani's tenth copy. Variations in DNA copying are passed on and accumulate from generation to generation.

    Common trap: "Copying errors are always harmful." Many small changes do no harm; only drastic ones stop the new cell working.

Worked example 2

A hot summer in the lake

Question. A lake has a large population of one kind of bacterium. In a very hot summer the water temperature rises sharply. (a) What will happen to the population if all the bacteria are exactly alike and cannot stand heat? (b) What if a few are heat-resistant variants? (c) Use your answer to explain "variation is beneficial to the species but not necessarily to the individual."

Given: a population; a sudden change in its niche. Asked: the outcome in two cases; the link to the statement.

Concept used: importance of variation (this topic). Why it applies: the niche has changed drastically.

  1. 1

    (a) All identical and none heat-resistant → all die → the population is wiped out.

  2. 2

    (b) A few heat-resistant variants survive, reproduce and the population grows again from them.

  3. 3

    (c) The species survives in case (b) because of variation. But most individuals still died — their variations did not help them. A variation helps only if it suits the new conditions.

  4. Check: this is the same reasoning as the textbook's bacteria in temperate waters warmed by global warming ✔.

  5. Answer (a) wiped out (b) the variants survive and rebuild the population (c) variation saved the species, but not most individuals.

    Common trap: "Variation helps every individual." It helps only those few whose variation suits the new conditions.

Worked example 3

Why not just push the copy out?

Question. A student says, "When a cell reproduces, it could just copy its DNA and push the copy out of the cell." Explain why this would not work.

Given: a suggested shortcut. Asked: why it fails.

Concept used: DNA copying is accompanied by making extra cellular apparatus (this topic). Why it applies: a new cell needs more than DNA to stay alive.

  1. 1

    DNA is only the information (the blueprint).

  2. 2

    To carry out life processes, a cell also needs an organised cellular structure (its apparatus).

  3. 3

    A DNA copy pushed out alone would have no cellular apparatus, so it could not maintain life.

  4. 4

    So the cell makes extra cellular apparatus as well, and each DNA copy separates with its own apparatus → two working cells.

  5. Check: this gives "a cell divides to give rise to two cells", each able to live ✔.

  6. Answer A DNA copy pushed out would have no organised cell structure to keep it alive, so the cell must also make extra cellular apparatus and divide into two complete cells.

    Common trap: "Reproduction = copying DNA only." It is DNA copying plus extra cellular apparatus.

Common mistakes!

  • "Reproduction is needed to keep the individual alive."

    An individual lives without reproducing; reproduction costs it energy.

    Reproduction keeps the species going; life processes keep the individual alive.

  • "Reproduction is just copying the DNA."

    A DNA copy alone has no cell structure to live.

    DNA copy + extra cellular apparatus, then the cell divides.

  • "Offspring are exact copies of the parent."

    DNA copying is never perfectly reliable.

    Copies are similar, not identical — small variations.

  • "Variation helps every individual."

    Most individuals may not benefit; some variants die.

    Variation helps the species survive when the niche changes.

  • "Variation is always bad (copying 'errors')."

    Many variations do no harm, and some help survival.

    Drastic changes → cell dies; small changes → variation (basis of evolution).

Key points

  • Reproduction is not needed to keep an individual alive, but it creates new individuals that look very much like their parents.
  • The basic event in reproduction is making a copy of the DNA, together with additional cellular apparatus; then the cell divides into two.
  • DNA copying is never absolutely accurate, so copies are similar but not identical — variation. Drastic changes make the new cell die; others survive.
  • Consistent DNA copying keeps body design suited to the niche → stability of populations.
  • Variation lets a few individuals survive when the niche changes (heat-resistant bacteria) → useful for the species, not necessarily for the individual.
