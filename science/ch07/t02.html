Topic 2 of 5

Asexual reproduction

About 60 minutes · 5 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe fission — binary (Amoeba, Leishmania) and multiple (Plasmodium).
  • Describe fragmentation (Spirogyra), regeneration (Planaria, Hydra) and why complex organisms cannot use it, and budding (Hydra, yeast).
  • Describe vegetative propagation (potato, Bryophyllum, money-plant; layering and grafting) and its uses.
  • Describe spore formation in Rhizopus and its benefits.
  • Compare the modes of reproduction in unicellular and multicellular organisms.

A farmer plants a whole field of bananas — not one seed is used. A slice of bread left in a damp tin turns furry and black in three days. A tiny Hydra in a pond grows a "baby" out of its side. All three make new individuals from one parent, with no partner at all. How?

Quick reminder (Class 9)

Unicellular organisms have one cell that does everything. In multicellular organisms, specialised cells form tissues, and tissues form organs, each in its fixed place in the body.

Asexual reproduction makes new generations from a single individual. The method an organism uses depends on its body design.

  1. Fission — one cell splits (unicellular organisms)

For unicellular organisms, cell division (fission) itself makes new individuals.

  • Binary fission — the cell splits into two equal halves. Many bacteria and protozoa do this.
  • Amoeba: the split can happen in any plane.
  • Leishmania (causes kala-azar): has a whip-like structure at one end; the cell always splits in a fixed direction (a definite orientation) relative to this structure.
  • Multiple fission — the cell divides into many daughter cells at the same time. Example: the malarial parasite Plasmodium.

Number of daughter cells two many, all at once
Example Amoeba, Leishmania (many bacteria and protozoa) Plasmodium

  1. Budding — a small outgrowth that separates

  • Yeast (a unicellular fungus) forms small buds on its side; each bud breaks away and grows into a new yeast cell (NCERT Activity 7.1: yeast in sugar solution, seen under a microscope).
  • Hydra uses its regenerative cells for budding: cells at one particular spot keep dividing, so a bud bulges out as an outgrowth. The bud grows into a tiny individual, and when fully mature it detaches from the parent and lives independently.

  1. Fragmentation — breaking into pieces (simple multicellular organisms)

Spirogyra, a green filamentous alga found in ponds, simply breaks up into smaller pieces (fragments) when mature; each fragment grows into a new individual. This works because its body is simple — the filament has no separate tissues (NCERT Activity 7.4), so any piece can carry out all life processes.

  1. Why most multicellular organisms cannot just split

Most multicellular organisms are not a random collection of cells. Their specialised cells form tissues, tissues form organs, and organs must sit at definite positions in the body. Splitting cell by cell cannot rebuild such an organised body. So these organisms need more complex methods — usually one specific cell type that can grow, multiply and make other cell types.

  1. Regeneration — growing a whole body from a piece

Many fully differentiated organisms can grow new individuals from body parts. If Planaria (a flatworm) or Hydra is cut into many pieces, each piece can grow into a complete organism — regeneration.

  • It is carried out by specialised cells. They multiply to form a mass of cells, and different cells in this mass change into the various cell types and tissues, in an organised order called development.
  • Regeneration is not the same as reproduction: in nature, most organisms are not cut into pieces as their way of having young.
  • Complex organisms cannot regenerate a whole body: their organs and organ systems are highly specialised and must be placed in definite positions, and most of their cells cannot multiply and turn into all the other cell types needed.

  1. Vegetative propagation — new plants from roots, stems or leaves

In many plants, a root, stem or leaf can develop into a new plant under suitable conditions. Unlike most animals, plants can use this to reproduce.

  • Potato: the notches ("eyes") on a potato have buds; only pieces with a bud grow fresh green shoots and roots (Activity 7.5).
  • Bryophyllum: small buds grow in the notches on the edge of the leaf; when they drop onto soil, each becomes a new plant.
  • Money-plant: pieces of stem with at least one leaf grow fresh leaves in water; pieces cut from between two leaves usually do not (Activity 7.6 — expected result).
  • Farming methods: layering and grafting are used to grow sugarcane, roses and grapes.
  • Layering (not described in your textbook): a branch is bent down and covered with soil until it grows roots, then cut from the parent.

Why farmers use it:

  1. Plants bear flowers and fruits earlier than plants grown from seeds.
  2. It lets us grow plants that have lost the capacity to produce seeds — banana, orange, rose and jasmine.
  3. The new plants are genetically almost the same as the parent, so they keep all its features — a good variety stays good.

NCERT information box

In tissue culture, a few cells are taken from the growing tip of a plant and kept in a nutrient jelly in the lab, where they multiply into a small lump called a callus. The callus is moved to a medium with hormones for growth and differentiation, and the little plantlets are then planted in soil. Many disease-free plants can be grown from one parent this way; it is often used for ornamental plants.

  1. Spore formation — Rhizopus (bread mould)

On damp bread kept in a cool, dark place (Activity 7.2), a fuzzy mould appears.

  • The fine threads spreading over the bread are hyphae of the bread mould Rhizopus — they are not reproductive parts.
  • The tiny round heads on upright stalks are reproductive. Each head is a sporangium, packed with spores.
  • Each spore can develop into a new Rhizopus. Spores have thick walls that protect them; a spore waits until it lands on a damp surface, and then it starts to grow.

Benefits of spores: they are produced in large numbers, they are light and spread easily, and their thick walls let them survive unfavourable conditions until moisture is available.

Method Organisms (your textbook) What happens
Binary fission Amoeba, Leishmania one cell splits into two
Multiple fission Plasmodium one cell divides into many daughter cells at once
Budding Hydra, yeast a bud (outgrowth) forms, grows and detaches
Fragmentation Spirogyra the mature filament breaks into pieces; each grows
Regeneration Planaria, Hydra a cut piece grows into a whole organism (specialised cells)
Vegetative propagation potato, Bryophyllum, money-plant; sugarcane, rose, grapes a root, stem or leaf grows into a new plant
Spore formation Rhizopus sporangia release thick-walled spores that grow on moist surfaces

Unicellular vs multicellular — in one line: in unicellular organisms the whole cell divides (fission, or budding in yeast); multicellular organisms use fragmentation (if very simple), regeneration or budding (specialised cells), vegetative propagation (plants) or specific reproductive parts such as sporangia.

Virtual lab — CBSE Practical 12: binary fission in Amoeba and budding in yeast and Hydra (prepared slides)

Designed from NCERT Activities 7.1 and 7.3 and usual school practice; your teacher will confirm the steps against the CBSE practical list and the lab manual.

What you do:

  1. Place a prepared (permanent) slide of Amoeba on the stage of the microscope and focus under low power, then high power (Activity 7.3).
  2. Replace it with a prepared slide of Amoeba showing binary fission. Compare the two slides.
  3. Look at a prepared slide of yeast showing budding. (Optional, Activity 7.1: dissolve about 10 g of sugar in 100 mL of water, add a pinch of yeast to 20 mL of it in a test tube, plug it with cotton and keep it warm for 1–2 hours; then put a drop on a slide under a coverslip.)
  4. Look at a prepared slide of Hydra showing budding under low power (Hydra is large, so high power shows only part of it).
  5. Draw what you see in each slide and label it.

What you observe:

Slide Observation
Amoeba one irregular cell with a nucleus, cytoplasm and finger-like projections
Amoeba — binary fission the nucleus has divided into two; the cytoplasm is pinching in the middle (a "waist"); in a later stage, two daughter Amoebae
Yeast — budding oval cells; some have a small bud attached at one side; some buds have buds of their own, forming a short chain
Hydra — budding a tube-shaped body with tentacles at the top; a bud growing out from the side of the body, sometimes with small tentacles of its own

What it shows:

  1. Amoeba reproduces by binary fission: the nucleus divides first, then the cytoplasm, giving two cells.
  2. Yeast and Hydra reproduce by budding: a small outgrowth forms at one site, grows and finally separates.
  3. In both, one parent gives the new individuals — asexual reproduction.
Safety

carry the microscope with both hands; handle slides by their edges; never focus downwards with the coarse knob while looking through the eyepiece under high power — the slide may crack.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Name the method

Question. Name the method of asexual reproduction in each case: (a) sugarcane grown from pieces of stem (setts); (b) a flatworm cut into three pieces becomes three worms; (c) black fuzz with tiny round heads on stale bread; (d) yeast cells in dough, each with a small knob on one side; (e) green threads in a pond that break up and each piece grows.

Given: five situations. Asked: the method in each.

Concept used: the method chooser card (this topic). Why it applies: each method has a typical organism and a typical event.

  1. 1

    (a) Stem pieces of a plant grow into new plants → vegetative propagation.

  2. 2

    (b) Cut pieces each grow a whole body → regeneration (as in Planaria).

  3. 3

    (c) Round heads on stalks → sporangia with spores → spore formation (Rhizopus).

  4. 4

    (d) A small knob growing out of a yeast cell → budding.

  5. 5

    (e) A filament breaking into pieces that grow → fragmentation (as in Spirogyra).

  6. Check: each answer matches the "What happens" column of the method chooser card ✔.

  7. Answer (a) vegetative propagation (b) regeneration (c) spore formation (d) budding (e) fragmentation

    Common trap: (d) "fission". Yeast buds — a small outgrowth forms and separates; the cell does not split into two equal halves.

Worked example 2

Roses by layering, not by seed

Question. A rose grower has one bush with large, deep-red flowers. Why does she grow new bushes by layering rather than from seeds? Give three reasons.

Given: a prized rose bush. Asked: three reasons for vegetative propagation.

Concept used: uses of vegetative propagation (this topic). Why it applies: layering is a vegetative method.

  1. 1

    Same characteristics → plants made by vegetative propagation are genetically similar enough to the parent to have all its characteristics — the same large, deep-red flowers.

  2. 2

    Earlier flowers → such plants bear flowers earlier than plants grown from seeds.

  3. 3

    Seeds not needed → the rose is one of the plants that have lost the capacity to produce seeds, so vegetative propagation lets it be grown at all.

  4. Check: all three reasons are the uses of vegetative propagation listed in this topic ✔.

  5. Answer The new bushes keep all the parent's characteristics, flower earlier, and roses have lost the capacity to produce seeds.

    Common trap: "Seeds take more water." Give the reasons your textbook gives: same characters, earlier flowering, seedless plants.

Worked example 3

Budding or regeneration?

Question. Hydra can reproduce by budding and can also regenerate. Explain the difference between the two, and why only one of them is a normal way of reproducing.

Given: two processes in Hydra. Asked: the difference; which is normal reproduction.

Concept used: budding and regeneration (this topic). Why it applies: both use specialised (regenerative) cells but start differently.

  1. 1

    Budding → an outgrowth forms at one specific site of an uncut body by repeated cell division, grows into a tiny Hydra and detaches.

  2. 2

    Regeneration → happens when the body is cut or broken into pieces; each piece grows the missing parts.

  3. 3

    Normal reproduction → budding happens on its own as part of the life of the organism; regeneration needs the body to be cut up, and organisms do not normally depend on being cut up to reproduce.

  4. Check: the textbook says regeneration "is not the same as reproduction" ✔.

  5. Answer Budding: a bud grows from one site of the whole body and detaches — a normal method of reproduction. Regeneration: a cut piece grows into a whole organism — it depends on injury, so it is not a normal way to reproduce.

    Common trap: "Hydra's bud is a regenerated piece." The bud grows on an uncut parent; regeneration starts from a cut piece.

Worked example 4

Why bread mould spreads so fast

Question. A slice of bread kept in a damp tin is covered with mould in three days, but a dry slice kept in an open plate stays almost clean. Explain, using what you know about spores.

Given: damp bread vs dry bread. Asked: why mould spreads on the damp slice.

Concept used: spore formation in Rhizopus (this topic). Why it applies: spores are everywhere, but they grow only on moist surfaces.

  1. 1

    Spores are everywhere → Rhizopus sporangia release large numbers of tiny spores that spread easily through the air.

  2. 2

    Thick walls → the spores survive until they land on a moist surface.

  3. 3

    Damp bread → it gives the spores moisture (and food), so they begin to grow, forming hyphae and new sporangia, which release still more spores.

  4. 4

    Dry bread → spores land but cannot begin to grow without moisture.

  5. Check: this fits the textbook: spores are protected "until they come into contact with another moist surface and can begin to grow" ✔.

  6. Answer Spores in the air land on both slices, but only the damp slice gives them the moisture they need to grow; once growing, the mould forms sporangia and releases still more spores.

    Common trap: "The hyphae spread the mould." Hyphae are not reproductive parts; the spores spread it.

Spirogyra can reproduce by simply breaking into pieces, but a goat cannot. Give the reason.

Answer: Spirogyra has a simple body — a filament without separate tissues — so every piece can carry out all life processes and grow. A goat's body is highly organised: tissues form organs placed at definite positions, so a piece of it cannot grow into a whole goat (cell-by-cell division is impractical).

Common mistakes!

  • "Yeast reproduces by fission."

    Yeast puts out buds.

    Yeast and Hydra — budding.

  • "Plasmodium shows binary fission" or "Amoeba shows multiple fission."

    The two are swapped.

    Binary (two): Amoeba, Leishmania. Multiple (many, at once): Plasmodium.

  • "Spirogyra regenerates."

    It breaks up on its own when mature.

    Spirogyra — fragmentation; Planaria — regeneration (after being cut).

  • "Regeneration is a normal way to reproduce."

    It depends on the body being cut.

    Regeneration is not the same as reproduction.

  • "Hydra's bud is a regenerated piece."

    The bud grows on an uncut body.

    Budding: outgrowth at one site → detaches.

  • "The hyphae of bread mould are its reproductive parts."

    Your textbook says they are not.

    Sporangia (blobs on stalks) contain the spores.

  • "Any piece of potato will grow."

    Only pieces with a bud (eye) grow.

    Buds in the notches → shoots and roots.

Key points

  • Asexual reproduction: new individuals from a single parent; the method depends on body design.
  • Fission (unicellular): binary — two cells (Amoeba, any plane; Leishmania, definite orientation); multiple — many cells at once (Plasmodium).
  • Budding: yeast; Hydra (outgrowth from repeated cell division at one site, which detaches). Fragmentation: Spirogyra.
  • Regeneration (Planaria, Hydra): specialised cells → mass of cells → development; not the same as reproduction; complex organisms cannot use it.
  • Vegetative propagation: root, stem or leaf → new plant (potato, Bryophyllum, money-plant; layering and grafting for sugarcane, roses, grapes); earlier flowers and fruits, seedless plants (banana, orange, rose, jasmine), all the parent's characteristics.
  • Spore formation (Rhizopus): sporangia contain spores with thick protective walls; they grow on moist surfaces. Hyphae are not reproductive.
