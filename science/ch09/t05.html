Topic 5 of 7

Refraction and refractive index

About 50 minutes · 5 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain refraction as the bending of light caused by a change in its speed, with everyday examples.
  • Trace refraction through a rectangular glass slab — towards the normal on entering, away from it on leaving, emergent ray parallel to the incident ray, with a lateral shift.
  • State the laws of refraction, including Snell's law.
  • Define absolute and relative refractive index and calculate the speed of light in a medium or a refractive index.
  • Compare the optical density of media using their refractive indices.

Drop a one-rupee coin into a bucket of water and try to pick it up in one quick grab, looking from the side. Most people miss — their hand goes in at the wrong place. The coin is not where it seems to be. Light coming up from the coin changes direction as it leaves the water, and your brain is fooled. This bending of light is called refraction.

Everyday clues that light bends

  • A pencil standing in a glass of water looks broken at the water surface.
  • The bottom of a pond or a bucket looks raised — the water seems shallower than it is.
  • Printed letters under a thick glass slab look raised when seen from above.
  • A lemon in a glass of water looks bigger from the side.

In each case, light travels from one transparent material (medium) into another, and its direction changes at the boundary.

Refraction is the change in the direction of light when it passes at an angle from one transparent medium into another. It happens because light travels at different speeds in different media.

(i) You usually miss the coin. (ii) The hidden coin comes back into view as water is poured. (iii) With the slab at an angle, the line under the glass seems shifted (bent) at the edges; with the slab at 90° to the line, it does not look bent — but seen from above, it looks raised.
Shows
Light coming from an object under water or glass changes direction at the surface, so the object appears to be somewhere else — usually raised. Light that meets the surface head-on (at 90°) does not bend.

Why does light bend? It changes speed

Light is fastest in a vacuum (empty space): about 3×108 m s−13 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}3×108 m s−1. In air it is only very slightly slower. In water and glass it is much slower.

Reminder: speed = distance ÷ time. 3×108 m s−13 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}3×108 m s−1 means 300 000 km every second — enough to go round the Earth about 7.5 times in one second.

An analogy (not what light "is", just a picture — see animation V11): a toy car rolling at an angle onto a carpet slows first on the wheel that touches the carpet, so it swings towards the normal; rolling off, the first wheel to leave speeds up and the car swings away from it. Light at a boundary behaves in a similar way.

Light goes from… Its speed… It bends…
a faster medium to a slower one (e.g. air → glass) decreases towards the normal
a slower medium to a faster one (e.g. glass → air) increases away from the normal
any medium to another, along the normal (at 90° to the surface) changes not at all — it goes straight through

The normal is the line drawn at 90° to the boundary at the point where the ray strikes. Angles are always measured from the normal, not from the surface.

Refraction through a rectangular glass slab (Activity 9.10, CBSE Practical 11)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Pin a white sheet on a drawing board and place a rectangular glass slab in the middle. Draw its outline ABCD. Fix two pins E and F upright so that the line EF meets the edge AB at an angle. Looking through the opposite face CD, fix two more pins G and H so that they appear to be in a straight line with the images of E and F. Remove the slab and pins. Join EF and extend it to meet AB at O; join HG and extend it to meet CD at O′. Join O to O′. Draw the normals at O and O′. Measure the angle of incidence iii (at O), the angle of refraction rrr (inside the glass) and the angle of emergence eee (at O′). Repeat for two or three different angles of incidence.
See
At O (air → glass) the ray bends towards the normal: rrr is smaller than iii. At O′ (glass → air) it bends away from the normal. The emergent ray O′H is parallel to the incident ray EO, but shifted sideways. Measured angles always give e=ie = ie=i. (For ordinary glass, i=30°i = 30°i=30° gives r≈19°r \approx 19°r≈19°; i=45°i = 45°i=45° gives r≈28°r \approx 28°r≈28°; i=60°i = 60°i=60° gives r≈35°r \approx 35°r≈35°.)
Shows
The two faces of the slab are parallel, so the bending at the second face exactly undoes the bending at the first. The ray comes out in its original direction, displaced sideways. This sideways distance between the incident ray (extended) and the emergent ray is the lateral shift (lateral displacement). A thicker slab gives a bigger shift.

If the ray strikes the slab along the normal (i=0°i = 0°i=0°), it passes straight through without bending, and there is no lateral shift.

The laws of refraction

  1. The incident ray, the normal at the point where it strikes the boundary, and the refracted ray all lie in one plane.
  2. For light of a given colour and a given pair of media, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant:
sin⁡isin⁡r=constant\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \text{constant}sinrsini​=constantSwipe to see the whole equation →

The second law is called Snell's law (it applies for angles between 0° and 90°). This constant is the refractive index of the second medium relative to the first (the medium the light enters, compared with the one it comes from).

Refractive index

The refractive index of a medium tells you how many times slower light travels in it compared with another medium.

Relative refractive index. For light going from medium 1 into medium 2, with speeds v1v_1v1​ and v2v_2v2​:

Air (n=1.0003n = 1.0003n=1.0003) is so close to vacuum that you can use nair=1n_{\text{air}} = 1nair​=1 and "speed in air" ≈ ccc in every calculation.

A useful link: n21=v1v2=c/n1c/n2=n2n1n_{21} = \dfrac{v_1}{v_2} = \dfrac{c / n_1}{c / n_2} = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}n21​=v2​v1​​=c/n2​c/n1​​=n1​n2​​.

Refractive index has no unit — it is a ratio of two speeds.

Optical density: which medium is "denser"?

Compare two media by their refractive indices:

  • the one with the larger nnn is optically denser — light is slower there;
  • the one with the smaller nnn is optically rarer — light is faster there.

From now on, "denser medium" in this chapter means optically denser.

Did you know?Not in board exam
NCERT information box

How strongly a material slows light (optical density) and how heavy it is for its size (mass density) are two different things. Kerosene has a higher refractive index than water, so it is optically denser — yet it is lighter than water and floats on it.

Worked example 4

Which way does it bend?

Question. A ray of light passes at an angle from crown glass (n=1.52n = 1.52n=1.52) into alcohol (n=1.36n = 1.36n=1.36). (a) Which medium is optically denser? (b) Does the ray speed up or slow down? (c) Does it bend towards or away from the normal?

Given: nglass=1.52n_{\text{glass}} = 1.52nglass​=1.52, nalcohol=1.36n_{\text{alcohol}} = 1.36nalcohol​=1.36. Asked: optical density, speed change, direction of bending.

Concept used: larger nnn → optically denser → slower light; denser to rarer → speeds up → bends away from the normal. Why it applies: the ray is oblique, so it will bend at the boundary.

  1. 1

    Compare: 1.52 > 1.36, so crown glass is optically denser than alcohol.

  2. 2

    Light is slower in the denser medium, so going from glass into alcohol it speeds up.

  3. 3

    A ray that speeds up bends away from the normal.

  4. Check: this matches the toy-car picture — the first "wheel" to reach the faster region speeds up and the path swings away from the normal ✔.

  5. Answer (a) Crown glass (b) It speeds up (c) It bends away from the normal.

    Common trap: Thinking that light always bends towards the normal when it enters a liquid. The direction depends only on which medium has the larger refractive index — here the liquid is the rarer one.

Quick check · 3 of 3Short answer2 marks

A ray enters a rectangular glass slab at an angle of incidence of 50°. (a) What is the angle of emergence? (b) Why is the emergent ray parallel to the incident ray?

Answer: (a) 50° — the angle of emergence equals the angle of incidence. (b) The two faces of the slab are parallel. The bending towards the normal at the first face is exactly undone by the equal and opposite bending away from the normal at the second face, so the ray leaves in its original direction (shifted sideways).

The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray, but not on the same line — there is a lateral shift.

Common mistakes!

  • "Light bends towards the normal when it speeds up"

    It is the other way round.

    Slows down (into denser) → towards; speeds up (into rarer) → away.

  • Measuring angles from the surface

    All angles in optics are measured from the normal.

    Draw the normal (dashed, at 90°) first, then measure.

  • Drawing the emergent ray continuing along the incident ray

    The ray is shifted sideways.

    Emergent ray parallel to the incident ray, with a lateral shift.

  • Giving refractive index a unit

    It is a ratio of two speeds.

    No unit.

  • "Heavier liquid = optically denser"

    Mass density and optical density are different.

    Compare refractive indices.

  • Writing n=vcn = \dfrac{v}{c}n=cv​

    That gives a number less than 1 for every material.

    n=cvn = \dfrac{c}{v}n=vc​ — the bigger speed on top.

Key points

  • Refraction = change in direction of light passing obliquely from one medium to another, caused by a change in speed.
  • Into an optically denser medium (slower): bends towards the normal. Into a rarer medium (faster): bends away. Along the normal: no bending.
  • Glass slab: e=ie = ie=i; emergent ray parallel to incident ray, with a lateral shift.
  • Laws of refraction: same plane; Snell's law sin⁡isin⁡r=\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} =sinrsini​= constant.
  • nm=cvn_m = \dfrac{c}{v}nm​=vc​; n21=v1v2=n2n1n_{21} = \dfrac{v_1}{v_2} = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}n21​=v2​v1​​=n1​n2​​. No unit. Larger nnn → optically denser → slower light.
