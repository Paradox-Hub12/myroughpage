Topic 3 of 7

Images formed by a convex mirror

About 30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Draw ray diagrams for a convex mirror for an object at infinity and at a finite distance.
  • State that a convex mirror always forms a virtual, erect and diminished image.
  • Explain why convex mirrors are used as rear-view mirrors (erect image, wider field of view).

On a narrow hill road, a big round mirror stands on a pole at a blind bend. Drivers coming from either side can see each other's vehicles in it, even round the corner. That mirror bulges outwards — it is convex. Why would anyone choose a mirror that makes everything look smaller?

The easy rays for a convex mirror

They are the same four rays as for a concave mirror, with one change in wording: F and C are now behind the mirror, so the light can only be aimed at them or appear to come from them.

Ray Goes in… Comes out…
Ray 1 parallel to the principal axis spreading out, as if it came from F behind the mirror (draw the extension back to F dashed)
Ray 2 aimed towards F (behind the mirror) parallel to the principal axis
Ray 3 aimed towards C (behind the mirror) straight back along itself — it travels along the normal
Ray 4 to the pole P, at an angle at the same angle on the other side of the axis

The easiest pair is ray 1 and ray 3.

Case 1: object at infinity

Parallel rays from a very distant object reflect and spread out. Extended backwards, they meet at F behind the mirror.
Image: at F, behind the mirror; highly diminished (point-sized); virtual and erect.

Case 2: object anywhere in front of the mirror

  1. Ray 1 from the top of the object (B) goes parallel to the axis and reflects outwards; its dashed extension goes back to F.
  2. Ray 3 from B is aimed at C; it reflects straight back; its dashed extension goes on to C.
  3. The two dashed lines meet between P and F, behind the mirror, above the axis. That is B′.

Image: between P and F, behind the mirror; diminished; virtual and erect.

Object position Image position Size Nature
At infinity At F, behind the mirror Highly diminished, point-sized Virtual, erect
Anywhere between infinity and P Between P and F, behind the mirror Diminished Virtual, erect

The one-line rule: a convex mirror always gives a virtual, erect, diminished image, behind the mirror, between P and F — wherever the object is. As the object comes closer, the image grows (but stays smaller than the object) and moves from F towards P.

Why is the image never real?

The reflected rays from a convex mirror always spread out — the mirror bulges and sends light away from its axis. Spreading rays never meet in front of the mirror, so no real image is possible. Only their backward extensions meet, behind the mirror.

Activity: a pencil in a convex mirror (Activity 9.5)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Hold a convex mirror in one hand and an upright pencil in the other. Look at the pencil's image. Slowly move the pencil away from the mirror.
See
The image is always upright and smaller than the pencil. As the pencil moves away, the image gets even smaller and moves towards the focus behind the mirror.
Shows
A convex mirror always forms an erect, diminished image; the image approaches F as the object approaches infinity.

Activity: seeing a whole tall tree (Activity 9.6)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Look at a distant tall tree or building in a plane mirror, then in a concave mirror, then in a convex mirror.
See
A small plane mirror shows only part of the tree. A concave mirror gives an inverted image that may be blurred or only partly visible. A small convex mirror shows the full tree, small and upright.
Shows
A convex mirror squeezes a large scene into a small image — it has a wide field of view.

Uses of convex mirrors

Rear-view (wing) mirrors of vehicles are convex because:

  1. they always give an erect image, so the driver never sees traffic upside down; and
  2. they bulge outwards, so they collect light from a much wider area behind the vehicle than a plane mirror of the same size (wider field of view).

The images are smaller, so vehicles look farther away than they are — which is why many wing mirrors carry the warning "objects in mirror are closer than they appear".

Convex mirrors are also used at blind turns on hill roads, in shops and ATM booths for security, and at parking exits — anywhere a wide view matters more than true size.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Ray diagram: object in front of a convex mirror

Question. A 3 cm tall object is placed 24 cm in front of a convex mirror of focal length 12 cm. Draw the ray diagram and describe the image.

Given: convex mirror, f = 12 cm, so C is 24 cm behind the mirror; object 3 cm tall, 24 cm in front. Asked: ray diagram and description of the image.

Concept used: ray 1 (parallel → appears to come from F) and ray 3 (aimed at C → back along itself), with dashed backward extensions. Why it applies: both rays have known paths, and for a convex mirror the reflected rays always diverge.

  1. 1

    Draw the principal axis and the mirror (bulging towards the object, back shaded). Mark P; mark F at 12 cm and C at 24 cm behind the mirror.

  2. 2

    Draw AB, 3 cm tall, at 24 cm in front of P.

  3. 3

    Ray 1: from B parallel to the axis to the mirror. Reflect it so that it moves up and away from the axis, in line with F. Draw its extension behind the mirror back to F as a dashed line.

  4. 4

    Ray 3: from B, draw a line aimed at C behind the mirror. Where it meets the mirror, it reflects straight back along itself. Continue the line behind the mirror to C as a dashed line.

  5. 5

    The two dashed lines cross behind the mirror, between P and F, above the axis. Mark B′ and draw A′B′ (dashed).

  6. Check: the convex-mirror rule says virtual, erect, diminished, between P and F ✔. To scale, A′B′ is about 8 cm behind the mirror and about 1 cm tall — we will confirm these in Topic 4.

  7. Answer Image behind the mirror, between P and F (about 8 cm behind); diminished (about 1 cm); virtual and erect.

    Common trap: Putting F and C in front of a convex mirror, which makes the rays cross in front and gives a (wrong) real image.

Worked example 2

Explaining the choice of a wing mirror

Question. Arnav says, "A plane mirror would make a better rear-view mirror, because it shows vehicles at their true size." Give two reasons why vehicles use convex mirrors instead.

Given: a claim comparing plane and convex rear-view mirrors. Asked: two reasons for using convex mirrors.

Concept used: a convex mirror always forms an erect, diminished, virtual image and has a wide field of view. Why it applies: a driver needs to see as much of the road behind as possible, always the right way up.

  1. 1

    A convex mirror always gives an erect image, whatever the distance of the vehicle behind — the driver never sees an inverted image.

  2. 2

    Because it bulges outwards, a convex mirror reflects light from a much wider region behind the vehicle into the driver's eye than a plane mirror of the same size. More lanes and more vehicles are visible — fewer blind spots.

  3. 3

    The price is that vehicles look smaller (so they seem farther away). Drivers learn to allow for this; safety from the wider view matters more than true size.

  4. Check: both reasons come straight from the convex-mirror rule (always erect) and its shape (diverging, wide view) ✔.

  5. Answer (1) It always gives an erect image. (2) It gives a much wider field of view, so the driver sees a larger area behind the vehicle.

    Common trap: Saying "because it gives a real image". A convex mirror never gives a real image.

In a ray diagram for a convex mirror, a student draws the ray that comes in parallel to the axis and reflects it so that it passes through a point F in front of the mirror. What is wrong? How should the reflected ray be drawn?

Answer: For a convex mirror, F is behind the mirror. The reflected ray must move away from the axis, along the line that points back to F; the part behind the mirror is drawn dashed from the mirror to F.

Mark F and C behind a convex mirror before drawing any ray — then the rays cannot go wrong.

Common mistakes!

  • Drawing F and C in front of a convex mirror

    A convex mirror bulges towards the object; its centre is behind it.

    Mark F and C behind the mirror, with PF = FC.

  • Showing the reflected rays meeting in front

    Reflected rays from a convex mirror always diverge.

    Extend them backwards (dashed) to meet behind the mirror.

  • "The image is at F" for every object

    Only an object at infinity gives an image at F.

    Finite distance → image between P and F.

  • "Convex mirrors give an enlarged image when the object is close"

    The image grows, but it is always smaller than the object.

    Always diminished, virtual and erect.

  • Arrowheads on the dashed extensions

    Extensions are not light paths.

    Arrowheads only on real (solid) rays.

Key points

  • Convex mirror: F and C are behind the mirror; reflected rays always diverge.
  • Object at infinity → image at F (behind), point-sized, virtual and erect.
  • Object anywhere else → image between P and F, behind the mirror, diminished, virtual and erect.
  • Rear-view mirrors are convex: always erect image and wider field of view.
  • Draw extensions behind the mirror dashed, with no arrowheads.
