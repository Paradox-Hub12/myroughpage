Spherical mirrors: the vocabulary

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • State the laws of reflection and the properties of the image formed by a plane mirror.
  • Distinguish concave and convex mirrors and identify the pole, centre of curvature, radius of curvature, principal axis, principal focus, focal length and aperture.
  • Use the relation R=2fR = 2fR=2f between radius of curvature and focal length.

Pick up a clean steel spoon and look into the hollow side, holding it close. Your face looks big. Hold it at arm's length and you suddenly appear upside down. Now turn the spoon over: on the bulging side you are always small and the right way up. Same spoon, same face, very different images. Why? To answer that, you first need the words scientists use for curved mirrors.

Quick reminder: reflection and the plane mirror

Laws of reflection

(you met these in earlier classes)

  1. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection (∠i=∠r\angle i = \angle r∠i=∠r). Both angles are measured from the normal — the line at 90° to the surface at the point where the ray strikes.
  2. The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal at that point all lie in one flat plane (think: drawn on the same sheet of paper).

These two laws work for every reflecting surface — flat or curved. A curved mirror is simply made of many tiny flat pieces, each obeying the laws.

The image in a plane (flat) mirror:

  • is virtual — it cannot be caught on a screen, because the light only seems to come from behind the mirror;
  • is erect (upright);
  • is the same size as the object;
  • forms the same distance behind the mirror as the object stands in front of it;
  • is laterally inverted — your right hand looks like a left hand in the mirror.

Real or virtual? A real image forms where light rays actually meet; you can catch it on a screen (a wall, a sheet of paper). A virtual image forms where rays only appear to come from; no screen can catch it, but your eye can still see it — that is exactly how you see yourself in a mirror.

Spherical mirrors: concave and convex

Imagine a hollow glass ball, silvered (made shiny) on one side. Cut out a small piece of it. That piece is a spherical mirror — its reflecting surface is part of the surface of a sphere.

Type Which side reflects? Everyday picture Effect on parallel light
Concave mirror the inner, hollow side ("caves in") inside of a spoon or a steel katori brings rays together (converging mirror)
Convex mirror the outer, bulging side back of a spoon; a shiny steel tumbler spreads rays apart (diverging mirror)
Memory tip

concave goes in like a cave.

In diagrams, the back of the mirror is shaded with short slanting lines. The unshaded side is the shiny, reflecting side.

The vocabulary — seven words to know

Use the figure in visual V2 as you read. Each term is something you must be able to mark on a diagram.

  1. Pole (P) — the centre point of the mirror's reflecting surface. It lies on the mirror.
  2. Centre of curvature (C) — the centre of the sphere that the mirror was cut from. It is not on the mirror. For a concave mirror, C is in front of the mirror (on the shiny side). For a convex mirror, C is behind the mirror.
  3. Radius of curvature (R) — the radius of that sphere, so R is the distance PC.
  4. Principal axis — the straight line through P and C. It meets the mirror at 90° at the pole, so it is the normal to the mirror at P.
  5. Principal focus (F) —
    - Concave mirror: send in light that travels parallel to the principal axis. After reflection, every one of these rays passes through one point on the axis. That point is F. It is in front of the mirror, so the light really meets there — a real focus.
    - Convex mirror: rays parallel to the axis spread out after reflection. If you extend the reflected rays backwards (behind the mirror), they all seem to start from one point on the axis. That point is F. Light never actually reaches it — a virtual focus.
  6. Focal length (f) — the distance from the pole to the principal focus, PF.
  7. Aperture — the width (diameter) of the reflecting surface. In this chapter we only use mirrors whose aperture is small compared with R. (Big, deep mirrors do not focus sharply to one point.)

Activity: burning paper with a concave mirror (Activity 9.2)

Safety

Never look at the Sun, or at sunlight reflected by a mirror. It can permanently damage your eyes. Do this activity with a teacher, away from anything that can catch fire.

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Point the shiny side of a concave mirror towards the Sun. Hold a sheet of paper in front of it and move the paper back and forth until a tiny, very bright spot of light appears. Hold the mirror and paper still for a few minutes.
See
The paper starts to smoke and may catch fire.
Shows
The Sun is so far away that its rays arrive parallel. The concave mirror brings them together at one point — its principal focus. All the energy lands on a tiny area, so the paper heats up. The bright spot is actually a tiny real image of the Sun, and its distance from the mirror is (approximately) the focal length.

This is also how you find the focal length of a concave mirror in CBSE Practical 10: form a sharp image of a distant object (a tree or a building far outside the window) on a screen and measure the mirror-to-screen distance.

How R and f are connected: R = 2f

For a spherical mirror with a small aperture, the principal focus sits exactly halfway between the pole and the centre of curvature:

R=2forf=R2R = 2f \qquad\text{or}\qquad f = \dfrac{R}{2}R=2forf=2R​Swipe to see the whole equation →

So on the principal axis the order is always P → F → C, with PF = FC. For a concave mirror, F and C are both in front of the mirror, on the object's side; for a convex mirror, both are behind it.

Why halfway? A ray parallel to the axis strikes the mirror and reflects. The radius drawn to that point is the normal there, so the ray makes equal angles with it. Simple geometry then puts the crossing point midway between P and C when the mirror is shallow. You do not need this proof for the exam — only the result.

Beyond the syllabus: light is not always well described as straight-line rays. When light passes a very tiny obstacle or gap it spreads around it (diffraction), which needs a wave picture; and when light interacts with matter it can behave like a stream of particles. Modern physics combines both ideas. In this chapter, straight-line rays are all you need.

Worked example 1

Focal length from the radius of curvature

Question. A convex mirror is cut from a sphere of radius 24 cm. What is its focal length? Where is the principal focus?

Given: convex mirror, radius of curvature R = 24 cm. Asked: the focal length f and the position of F.

Concept used: R=2fR = 2fR=2f, so f=R2f = \dfrac{R}{2}f=2R​. Why it applies: the mirror is spherical (and we assume a small aperture), so F is halfway between P and C.

  1. 1

    Write the relation.

    f=R2f = \dfrac{R}{2}f=2R​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  2. 2

    Substitute R = 24 cm.

    f=24 cm2f = \dfrac{24\ \text{cm}}{2}f=224 cm​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  3. 3

    Divide.

    f=12 cmf = 12\ \text{cm}f=12 cmSwipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    For a convex mirror, C is behind the mirror, so F (halfway between P and C) is also behind the mirror.

  5. Check: 2 × 12 cm = 24 cm = R ✔. F lies between P and C, as it must.

  6. Answer f = 12 cm; the principal focus is 12 cm behind the mirror (on the side away from the object).

    Common trap: Doubling instead of halving (writing f = 48 cm). Remember: the focus is between P and C, so f is smaller than R.

Which statement about the centre of curvature (C) of a convex mirror is correct?

That is the pole, P. The centre of curvature is never on the mirror.
A convex mirror bulges towards you, so the sphere it came from — and its centre — is behind the mirror.
F lies between P and C, not the other way round — and for a convex mirror both are behind the mirror.
P is on the mirror; C is a distance R away from it.

Quick check · 3 of 3True/False with justification1 mark

True or false? "A virtual image cannot be seen, because no light reaches it." Justify.

Answer: False. A virtual image cannot be caught on a screen, because the rays do not actually meet there. But reflected light does enter your eye, and your eye traces it back to where it seems to come from — so you see the image. Your reflection in a plane mirror is a virtual image you see every day.

The test for "real" is the screen, not the eye.

Common mistakes!

  • Marking C on the mirror

    C is the centre of the sphere, not a point on its surface.

    P is on the mirror; C is a distance R away along the principal axis.

  • Writing R=f2R = \dfrac{f}{2}R=2f​ or f=2Rf = 2Rf=2R

    F is between P and C, so fff is the smaller distance.

    R=2fR = 2fR=2f and f=R2f = \dfrac{R}{2}f=2R​.

  • Putting F of a convex mirror in front of it

    For a convex mirror, C — and so F — is behind the mirror.

    Concave: F and C in front. Convex: F and C behind.

  • "Virtual images can't be seen"

    They can be seen; they just can't be caught on a screen.

    Real = can be caught on a screen; virtual = cannot.

  • Shading the shiny side

    The shaded side is the back (non-reflecting).

    Shade the side that does not reflect.

Key points

  • Laws of reflection hold for all mirrors: angle of incidence = angle of reflection, measured from the normal.
  • Concave (caves in) = converging; convex (bulges out) = diverging.
  • P on the mirror; C = centre of the sphere; R = PC; principal axis through P and C; F = point where parallel rays meet (concave, real) or seem to come from (convex, virtual); f = PF.
  • R = 2f: F is midway between P and C.
  • Real image: can be caught on a screen. Virtual image: cannot, but can still be seen.
