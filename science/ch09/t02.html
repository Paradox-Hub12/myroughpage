Topic 2 of 7

Images formed by a concave mirror

About 50 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • State the four standard rays for a concave mirror and how each one reflects.
  • Draw ray diagrams for all six object positions in front of a concave mirror.
  • State the position, size and nature of the image for each object position.
  • Relate the uses of concave mirrors (torch, shaving mirror, dentist's mirror, solar furnace) to the images they form.

A dentist's small round mirror shows a tooth bigger than it is. The reflector behind a torch bulb sends out a straight, strong beam. A giant dish of mirrors can melt metal with sunlight. All three are concave mirrors. The only thing that changes is where the object sits compared with F and C. Learn that one idea, and the six "cases" stop being things to memorise.

Why you only need two rays

Every point on an object sends out rays in all directions. To find where the image of the top of the object forms, you only need two rays from that top point. Where the two reflected rays meet (or seem to meet), the image of the top is formed. Pick rays whose path after reflection you already know — the "easy rays".

The four easy rays for a concave mirror

Ray Goes in… Comes out… Why
Ray 1 parallel to the principal axis through F that is what "principal focus" means (Topic 1)
Ray 2 through F parallel to the principal axis ray 1 run backwards — light paths are reversible
Ray 3 through C straight back along itself a line from C is a radius, so it hits the mirror along the normal: angle of incidence = 0°
Ray 4 to the pole P, at an angle at the same angle on the other side of the axis the principal axis is the normal at P, so ∠i = ∠r

Any two of these will do. Rays 1 and 3, or rays 1 and 2, are usually the neatest.

The ray-diagram method (use it every time)

  1. Draw the principal axis as a long straight line, and the mirror as an arc (back shaded). Mark P, then F and C with PF = FC.
  2. Draw the object as an upright arrow standing on the axis at the given position.
  3. From the top of the object, draw ray 1 and then ray 2 (or ray 3). Put an arrowhead on every ray, before and after the mirror.
  4. Find where the reflected rays meet. If they do not meet in front of the mirror, extend them backwards behind the mirror as dashed lines and find where the extensions meet.
  5. Draw the image as an arrow from the axis up (or down) to that meeting point. A real image (rays meet) — solid arrow; a virtual image (extensions meet) — dashed arrow.
  6. Describe the image in three words-groups: position (where?), size (enlarged / same size / diminished), nature (real and inverted, or virtual and erect).

Draw-it-in-the-exam checklist

  • ☐ Ruler and sharp pencil; principal axis drawn first.
  • ☐ P, F and C labelled; F exactly halfway between P and C.
  • ☐ Object drawn as an upright arrow, labelled AB (A on the axis, B at the top).
  • ☐ At least two rays from B, each with an arrowhead.
  • ☐ Rays "through F" go through the F in front of the mirror.
  • ☐ Real rays solid; extensions behind the mirror dashed (no arrowheads on dashed lines).
  • ☐ Image drawn and labelled A′B′.
  • ☐ One line describing position, size and nature.

The six object positions

As the object moves towards the mirror, the image moves away from it and gets bigger — until the object reaches F. Then the image "jumps" behind the mirror.

1. Object at infinity (very far away, e.g. the Sun). Rays from it arrive parallel to one another. They all meet at F.
Image: at F; highly diminished (point-sized); real and inverted.

2. Object beyond C. Ray 1 goes through F; ray 3 comes back through C. They cross between F and C.
Image: between F and C; diminished; real and inverted.

3. Object at C. The reflected rays cross at C itself.
Image: at C; same size; real and inverted.

4. Object between C and F. The crossing point moves out beyond C.
Image: beyond C; enlarged; real and inverted.

5. Object at F. A ray from B "through F" would run straight down and never reach the mirror, so use ray 1 and ray 3 (or the ray to P). Every reflected ray comes out parallel, and parallel rays never meet.
Image: at infinity; highly enlarged; real and inverted. (We say "the image is formed at infinity".)

6. Object between P and F. The reflected rays spread out and never meet in front of the mirror. Extended backwards (dashed), they meet behind the mirror.
Image: behind the mirror; enlarged; virtual and erect.

Object position Image position Size Nature
At infinity At F Highly diminished, point-sized Real, inverted
Beyond C Between F and C Diminished Real, inverted
At C At C Same size Real, inverted
Between C and F Beyond C Enlarged Real, inverted
At F At infinity Highly enlarged Real, inverted
Between P and F Behind the mirror Enlarged Virtual, erect

Two patterns that replace memorising:

  • Object outside F → real and inverted. Object inside F → virtual, erect and enlarged. F is the dividing line.
  • C is the "same size" point. Object farther than C → image smaller; object between C and F → image bigger.

Activity: hunting for the image with a candle (Activities 9.3 and 9.4)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Find the approximate focal length of a concave mirror from a distant object. On a table, chalk three lines one focal length apart to mark P, F and C. Place a burning candle far beyond C, then just beyond C, at C, between F and C, at F, and between P and F. Each time, move a paper screen to get a sharp image of the flame. Then draw neat ray diagrams for each position.
See
For the first four positions (far beyond C, just beyond C, at C, between C and F) a sharp, upside-down flame appears on the screen at the positions in the table. At F no sharp image forms, however far you move the screen. When the candle is between P and F, no image forms on the screen at all — but looking into the mirror you see an enlarged, upright flame.
Shows
The size, position and nature of the image depend only on where the object is relative to P, F and C. Your ray diagrams predict exactly what the screen shows.

Uses of concave mirrors — and which image each one uses

Use Where the object (or bulb) is Which property is used
Torches, searchlights, vehicle headlights bulb at (very near) F rays from F reflect parallel — a strong, straight beam (ray 2)
Shaving or make-up mirror face between P and F (close to the mirror) enlarged, erect, virtual image of the face
Dentist's mirror tooth between P and F enlarged image of the tooth
Solar furnace / solar cooker dish Sun at infinity sunlight meets at F — heat concentrated at one spot

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Ray diagram: object between C and F

Question. A 2 cm tall object stands 10 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 6 cm. Draw the ray diagram and describe the image.

Given: concave mirror, f = 6 cm (so R = 12 cm); object height 2 cm at 10 cm from P. Asked: a ray diagram and the image's position, size and nature.

Concept used: the ray-diagram method with ray 1 (parallel → through F) and ray 3 (through C → back on itself). Why it applies: both rays have known paths, and the object (at 10 cm) lies between F (6 cm) and C (12 cm).

  1. 1

    Draw the principal axis and the mirror. Mark P on the mirror, F at 6 cm and C at 12 cm in front of it (scale: 1 cm on paper = 1 cm).

  2. 2

    Draw the object AB, 2 cm tall, standing on the axis at 10 cm — between F and C.

  3. 3

    Ray 1: from B, draw a line parallel to the axis to the mirror. After reflection, draw it through F and carry on past the axis. Add arrowheads.

  4. 4

    Ray 3: draw the straight line from C through B and continue it to the mirror. The ray from B travelling along this line strikes the mirror along the normal, so it reflects straight back along the same line — back through B, through C and onwards below the axis. Add arrowheads.

  5. 5

    The two reflected rays cross below the axis, beyond C. Mark this point B′.

  6. 6

    Draw A′B′ from the axis down to B′, as a solid arrow.

  7. 7

    Describe: A′B′ is beyond C, larger than AB and upside down.

  8. Check: the table says "object between C and F → image beyond C, enlarged, real and inverted" ✔. If you draw to scale, A′B′ comes out at about 15 cm from P and about 3 cm tall; you will learn to calculate these exact values in Topic 4.

  9. Answer Image beyond C (about 15 cm from the mirror); enlarged (about 3 cm); real and inverted.

    Common trap: Drawing ray 1 through a point "F" behind the mirror. For a concave mirror, F is in front, on the same side as the object.

Worked example 2

Ray diagram: object between P and F

Question. A 2 cm tall object is 5 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 10 cm. Draw the ray diagram and describe the image.

Given: concave mirror, f = 10 cm, R = 20 cm; object 2 cm tall at 5 cm from P. Asked: ray diagram and description.

Concept used: ray 1 (parallel → through F) and ray 3 (along the line from C → back on itself); when reflected rays spread out, extend them backwards as dashed lines. Why it applies: the object is inside F, so the reflected rays will diverge.

  1. 1

    Draw the axis and the mirror; mark P, F (10 cm) and C (20 cm).

  2. 2

    Draw AB, 2 cm tall, at 5 cm — between P and F.

  3. 3

    Ray 1: from B parallel to the axis; after reflection, through F. Arrowheads on both parts.

  4. 4

    Ray 3: the line from C through B, continued to the mirror. It reflects back along itself (towards C). Arrowheads.

  5. 5

    In front of the mirror the two reflected rays move apart — they will never meet there.

  6. 6

    Extend both reflected rays backwards behind the mirror as dashed lines. They meet above the axis, behind the mirror. Mark this point B′.

  7. 7

    Draw A′B′ (dashed) from the axis up to B′.

  8. Check: table row "between P and F → behind the mirror, enlarged, virtual and erect" ✔. To scale, A′B′ is about 10 cm behind the mirror and about 4 cm tall.

  9. Answer Image behind the mirror (about 10 cm behind); enlarged (about 4 cm); virtual and erect.

    Common trap: Drawing the extensions as solid lines with arrowheads. No light travels behind a mirror — extensions are dashed.

Worked example 3

Choosing the object position for a job

Question. Meera wants to use a concave mirror of focal length 30 cm (a) as a shaving/make-up mirror and (b) to throw a sharp, enlarged image of a candle flame onto a wall. Where should her face and the candle be placed in each case? Give a reason.

Given: concave mirror, f = 30 cm, so C is at R = 60 cm. Asked: suitable object positions for (a) and (b).

Concept used: the six-position table. Why it applies: each job needs a particular kind of image — so we read the table backwards, from image to object.

  1. 1

    (a) Shaving mirror

    She needs to see her face upright and bigger.

  2. 2

    The only position giving an enlarged, erect image is between P and F.

  3. 3

    So her face must be less than 30 cm from the mirror.

  4. 4

    (b) Enlarged flame on a wall

    An image on a wall must be real (caught on a screen).

  5. 5

    It must also be enlarged. Real and enlarged happens when the object is between C and F.

  6. 6

    So the candle should be between 30 cm and 60 cm from the mirror; the wall must be beyond C (more than 60 cm away).

  7. Check: in (a) the image is virtual — fine, because she looks into the mirror, not at a screen. In (b) the image is inverted, so the flame appears upside down on the wall — expected for any real image from a single mirror ✔.

  8. Answer (a) Face closer than 30 cm (between P and F): image enlarged, erect, virtual. (b) Candle between 30 cm and 60 cm (between F and C); wall beyond 60 cm: image real, inverted, enlarged.

    Common trap: Choosing "at C" for (b). At C the image is real but only the same size, not enlarged.

A ray passes through the centre of curvature C of a concave mirror before striking it. After reflection it:

Quick check · 3 of 3Short answer2 marks

(a) Where is the bulb placed in a torch reflector, and why? (b) Name the ray rule your answer uses.

Answer: (a) At (very near) the principal focus of the concave reflector. Light from the bulb that strikes the mirror reflects parallel to the principal axis, giving a strong, straight beam. (b) A ray passing through F reflects parallel to the principal axis (ray 2).

"At C" is a common slip — light from C would come straight back to the bulb, not go forward as a beam.

Common mistakes!

  • These are the ray-diagram errors that cost the most marks.

  • No arrowheads on rays

    The examiner cannot tell which way light travels — marks are deducted.

    Put an arrowhead on every ray, before and after the mirror.

  • Virtual rays drawn solid (or with arrowheads) behind the mirror

    No light goes behind a mirror; those lines only show where rays seem to come from.

    Real rays solid with arrows; extensions dashed, no arrows.

  • Ray "through F" drawn through a point on the wrong side

    For a concave mirror, F is in front of the mirror, on the object's side.

    Mark F before drawing any ray; check it is between P and C.

  • F not halfway between P and C

    Spoils the whole diagram — the image lands in the wrong place.

    Measure: PF = FC.

  • Only one ray drawn

    One ray cannot fix a point; you need two rays to meet.

    Always two rays from the top of the object.

  • Image described in one word ("real")

    Board answers expect position and size and nature.

    Write all three: "beyond C; enlarged; real and inverted".

  • "A concave mirror always magnifies"

    It magnifies only when the object is inside C; beyond C the image is smaller.

    Check the object's position against F and C first.

Key points

  • Four easy rays: parallel → through F; through F → parallel; through C → back on itself; to P → equal angles. Use any two.
  • Real rays are solid with arrowheads; backward extensions are dashed.
  • Object outside F → real, inverted. Object inside F → virtual, erect, enlarged. At C → same size.
  • As the object moves from infinity towards F, the real image moves out from F to infinity and grows.
  • Uses follow from the images: bulb at F (torch, headlight), face inside F (shaving, dentist), Sun at infinity (solar furnace).
