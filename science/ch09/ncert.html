Intext p.142, Q1Principal focus of a concave mirror1 mark

Define the principal focus of a concave mirror.

Given: a concave mirror. Asked: the meaning of its principal focus.

Concept used: a concave mirror converges light (Topic 1). Why it applies: the principal focus is defined by what happens to rays parallel to the principal axis.

  1. 1

    Let a beam of rays travel parallel to the principal axis towards the concave mirror.

  2. 2

    After reflection, all these rays converge and pass through one point on the principal axis.

  3. 3

    That point is the principal focus (F) of the concave mirror. It lies in front of the mirror, midway between P and C.

  4. Check: this matches Activity 9.2 — sunlight (parallel rays) is brought to a sharp bright spot in front of the mirror ✔.

  5. Answer The principal focus of a concave mirror is the point on its principal axis where rays of light, travelling parallel to the principal axis, meet after reflection from the mirror.

    Common trap: Leaving out "parallel to the principal axis". Rays from a nearby point do not meet at F.

Intext p.142, Q3Mirror giving an erect, enlarged image1 mark

Name a mirror that can give an erect and enlarged image of an object.

Given: the image must be erect and enlarged. Asked: the type of mirror.

Concept used: the image tables for concave and convex mirrors (Topic 2, Topic 3). Why it applies: we look for the mirror (and object position) that gives an erect, enlarged image.

  1. 1

    A plane mirror gives an erect image of the same size — not enlarged.

  2. 2

    A convex mirror gives an erect image that is always diminished.

  3. 3

    A concave mirror, with the object between P and F, gives a virtual, erect and enlarged image.

  4. Check: a shaving mirror (concave, face held close) shows a bigger, upright face ✔.

  5. Answer A concave mirror (with the object placed between its pole and principal focus).

    Common trap: Answering "convex mirror" because it gives an erect image. Its image is always smaller.
Intext p.142, Q4Why a convex rear-view mirror?2 marks

Why do we prefer a convex mirror as a rear-view mirror in vehicles?

Given: a rear-view mirror in a vehicle. Asked: why a convex mirror is preferred.

Concept used: a convex mirror always forms a virtual, erect, diminished image and has a wide field of view (Topic 3). Why it applies: a driver needs an upright view of as much of the road behind as possible.

  1. 1

    A convex mirror always forms an erect image, whatever the distance of the vehicles behind.

  2. 2

    Because it bulges outwards, it collects light from a much wider area behind the vehicle than a plane mirror of the same size — a wider field of view.

  3. 3

    Its image is diminished, which is why a wide scene fits into a small mirror.

  4. Check: both reasons follow from the convex-mirror rule (always erect, diminished) and its outward-curved shape ✔.

  5. Answer Because a convex mirror always gives an erect (though diminished) image, and being curved outwards it has a wider field of view, so the driver can see a much larger area of traffic behind the vehicle.

    Common trap: Saying it gives an "enlarged" or "real" image. It gives neither.

Intext p.150, Q1Air into water: which way does the ray bend?2 marks

A ray of light travelling in air enters obliquely into water. Does the light ray bend towards the normal or away from the normal? Why?

Given: a ray passes obliquely from air into water. Asked: direction of bending, with a reason.

Concept used: light bends towards the normal when it slows down, i.e. when it enters an optically denser medium (Topic 5). Why it applies: water (n=1.33n = 1.33n=1.33) is optically denser than air (n≈1n \approx 1n≈1).

  1. 1

    Compare refractive indices: water 1.33 > air 1.0003, so water is optically denser.

  2. 2

    Light travels more slowly in water than in air.

  3. 3

    When light slows down on entering a medium obliquely, it bends towards the normal.

  4. Check: the angle of refraction in water is smaller than the angle of incidence in air — consistent with Snell's law, since sin⁡isin⁡r=1.33>1\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} = 1.33 > 1sinrsini​=1.33>1 means sin⁡r<sin⁡i\sin r < \sin isinr<sini ✔.

  5. Answer It bends towards the normal, because water is optically denser than air: light slows down on entering water.

    Common trap: Saying "away from the normal because water is heavier". Bending depends on optical density (speed), not on weight.
