Topic 7 of 7

Bleaching powder, baking soda, washing soda, Plaster of Paris

About 45 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Give the formula, preparation (with equation) and uses of bleaching powder, baking soda, washing soda and Plaster of Paris.
  • Explain water of crystallisation using copper sulphate crystals and gypsum.
  • Explain everyday facts (why baking powder makes cake rise, why PoP is stored dry, how PoP sets).

A municipal tank smells faintly of chlorine. A sponge cake comes out of the oven twice the height of the batter. A dhobi adds a spoon of white crystals to hard water so the soap lathers. A doctor wraps a broken wrist in a white paste that turns rock-hard in minutes. Four everyday jobs. Three of these chemicals start life as common salt; the fourth, Plaster of Paris, comes from the mineral gypsum.

  1. Bleaching powder

How it is made. Chlorine from the chlor-alkali process (Topic 6) is passed over dry slaked lime, Ca(OH)X2\ce{Ca(OH)2}Ca(OH)X2​. Your textbook writes the reaction as:

2 Ca(OH)X2+2 ClX2→Ca(ClO)X2+CaClX2+2 HX2O\ce{2Ca(OH)2 + 2Cl2 -> Ca(ClO)2 + CaCl2 + 2H2O}2Ca(OH)X2​+2ClX2​​Ca(ClO)X2​+CaClX2​+2HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

Formula. The real composition of bleaching powder is complicated. Your textbook represents it as Ca(ClO)X2\ce{Ca(ClO)2}Ca(ClO)X2​ (calcium hypochlorite, its active part). It is also commonly written as CaOClX2\ce{CaOCl2}CaOClX2​ (calcium oxychloride). The two ways of writing it describe the same reaction — add the two calcium products together and divide by two:

Ca(OH)X2+ClX2→CaOClX2+HX2O\ce{Ca(OH)2 + Cl2 -> CaOCl2 + H2O}Ca(OH)X2​+ClX2​​CaOClX2​+HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

Learn the textbook equation for the exam; if a question uses either formula, it means bleaching powder.

Uses

  • bleaching cotton and linen cloth, wood pulp for paper, and clothes in laundries;
  • as an oxidising agent in chemical factories;
  • disinfecting drinking water (killing germs).

  1. Baking soda — sodium hydrogencarbonate, NaHCOX3\ce{NaHCO3}NaHCOX3​

How it is made. From sodium chloride, water, carbon dioxide and ammonia:

NaCl+HX2O+COX2+NHX3→NHX4Cl+NaHCOX3\ce{NaCl + H2O + CO2 + NH3 -> NH4Cl + NaHCO3}NaCl+HX2​O+COX2​+NHX3​​NHX4​Cl+NaHCOX3​Swipe to see the whole equation →

Baking soda is a mild, non-corrosive base — its solution is basic (Topic 6). On heating it decomposes:

2 NaHCOX3→heatNaX2COX3+HX2O+COX2\ce{2NaHCO3 ->[\text{heat}] Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2}2NaHCOX3​heat​NaX2​COX3​+HX2​O+COX2​Swipe to see the whole equation →

Uses

  • In the kitchen: for crisp pakoras and faster cooking.
  • Baking powder = baking soda + tartaric acid (a weak acid that is safe to eat). As soon as the powder gets wet or hot, the acid reacts with the baking soda:
sodium hydrogencarbonate+acid⟶sodium salt of the acid+COX2+HX2O\text{sodium hydrogencarbonate} + \text{acid} \longrightarrow \text{sodium salt of the acid} + \ce{CO2} + \ce{H2O}sodium hydrogencarbonate+acid⟶sodium salt of the acid+COX2​+HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

The carbon dioxide bubbles make bread and cake rise, so they turn soft and spongy. The tartaric acid also uses up the baking soda, so no bitter-tasting sodium carbonate is left behind.

  • As an antacid: being alkaline, it neutralises excess stomach acid.
  • In soda-acid fire extinguishers: acid mixes with a solution of baking soda, and the carbon dioxide formed pushes out a stream of liquid and gas that helps put out the fire.

  1. Washing soda — NaX2COX3 ⋅ 10 HX2O\ce{Na2CO3.10H2O}NaX2​COX3​⋅10HX2​O

How it is made. Heat baking soda to get sodium carbonate (equation above); then recrystallise sodium carbonate from water:

NaX2COX3+10 HX2O→NaX2COX3 ⋅ 10 HX2O\ce{Na2CO3 + 10H2O -> Na2CO3.10H2O}NaX2​COX3​+10HX2​O​NaX2​COX3​⋅10HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

Like baking soda, it is a basic salt.

Uses

  • in the glass, soap and paper industries;
  • to manufacture sodium compounds such as borax;
  • as a cleaning agent at home;
  • for removing permanent hardness of water.

  1. Water of crystallisation

Heating copper sulphate crystals (NCERT Activity 2.15)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Take a few blue crystals of copper sulphate in a boiling tube that is completely dry, and heat them. Then add 2–3 drops of water to the cooled powder.
See
The crystals turn white, and water droplets appear on the cooler upper part of the tube. On adding water, the blue colour returns.
Shows
The "dry" crystals contained water locked inside them. Heat drives it out; water puts it back.

Water of crystallisation is the definite number of water molecules that are part of each formula unit of a crystalline salt. Blue copper sulphate is CuSOX4 ⋅ 5 HX2O\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}CuSOX4​⋅5HX2​O (five molecules); washing soda is NaX2COX3 ⋅ 10 HX2O\ce{Na2CO3.10H2O}NaX2​COX3​⋅10HX2​O (ten); gypsum is CaSOX4 ⋅ 2 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅2HX2​O (two). These crystals are not wet — the water is part of their structure.

CuSOX4 ⋅ 5 HX2O→heatCuSOX4+5 HX2O\ce{CuSO4.5H2O ->[\text{heat}] CuSO4 + 5H2O}CuSOX4​⋅5HX2​Oheat​CuSOX4​+5HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

  1. Plaster of Paris — CaSOX4 ⋅ 12 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.1/2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅21​HX2​O

How it is made. Heat gypsum at 373 K (100 °C). It loses part of its water and becomes calcium sulphate hemihydrate — Plaster of Paris:

CaSOX4 ⋅ 2 HX2O→373 KCaSOX4 ⋅ 12 HX2O+1 12 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.2H2O ->[\text{373 K}] CaSO4.1/2H2O + 1 1/2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅2HX2​O373 K​CaSOX4​⋅21​HX2​O+1 21​HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

Half a water molecule? You cannot have half a molecule. The formula means that two formula units of CaSOX4\ce{CaSO4}CaSOX4​ share one water molecule.

How it sets. Plaster of Paris is sold as a fine white powder. Mixed with water, it turns back into gypsum — a hard solid mass:

CaSOX4 ⋅ 12 HX2O+1 12 HX2O→CaSOX4 ⋅ 2 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.1/2H2O + 1 1/2H2O -> CaSO4.2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅21​HX2​O+1 21​HX2​O​CaSOX4​⋅2HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

That is why it must be kept in an airtight, moisture-proof container: moisture from the air would slowly turn the powder into hard gypsum, and it would be useless.

Uses: plaster casts that hold broken bones still while they heal; making toys and materials for decoration; making surfaces smooth.

Did you know?Not in board exam

It is called Plaster of Paris because large gypsum deposits near Paris, in France, were once a major source of it.

Preparation equations for four chemicals

Given: four chemicals. Asked: a balanced preparation equation for each.

  1. (a) Bleaching powder — chlorine on dry slaked lime:

  2. 1

    2 Ca(OH)X2+2 ClX2→Ca(ClO)X2+CaClX2+2 HX2O\ce{2Ca(OH)2 + 2Cl2 -> Ca(ClO)2 + CaCl2 + 2H2O}2Ca(OH)X2​+2ClX2​​Ca(ClO)X2​+CaClX2​+2HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

    Count: Na 1 | 1; Cl 1 | 1; H 2 + 3 = 5 | 4 + 1 = 5; O 1 + 2 = 3 | 3; C 1 | 1; N 1 | 1 ✔.

Worked example 2

Which chemical, and why?

Question. Name the chemical you would use, and give the reason, for each job: (a) disinfecting the water of a village well; (b) making a dough of flour rise without yeast; (c) getting soap to lather in water that stays hard even after boiling; (d) making a cast of a footprint found at a crime scene; (e) making soap.

Given: five jobs. Asked: chemical + reason.

Concept used: each of the five chemicals has a property that suits particular uses. Why it applies: the reason must come from that property.

  1. 1

    (a) Bleaching powder — it kills germs (it is an oxidising agent).

  2. 2

    (b) Baking powder (baking soda + tartaric acid) — it gives carbon dioxide when wet or heated, and the gas makes the dough rise.

  3. 3

    (c) Water that stays hard after boiling has permanent hardness → washing soda removes it.

  4. 4

    (d) Plaster of Paris — the paste fills the shape and sets hard as it turns into gypsum.

  5. 5

    (e) Sodium hydroxide — the alkali used in making soaps and detergents.

  6. Check: each choice matches the "uses" lists in this topic and in Topic 6 ✔.

  7. Answer (a) bleaching powder (b) baking powder / baking soda (c) washing soda (d) Plaster of Paris (e) sodium hydroxide — reasons as above.

    Common trap: Choosing baking soda alone for (b) without saying where the acid comes from — baking powder contains its own acid; baking soda alone needs heat (and leaves a bitter taste).

Worked example 3

Blue crystals that lose their colour

Question. A student heats blue crystals of copper sulphate in a dry boiling tube. (a) State two observations. (b) Write the equation. (c) What will she see if she adds a few drops of water to the cooled residue? (d) How many water molecules of crystallisation are there in one formula unit of washing soda and of gypsum?

Given: heating CuSOX4 ⋅ 5 HX2O\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}CuSOX4​⋅5HX2​O. Asked: (a)–(d).

Concept used: water of crystallisation is driven out by heat and can be restored by water. Why it applies: blue copper sulphate is a hydrated salt.

  1. 1

    (a) Observation 1: the blue crystals turn white.

  2. 2

    (a) Observation 2: water droplets collect on the cooler upper part of the tube.

  3. 3

    (b) CuSOX4 ⋅ 5 HX2O→heatCuSOX4+5 HX2O\ce{CuSO4.5H2O ->[\text{heat}] CuSO4 + 5H2O}CuSOX4​⋅5HX2​Oheat​CuSOX4​+5HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

  4. 4

    (c) The white powder turns blue again (the water of crystallisation is restored).

  5. 5

    (d) Washing soda NaX2COX3 ⋅ 10 HX2O\ce{Na2CO3.10H2O}NaX2​COX3​⋅10HX2​O → 10; gypsum CaSOX4 ⋅ 2 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅2HX2​O → 2.

  6. Check: Cu 1 | 1; S 1 | 1; O 4 + 5 = 9 | 4 + 5 = 9; H 10 | 10 ✔.

  7. Answer (a) blue → white; droplets of water (b) equation above (c) turns blue again (d) 10 and 2.

    Common trap: Saying the crystals "melt" or "burn". The colour change is due to losing water, and it is reversed by adding water.

Common mistakes!

  • Swapping NaHCOX3\ce{NaHCO3}NaHCOX3​ (baking soda) and NaX2COX3 ⋅ 10 HX2O\ce{Na2CO3.10H2O}NaX2​COX3​⋅10HX2​O (washing soda)

    They are different salts.

    Baking soda has H (hydrogencarbonate); washing soda has water (10).

  • PoP = CaSOX4 ⋅ 2 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅2HX2​O

    That is gypsum.

    PoP CaSOX4 ⋅ 12 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.1/2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅21​HX2​O · gypsum CaSOX4 ⋅ 2 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅2HX2​O.

  • Bleaching powder = CaClX2\ce{CaCl2}CaClX2​ or "CaOCl\ce{CaOCl}CaOCl"

    Wrong formulae.

    CaOClX2\ce{CaOCl2}CaOClX2​ (textbook: Ca(ClO)X2\ce{Ca(ClO)2}Ca(ClO)X2​).

  • Chlorine + lime water → bleaching powder

    Needs dry slaked lime.

    "Chlorine on dry slaked lime."

  • Washing soda made by heating washing soda

    It is made by heating baking soda, then recrystallising.

    Baking soda → heat → NaX2COX3\ce{Na2CO3}NaX2​COX3​ → + 10 water.

  • "Crystals with water of crystallisation are wet."

    The water is locked inside the crystal.

    They look and feel dry.

  • "Plaster of Paris is made from common salt."

    It is made by heating gypsum at 373 K.

    From common salt: NaOH, bleaching powder, baking soda, washing soda. From gypsum: Plaster of Paris.

Key points

  • Bleaching powder CaOClX2\ce{CaOCl2}CaOClX2​ (textbook Ca(ClO)X2\ce{Ca(ClO)2}Ca(ClO)X2​): chlorine on dry slaked lime; bleaching, oxidising agent, germ-free water.
  • Baking soda NaHCOX3\ce{NaHCO3}NaHCOX3​: from NaCl, water, COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​, NHX3\ce{NH3}NHX3​; heating gives NaX2COX3+HX2O+COX2\ce{Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2}NaX2​COX3​+HX2​O+COX2​; baking powder, antacid, fire extinguisher.
  • Washing soda NaX2COX3 ⋅ 10 HX2O\ce{Na2CO3.10H2O}NaX2​COX3​⋅10HX2​O: heat baking soda, recrystallise; glass, soap, paper, borax, cleaning, permanent hardness.
  • Water of crystallisation = fixed number of water molecules per formula unit: CuSOX4 ⋅ 5 HX2O\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}CuSOX4​⋅5HX2​O (blue → white on heating), gypsum CaSOX4 ⋅ 2 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅2HX2​O.
  • Plaster of Paris CaSOX4 ⋅ 12 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.1/2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅21​HX2​O: gypsum heated at 373 K; sets to hard gypsum with water — store dry; casts, toys, decoration, smooth surfaces.
