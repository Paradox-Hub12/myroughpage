Topic 4 of 7

What all acids (and all bases) have in common

About 35 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain acids as substances giving H⁺(aq) / H₃O⁺ in water and bases as giving OH⁻(aq); alkalis as water-soluble bases.
  • Explain why acid solutions conduct electricity, and why glucose/alcohol solutions don't.
  • Explain why dry HCl gas doesn't change dry litmus.
  • Explain dilution, its effect on ion concentration, and the safe way to dilute an acid.

Sugar contains hydrogen. So does alcohol. So does hydrochloric acid. Yet sugar water is sweet and harmless, while hydrochloric acid turns blue litmus red and fizzes with zinc. If "having hydrogen" is not what makes an acid, what is?

Quick reminder: ions carry current through liquids

In Class 9 you met ions — atoms or groups of atoms with an electric charge. A liquid conducts electricity only if it contains ions that can move. A solution with no ions does not conduct, however much you dissolve in it.

The conductivity test (NCERT Activity 2.8)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Fix two iron nails through a cork in a small beaker. Connect the nails to a 6 V battery through a bulb and a switch. Pour in dilute HCl and switch on. Repeat with dilute sulphuric acid, then with glucose solution and alcohol solution.
See
The bulb glows with both acids. It does not glow with glucose or alcohol solution.
Shows
The acid solutions contain ions; the glucose and alcohol solutions do not, even though glucose and alcohol contain hydrogen.

Every acid solution turns out to contain the same positive ion — the hydrogen ion, HX+\ce{H+}HX+ — together with a different negative ion:

Acid Ions in water
Hydrochloric acid, HCl\ce{HCl}HCl HX+\ce{H+}HX+ and ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX−
Nitric acid, HNOX3\ce{HNO3}HNOX3​ HX+\ce{H+}HX+ and NOX3X−\ce{NO3-}NOX3​X−
Sulphuric acid, HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​ HX+\ce{H+}HX+ and SOX4X2−\ce{SO4^2-}SOX4​X2−
Acetic acid, CHX3COOH\ce{CH3COOH}CHX3​COOH HX+\ce{H+}HX+ and CHX3COOX−\ce{CH3COO-}CHX3​COOX−

So an acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions, HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq), in water, and these ions give it its acidic properties. Glucose and alcohol hold their hydrogen tightly and release no HX+\ce{H+}HX+, so they are not acids. Solutions of alkalis such as sodium hydroxide also light the bulb — they contain ions too.

Water is essential (NCERT Activity 2.9)

Note
(Teacher demonstration — concentrated acid and corrosive HCl gas.) What you do: Make hydrogen chloride gas by adding concentrated sulphuric acid to solid sodium chloride in a dry test tube. Test the gas first with dry blue litmus paper, then with moist blue litmus paper. (On a humid day, pass the gas through a guard tube containing calcium chloride, which absorbs moisture, so that the gas is truly dry.)
See
Dry litmus does not change. Moist litmus turns red.
Shows
Hydrogen chloride releases HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions only in the presence of water. Dry HCl gas is made of HCl molecules, not ions, so it shows no acidic behaviour.

In water, each HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ion joins a water molecule. A bare HX+\ce{H+}HX+ cannot exist on its own; it exists as the hydronium ion, HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+:

HCl+HX2O→HX3OX++ClX−\ce{HCl + H2O -> H3O+ + Cl-}HCl+HX2​O​HX3​OX++ClX−Swipe to see the whole equation →
HX++HX2O→HX3OX+\ce{H+ + H2O -> H3O+}HX++HX2​O​HX3​OX+Swipe to see the whole equation →

That is why we always write the hydrogen ion as HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) or HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+.

What bases have in common

Bases that dissolve in water release hydroxide ions, OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX−:

NaOH(s)→HX2ONaX+(aq)+OHX−(aq)\ce{NaOH(s) ->[H2O] Na+(aq) + OH-(aq)}NaOH(s)HX2​O​NaX+(aq)+OHX−(aq)Swipe to see the whole equation →
Mg(OH)X2(s)→HX2OMgX2+(aq)+2 OHX−(aq)\ce{Mg(OH)2(s) ->[H2O] Mg^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}Mg(OH)X2​(s)HX2​O​MgX2+(aq)+2OHX−(aq)Swipe to see the whole equation →

A base that dissolves in water is called an alkali. Sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide and calcium hydroxide are alkalis. Many bases — copper oxide, for example — do not dissolve in water, so every alkali is a base, but not every base is an alkali. Alkalis feel soapy, taste bitter and are corrosive: never touch or taste them.

Neutralisation, seen as ions

Since all acids give HX+\ce{H+}HX+ and all alkalis give OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX−, every neutralisation is really the same tiny reaction:

HX+(aq)+OHX−(aq)→HX2O(l)\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}HX+(aq)+OHX−(aq)​HX2​O(l)Swipe to see the whole equation →

The other two ions (for example NaX+\ce{Na+}NaX+ and ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX−) stay behind and form the salt.

Mixing acids and bases with water: heat and safety (NCERT Activity 2.10)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Note
(Teacher demonstration — concentrated acid and sodium hydroxide pellets burn the skin; wear goggles.) What you do: Add a few drops of concentrated sulphuric acid to 10 mL of water in a beaker; swirl; touch the bottom of the beaker. Repeat with a few pellets of sodium hydroxide.
See
The beaker becomes hot both times.
Shows
Dissolving a concentrated acid or a base in water is highly exothermic — it gives out a lot of heat.
Safety

Always add the acid to water — slowly, with constant stirring — and never water to acid. If water is poured into concentrated acid, the first drops meet a large amount of acid; so much heat is produced in one spot that the mixture can boil and splash out, causing burns, and the glass can crack from the sudden local heating. When acid is added slowly to a large amount of water, the heat spreads through all that water. Containers of concentrated acids and of sodium hydroxide pellets carry a corrosive warning sign for this reason.

Dilution

Mixing an acid or a base with water is called dilution. The number of HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ (or OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX−) ions stays about the same, but they are spread through a larger volume, so the concentration of ions per unit volume decreases. A diluted acid is less acidic, not more.

Worked example 1

Why does vinegar solution conduct but sugar solution does not?

Question. In the conductivity set-up, a bulb glows faintly with dilute vinegar but does not glow at all with sugar solution. Explain.

Given: vinegar solution → bulb glows (faintly); sugar solution → no glow. Asked: the reason.

Concept used: a solution conducts only if it contains ions; acids produce HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions in water. Why it applies: vinegar contains acetic acid; sugar contains hydrogen but is not an acid.

  1. 1

    The bulb glows only when current flows through the solution.

  2. 2

    Current in a solution is carried by moving ions.

  3. 3

    Vinegar contains acetic acid, which gives HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) and CHX3COOX−(aq)\ce{CH3COO-(aq)}CHX3​COOX−(aq) ions in water.

  4. 4

    These ions carry the current, so the bulb glows.

  5. 5

    Sugar dissolves as whole molecules; it does not release HX+\ce{H+}HX+ or any other ions.

  6. 6

    With no ions, no current flows, so the bulb stays dark.

  7. Check: this matches the textbook result: acids (HCl, HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​) glow, glucose and alcohol do not ✔. (The faint glow fits acetic acid being a weak acid — Topic 5.)

  8. Answer Acetic acid in vinegar produces ions (HX+\ce{H+}HX+ and CHX3COOX−\ce{CH3COO-}CHX3​COOX−) in water, which carry current; sugar produces no ions, so its solution does not conduct.

    Common trap: "Sugar has no hydrogen." It does (CX12HX22OX11\ce{C12H22O11}CX12​HX22​OX11​) — the point is that it does not release hydrogen ions.

Worked example 2

Dry ammonia and dry litmus

Question. Dry ammonia gas does not change dry red litmus paper, but it turns moist red litmus paper blue. Explain, using an equation.

Given: dry NHX3\ce{NH3}NHX3​ → no change; moist litmus → blue. Asked: the reason, with an equation.

Concept used: bases show basic behaviour by producing OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions, and ions form only in water. Why it applies: this is the base version of the dry-HCl experiment.

  1. 1

    Red litmus turns blue only when OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions are present.

  2. 2

    Dry ammonia gas is made of NHX3\ce{NH3}NHX3​ molecules — no ions — so dry litmus does not change.

  3. 3

    On moist litmus, ammonia dissolves in the water and reacts with it:

    NHX3+HX2O→NHX4X++OHX−\ce{NH3 + H2O -> NH4+ + OH-}NHX3​+HX2​O​NHX4​X++OHX−Swipe to see the whole equation →
  4. 4

    The OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions formed turn the red litmus blue.

  5. Check: atoms N 1 | 1, H 5 | 5, O 1 | 1; charge 0 | (+1) + (−1) = 0 ✔. The reasoning mirrors dry HCl ✔.

  6. Answer Ammonia produces OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions only in water (NHX3+HX2O→NHX4X++OHX−\ce{NH3 + H2O -> NH4+ + OH-}NHX3​+HX2​O​NHX4​X++OHX−); dry gas has no ions, so dry litmus does not change.

    Common trap: Saying "dry ammonia is not a base". It is a base; it simply cannot show it without water.

Worked example 3

Comparing two dilutions

Question. Beaker X: 10 mL of an acid + 10 mL of water. Beaker Y: 10 mL of the same acid + 90 mL of water. (a) Which has the greater concentration of HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ ions? (b) Which is less acidic? (c) Describe how the acid should have been mixed with the water.

Given: same amount of acid in 20 mL (X) and 100 mL (Y) of solution. Asked: (a), (b), (c).

Concept used: dilution spreads the same ions through a larger volume, lowering the concentration per unit volume. Why it applies: X and Y contain the same acid but different total volumes.

  1. 1

    Both beakers contain the same 10 mL of acid, so about the same number of HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ ions.

  2. 2

    In X, the ions are spread through 20 mL; in Y, through 100 mL — five times more volume.

  3. 3

    So each mL of X holds about five times as many HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ ions as each mL of Y → X has the greater concentration.

  4. 4

    Fewer HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ ions per mL means less acidic → Y is less acidic.

  5. 5

    Mixing: pour the acid slowly into the water, stirring all the time, never the other way round.

  6. Check: 100 mL ÷ 20 mL = 5, so Y is about five times more dilute ✔; dilution lowers acidity, as expected.

  7. Answer (a) X (b) Y (c) Add the acid slowly to the water with constant stirring.

    Common trap: Thinking "more water = more acid" or that dilution removes the acid. The ions are still there — just more spread out.

Common mistakes!

  • "Anything with hydrogen is an acid."

    Glucose and alcohol contain hydrogen but release no HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions.

    Acid = gives HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) in water.

  • "Dry HCl gas is acidic."

    Without water there are no ions.

    Acidic behaviour needs water.

  • "All bases are alkalis."

    Only bases that dissolve in water are alkalis.

    Every alkali is a base; not every base is an alkali.

  • "Add water to the acid."

    It can splash and crack the glass.

    Acid into water, slowly, stirring.

  • "Dilution makes an acid more acidic."

    Ions are spread out; concentration falls.

    Dilution makes an acid less acidic.

Key points

  • Acids give HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) (written HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+) ions in water; bases give OHX−(aq)\ce{OH-(aq)}OHX−(aq) ions.
  • Acid and alkali solutions conduct because they contain ions; glucose and alcohol solutions do not.
  • Water is needed for ions to form: dry HCl gas does not change dry litmus.
  • An alkali is a water-soluble base. Neutralisation: HX+(aq)+OHX−(aq)→HX2O(l)\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}HX+(aq)+OHX−(aq)​HX2​O(l).
  • Dissolving acids/bases in water is highly exothermic: add acid to water, slowly, with stirring. Dilution lowers the ion concentration.
