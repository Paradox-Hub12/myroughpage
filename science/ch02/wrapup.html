Wrap-up

Identify the unknown

About 30 minutes · 5 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Write equations from the six reaction patterns.
  • Use the hydrogen and carbon dioxide tests to name a gas.
  • Use the pH scale to compare solutions.
  • Predict the nature of a salt solution from its parents.
  • Recognise the five named chemicals from their properties and uses.

Board questions in this chapter love a mystery: "A compound X reacts with … to give a gas that … Identify X, the gas and write the equation." You already have every tool you need. This page puts them on three cards and gives you a clue table, then works through six mysteries.

Card 1 — Six reaction patterns

Pattern Word template Gas?
1 acid + metal → salt + hydrogen HX2\ce{H2}HX2​ — pop
2 sodium hydroxide + zinc/aluminium → salt + hydrogen HX2\ce{H2}HX2​ — pop
3 acid + carbonate / hydrogencarbonate → salt + carbon dioxide + water COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ — lime water milky
4 acid + base → salt + water (neutralisation) none
5 acid + metal oxide → salt + water none
6 base + non-metal oxide → salt + water none

Card 2 — The pH ruler

0 ← more acidic · · · 7 neutral · · · more basic → 14. Smaller number = more acidic. Dilution moves pH towards 7. Strong acid + weak base → acidic salt; weak acid + strong base → basic salt; strong + strong → neutral salt.

Name Formula One-line identity
Sodium hydroxide NaOH\ce{NaOH}NaOH made near the cathode when brine is electrolysed
Bleaching powder CaOClX2\ce{CaOCl2}CaOClX2​ (textbook Ca(ClO)X2\ce{Ca(ClO)2}Ca(ClO)X2​) chlorine on dry slaked lime; smells of chlorine; disinfects

Six mixed worked examples

Mixed example 1

A carbonate in disguise

Question. A white solid A reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid with brisk effervescence. The gas puts out a burning candle and turns lime water milky. One product is magnesium chloride. Identify A and write the balanced equation.

Given: effervescence; gas puts out a flame and turns lime water milky; product MgClX2\ce{MgCl2}MgClX2​. Asked: A and the equation.

Concept used: the clue table and Pattern 3 (acid + carbonate → salt + COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ + water). Why it applies: the gas test points to carbon dioxide.

  1. 1

    A gas that turns lime water milky and puts out a flame is carbon dioxide.

  2. 2

    Carbon dioxide with an acid → A is a carbonate (or hydrogencarbonate).

  3. 3

    The salt is magnesium chloride → A contains magnesium.

  4. 4

    A solid magnesium compound that gives COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ → A = magnesium carbonate, MgCOX3\ce{MgCO3}MgCOX3​.

  5. 5

    Equation:

    MgCOX3(s)+2 HCl(aq)→MgClX2(aq)+HX2O(l)+COX2(g)\ce{MgCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}MgCOX3​(s)+2HCl(aq)​MgClX2​(aq)+HX2​O(l)+COX2​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →

  6. Check: Mg 1 | 1; C 1 | 1; O 3 | 1 + 2 = 3; H 2 | 2; Cl 2 | 2 ✔.

  7. Answer A = magnesium carbonate; MgCOX3+2 HCl→MgClX2+HX2O+COX2\ce{MgCO3 + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2O + CO2}MgCOX3​+2HCl​MgClX2​+HX2​O+COX2​

    Common trap: Writing hydrogen as the gas. A gas that puts out a flame is carbon dioxide; hydrogen burns.

Mixed example 2

The kitchen powder

Question. A white powder P is used in fire extinguishers. Its solution turns red litmus blue. With vinegar it gives a gas that turns lime water milky. On strong heating, P leaves a white solid Q. Identify P and Q and give the heating equation.

Given: fire-extinguisher use; basic solution; COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ with acid; decomposes on heating. Asked: P, Q, equation.

Concept used: the five-chemicals card and the clue table. Why it applies: the uses and tests match one chemical.

  1. 1

    A basic salt that gives COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ with an acid and is used in soda-acid fire extinguishers → P = baking soda, NaHCOX3\ce{NaHCO3}NaHCOX3​.

  2. 2

    Baking soda on heating gives sodium carbonate, water and carbon dioxide → Q = sodium carbonate, NaX2COX3\ce{Na2CO3}NaX2​COX3​.

    2 NaHCOX3(s)→heatNaX2COX3(s)+HX2O(g)+COX2(g)\ce{2NaHCO3(s) ->[\text{heat}] Na2CO3(s) + H2O(g) + CO2(g)}2NaHCOX3​(s)heat​NaX2​COX3​(s)+HX2​O(g)+COX2​(g)Swipe to see the whole equation →

  3. Check: Na 2 | 2; H 2 | 2; C 2 | 2; O 6 | 3 + 1 + 2 = 6 ✔.

  4. Answer P = sodium hydrogencarbonate (baking soda); Q = sodium carbonate; equation above.

    Common trap: Naming Q as washing soda. Washing soda is the crystal NaX2COX3 ⋅ 10 HX2O\ce{Na2CO3.10H2O}NaX2​COX3​⋅10HX2​O, made only after recrystallising Q from water.

Mixed example 3

Three tubes, three gases

Question. Tube 1: zinc + dilute sulphuric acid. Tube 2: marble chips + dilute hydrochloric acid. Tube 3: gas collected at the anode while brine is electrolysed. Name each gas and give one test or property for each.

Given: three sources of gas. Asked: gas + test/property.

Concept used: Patterns 1 and 3 and the chlor-alkali process. Why it applies: each tube is one of these.

  1. 1

    Tube 1: acid + metal (Pattern 1) → hydrogen — burns with a pop.

  2. 2

    Tube 2: marble is calcium carbonate; acid + carbonate (Pattern 3) → carbon dioxide — turns lime water milky.

  3. 3

    Tube 3: the anode in brine electrolysis → chlorine — a gas with a sharp smell that bleaches moist coloured paper (used to make bleaching powder).

  4. Check: the three gases come from three different patterns, so no two tubes should give the same gas ✔.

  5. Answer 1 hydrogen (pop) · 2 carbon dioxide (lime water milky) · 3 chlorine (bleaches; made into bleaching powder).

    Common trap: Saying tube 3 gives hydrogen. Hydrogen comes from the cathode.

Mixed example 4

Matching three salt solutions

Question. Three bottles hold solutions of ammonium nitrate, sodium nitrate and potassium carbonate (labels lost). Bottle X turns red litmus blue; bottle Y turns blue litmus red; bottle Z changes neither. Match the bottles.

Given: three salts and their litmus results. Asked: which salt is in X, Y, Z.

Concept used: the strong parent decides (Topic 6). Why it applies: each salt has a different combination of strong and weak parents.

  1. 1

    Ammonium nitrate: NHX4OH\ce{NH4OH}NHX4​OH (weak) + HNOX3\ce{HNO3}HNOX3​ (strong) → acidic.

  2. 2

    Sodium nitrate: NaOH (strong) + HNOX3\ce{HNO3}HNOX3​ (strong) → neutral.

  3. 3

    Potassium carbonate: KOH (strong) + HX2COX3\ce{H2CO3}HX2​COX3​ (weak) → basic.

  4. 4

    X is basic → potassium carbonate; Y is acidic → ammonium nitrate; Z is neutral → sodium nitrate.

  5. Check: one acidic, one basic, one neutral salt — exactly matching one result each ✔.

  6. Answer X = potassium carbonate, Y = ammonium nitrate, Z = sodium nitrate.

    Common trap: Expecting every salt to be neutral, which leaves X and Y unexplained.

Mixed example 5

An oxide that behaves like an acid

Question. A colourless gas G with a choking smell is formed when sulphur burns in air. G turns moist blue litmus red, and with lime water it forms a white solid and water. Identify G, say whether it is an acidic or basic oxide, and write the equation with lime water.

Given: G from burning sulphur; acidic to litmus; reacts with lime water. Asked: G, its nature, equation.

Concept used: Pattern 6 — base + non-metal oxide → salt + water. Why it applies: G is an oxide of a non-metal that reacts with a base.

  1. 1

    Sulphur burning in air → sulphur dioxide, SOX2\ce{SO2}SOX2​.

  2. 2

    It turns moist blue litmus red → it forms an acid with water → acidic oxide.

  3. 3

    With calcium hydroxide (a base) it gives the salt calcium sulphite and water:

    Ca(OH)X2(aq)+SOX2(g)→CaSOX3(s)+HX2O(l)\ce{Ca(OH)2(aq) + SO2(g) -> CaSO3(s) + H2O(l)}Ca(OH)X2​(aq)+SOX2​(g)​CaSOX3​(s)+HX2​O(l)Swipe to see the whole equation →

  4. Check: Ca 1 | 1; O 2 + 2 = 4 | 3 + 1 = 4; H 2 | 2; S 1 | 1 ✔.

  5. Answer G = sulphur dioxide, an acidic (non-metal) oxide; equation above.

    Common trap: Concluding that G must be carbon dioxide because "lime water went milky". Sulphur dioxide also reacts with lime water — read every clue (burning sulphur).

Mixed example 6

Two white powders

Question. Two white powders R and S are both calcium sulphates. R is a natural mineral; S is made by heating R at 373 K. When S is mixed with water, it sets into a hard mass of R. Identify R and S with formulae, and write the setting equation.

Given: R → (373 K) S; S + water → R (hard). Asked: R, S, equation.

Concept used: water of crystallisation; gypsum and Plaster of Paris. Why it applies: heating at 373 K and setting with water are the identity marks of PoP.

  1. 1

    The calcium sulphate mineral that gives PoP on heating at 373 K is gypsum → R = CaSOX4 ⋅ 2 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅2HX2​O.

  2. 2

    S = Plaster of Paris, CaSOX4 ⋅ 12 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.1/2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅21​HX2​O.

  3. 3

    Setting:

    CaSOX4 ⋅ 12 HX2O+1 12 HX2O→CaSOX4 ⋅ 2 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.1/2H2O + 1 1/2H2O -> CaSO4.2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅21​HX2​O+1 21​HX2​O​CaSOX4​⋅2HX2​OSwipe to see the whole equation →

  4. Check: water: ½ + 1½ = 2 ✔; all other atoms unchanged ✔.

  5. Answer R = gypsum, CaSOX4 ⋅ 2 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅2HX2​O; S = Plaster of Paris, CaSOX4 ⋅ 12 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.1/2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅21​HX2​O; equation above.

    Common trap: Swapping the formulae — the one with more water (2) is gypsum.
