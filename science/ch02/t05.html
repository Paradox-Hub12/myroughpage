Topic 5 of 7

How strong? The pH scale

About 45 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Use universal indicator and the pH scale (0–14) to compare acidity and basicity.
  • Distinguish strong/weak from concentrated/dilute.
  • Predict how pH changes on dilution, neutralisation or adding excess acid/base.
  • Explain the importance of pH in everyday life — digestion and antacids, tooth decay, soil treatment, plants and animals, stings, acid rain.

Lemon juice and the acid in a car battery are both acids. You squeeze one onto your food; the other can burn a hole in your clothes. Litmus turns red in both, so litmus cannot tell them apart. We need a number that says how acidic — or how basic — a solution is.

Universal indicator

Universal indicator is a mixture of several indicators. Instead of just two colours, it shows a range of colours, one for each level of acidity or basicity. It is sold as a solution and as pH paper (paper soaked in universal indicator), with a colour chart on the box.

The pH scale

The pH scale is a number scale, usually from 0 to 14, that tells you how acidic or basic a solution is. ("p" comes from potenz, the German word for power.)

pH Nature Typical universal-indicator colour
0–2 strongly acidic red
3–4 acidic orange
5–6 weakly acidic yellow
7 neutral green
8–10 weakly to moderately basic blue
11–14 strongly basic (alkaline) violet / purple

(Exact shades differ a little between brands — always compare with the chart that comes with the paper.)

Careful with two similar phrases

Strongly acidic / weakly acidic describe a solution — how far its pH is below 7. Strong acid / weak acid describe the acid itself — how readily it gives HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions. They are different: one drop of hydrochloric acid (a strong acid) in a bucket of water gives only a weakly acidic solution, while a concentrated solution of acetic acid (a weak acid) is strongly acidic, with a pH between 2 and 3. In comparison questions (like NCERT Exercise Q9), the acidic solution farther from 7 is called strongly acidic and the one nearer 7 weakly acidic; the same goes for alkaline solutions.

Three rules carry almost every pH question:

  1. pH 7 is neutral. Pure water has pH 7.
  2. Below 7 is acidic — and the smaller the number, the more acidic. More HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ ions → lower pH.
  3. Above 7 is basic — and the bigger the number, the more basic. More OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions → higher pH.

pH 0 and pH 14 are perfectly possible: very concentrated hydrochloric acid can have pH about 0, and sodium hydroxide solution about 14.

Beyond the syllabus

pH is defined using logarithms, so each step of 1 on the scale means ten times more (or fewer) hydrogen ions. You do not need this definition for the board exam — just think of pH as a number that tells you how acidic or basic a solution is.

Strong and weak; concentrated and dilute — two different ideas

Strength is about how many HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions an acid releases from the amount dissolved.

  • A strong acid releases a large number of HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions — hydrochloric, sulphuric and nitric acids.
  • A weak acid releases far fewer — acetic acid, citric acid, carbonic acid.
  • Compare equal concentrations of hydrochloric acid and acetic acid: hydrochloric acid gives many more HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions, so its pH is lower.
  • Bases are the same: sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide are strong bases (many OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions); ammonium hydroxide is a weak base.

Concentration is about how much acid is dissolved in a given volume of water.

  • A concentrated acid has a lot of acid in little water; a dilute acid has a little acid in a lot of water.

So "strong" does not mean "concentrated", and "weak" does not mean "dilute". Vinegar is a dilute solution of a weak acid; a few drops of hydrochloric acid in a bucket of water make a dilute solution of a strong acid.

Predicting how pH changes

What you do What happens to pH
Add water to an acid pH rises towards 7 — but never goes above 7
Add water to an alkali pH falls towards 7 — but never goes below 7
Add a base, little by little, to an acid pH rises; reaches 7 when the acid is exactly neutralised (strong acid + strong base)
Keep adding base after that pH goes above 7
Add more acid to an acid pH falls

Why pH matters in everyday life

  • Our body works within a narrow pH range, about 7.0 to 7.8. Living things survive only small changes of pH.
  • Acid rain. Rain with pH below 5.6 is called acid rain. When it flows into rivers, it lowers their pH and makes it hard for aquatic life to survive.
  • Soil. Each plant grows best in a particular pH range. You can test soil by shaking it with water, filtering, and testing the filtrate with pH paper (NCERT Activity 2.12). If soil is too acidic, farmers add a base or a carbonate — quicklime (calcium oxide), slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) or chalk (calcium carbonate).
  • Digestion. The stomach makes hydrochloric acid, which helps digestion. In indigestion it makes too much, causing pain and irritation. Antacids — mild bases such as magnesium hydroxide (milk of magnesia) — neutralise the extra acid.
  • Tooth decay begins when the pH in the mouth falls below 5.5. Bacteria break down sugar and food bits left in the mouth and make acids. Tooth enamel, made of calcium hydroxyapatite (a crystalline calcium phosphate), is harder than any other part of the body and is insoluble in water — yet acid slowly eats it away. Cleaning the mouth after eating helps; toothpastes are generally basic, so they neutralise the acid.
  • Stings. A honey-bee sting leaves an acid; a mild base such as baking soda rubbed on it gives relief. Nettle leaves have stinging hairs that inject methanoic acid.

Virtual lab — CBSE Practical 1(A): finding pH with pH paper

What you do:

  1. Place small strips of pH paper on a clean white tile.
  2. Dip a clean glass rod into the first solution and touch it to a strip. (Do not dip the paper into the bottle — you would contaminate the solution.)
  3. Compare the colour with the chart at once and write down the pH.
  4. Wash and dry the rod before the next solution.

What you observe (typical results):

Solution Colour of pH paper Approximate pH Nature
Dilute hydrochloric acid red 1 strongly acidic
Lemon juice red / orange-red 2 acidic
Dilute ethanoic (acetic) acid orange 3 acidic (weak acid)
Water (distilled) green 7 neutral
Dilute sodium hydrogencarbonate blue-green / blue 8 weakly basic
Dilute sodium hydroxide violet / dark blue 13 strongly basic

What it shows: The order of increasing pH is HCl < lemon juice < ethanoic acid < water < sodium hydrogencarbonate < sodium hydroxide. Ethanoic acid has a higher pH than hydrochloric acid because it is a weak acid.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Ranking five solutions

Question. Five solutions give these colours with universal indicator: P red, Q green, R blue, S orange, T violet. (a) Arrange them in increasing order of pH. (b) Which has the highest concentration of hydrogen ions? (c) Which two are acidic?

Given: five universal-indicator colours. Asked: order of pH; highest HX+\ce{H+}HX+; the acidic ones.

Concept used: the colour–pH table and the rule "more HX+\ce{H+}HX+ → lower pH". Why it applies: each colour stands for a range of pH.

  1. 1

    Read off approximate pH: P red → 0–2; S orange → 3–4; Q green → 7; R blue → 8–10; T violet → 11–14.

  2. 2

    Increasing pH: P < S < Q < R < T.

  3. 3

    Highest HX+\ce{H+}HX+ concentration = lowest pH → P.

  4. 4

    Acidic = pH below 7 → P and S.

  5. Check: P (the most acidic) is first in the order and also has the most HX+\ce{H+}HX+ — consistent ✔. Q, at pH 7, is neutral, so it correctly sits between the acids and the bases ✔.

  6. Answer (a) P < S < Q < R < T (b) P (c) P and S

    Common trap: Picking T for "most hydrogen ions" because it has the biggest number. The biggest pH has the fewest hydrogen ions.

Worked example 2

Predicting pH in two everyday situations

Question. Say whether the pH rises or falls, and why: (a) a person with acidity takes a spoonful of milk of magnesia; (b) a child eats toffees and does not rinse her mouth for an hour.

Given: two situations. Asked: direction of pH change and the reason.

Concept used: adding a base to an acid raises pH; acids formed from sugar lower pH. Why it applies: (a) adds a base to stomach acid; (b) makes acid in the mouth.

  1. 1

    (a)

    Stomach contents are acidic (low pH) because of extra hydrochloric acid.

  2. 2

    Milk of magnesia is magnesium hydroxide, a mild base.

  3. 3

    It neutralises some of the acid: Mg(OH)X2+2 HCl→MgClX2+2 HX2O\ce{Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + 2H2O}Mg(OH)X2​+2HCl​MgClX2​+2HX2​O.

  4. 4

    Fewer HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions remain → the pH rises (towards 7), and the pain eases.

  5. 5

    (b)

    Bacteria in the mouth break down the sugar left on the teeth.

  6. 6

    They produce acids → more HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions → the pH falls.

  7. 7

    If it falls below 5.5, tooth enamel begins to corrode — the start of tooth decay.

  8. Check: (a) base added → pH up; (b) acid made → pH down. Both fit the table "what happens to pH" ✔. The equation in (a): Mg 1 | 1, O 2 | 2, H 4 | 4, Cl 2 | 2 ✔.

  9. Answer (a) pH rises — the antacid (a base) neutralises excess acid. (b) pH falls — bacteria make acids from sugar; below 5.5 the enamel is attacked.

    Common trap: Calling an antacid an acid because of its name. Ant-acid means "against acid" — it is a base.

Worked example 3

Strong or weak? Concentrated or dilute?

Question. Describe each as strong or weak and concentrated or dilute: (i) pure (glacial) acetic acid, (ii) table vinegar (about 5% acetic acid in water), (iii) 98% sulphuric acid, (iv) one drop of hydrochloric acid in a bucket of water.

Given: four acids. Asked: two labels for each.

Concept used: strength depends on which acid it is (how readily it releases HX+\ce{H+}HX+); concentration depends on how much water is present. Why it applies: each sample needs both labels, judged separately.

  1. 1

    Acetic acid is a weak acid. Pure acetic acid has almost no water → (i) weak, concentrated.

  2. 2

    Vinegar is still acetic acid, but mostly water → (ii) weak, dilute.

  3. 3

    Sulphuric acid is a strong acid; 98% means almost no water → (iii) strong, concentrated.

  4. 4

    Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid; one drop in a bucket is mostly water → (iv) strong, dilute.

  5. Check: both acetic acid samples are "weak" whatever their water content, and both mineral acids are "strong" — strength did not change with dilution ✔.

  6. Answer (i) weak, concentrated (ii) weak, dilute (iii) strong, concentrated (iv) strong, dilute.

    Common trap: Calling vinegar "weak because it is dilute" or the bucket of HCl "weak". Diluting changes the concentration, not the strength.

Common mistakes!

  • "Higher pH = more acidic."

    More HX+\ce{H+}HX+ gives a lower pH.

    Below 7: the smaller the number, the more acidic.

  • "pH 0 and pH 14 are impossible."

    Very strong, concentrated acids and alkalis reach them.

    The usual scale runs from 0 to 14.

  • "Strong = concentrated, weak = dilute."

    Strength is about the acid itself; concentration is about the water.

    Give two separate labels.

  • "A weak acid always gives a weakly acidic solution."

    "Weakly acidic" describes the pH of a solution; "weak acid" describes the acid. Concentration matters too.

    Ask two questions: which acid? (strong/weak) and how much water? (concentrated/dilute).

  • "Adding water to an acid can make its pH 8."

    Water alone only moves pH towards 7.

    Dilution: acid pH rises to 7, never past it.

  • "Antacids are acids."

    They neutralise acid — they are mild bases.

    Milk of magnesia = Mg(OH)X2\ce{Mg(OH)2}Mg(OH)X2​, a base.

  • "Toothpaste is acidic."

    Most toothpastes are basic, to neutralise mouth acids.

    Basic toothpaste fights tooth decay.

Key points

  • Universal indicator shows a range of colours; the pH scale (0–14) tells how acidic or basic a solution is.
  • pH 7 neutral · below 7 acidic (smaller = more acidic) · above 7 basic (bigger = more basic).
  • Strong/weak = how many HX+\ce{H+}HX+ (or OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX−) ions are released; concentrated/dilute = how much acid or base per volume of water.
  • Dilution moves pH towards 7; neutralising a strong acid with a strong base gives pH 7.
  • pH matters: body 7.0–7.8 · acid rain below 5.6 · tooth decay below 5.5 · antacids (bases) for indigestion · lime for acidic soil · baking soda for bee stings.
