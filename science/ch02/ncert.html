Intext p.18, Q1Three test tubes and only red litmus3 marks

You have been provided with three test tubes. One of them contains distilled water and the other two contain an acidic solution and a basic solution, respectively. If you are given only red litmus paper, how will you identify the contents of each test tube?

Given: three unlabelled tubes (water, acid, base) and red litmus paper only. Asked: how to identify each.

Concept used: red litmus turns blue in a base and does not change in an acid or in water; blue litmus turns red in an acid (Topic 2). Why it applies: once red litmus has turned blue, we have made our own blue litmus to test for the acid.

  1. 1

    Dip a strip of red litmus into a drop of each solution.

  2. 2

    The solution that turns red litmus blue is the basic solution.

  3. 3

    The other two leave red litmus red, so they are the acid and the water. We cannot yet tell them apart.

  4. 4

    Take the strip that has just turned blue (it now behaves like blue litmus).

  5. 5

    Touch it to a drop of each of the two remaining solutions.

  6. 6

    The solution that turns it back to red is the acidic solution.

  7. 7

    The solution that causes no change is distilled water.

  8. Check: each tube has now given a different result: blue (base), turns blue strip red (acid), no change (water) ✔.

  9. Answer Red litmus turning blue identifies the base. Then use that blued litmus on the other two: the one that turns it red is the acid; the one with no effect is distilled water.

    Common trap: Saying "the one that doesn't change red litmus is the acid". Both the acid and water leave red litmus unchanged — a second test is needed.

Intext p.22, Q1Curd in brass and copper vessels2 marks

Why should curd and sour substances not be kept in brass and copper vessels?

Given: curd and sour foods; brass and copper vessels. Asked: why they should not be stored together.

Concept used: sour substances contain acids, and acids react with metals to form salts (Topic 3). Why it applies: brass is an alloy of copper and zinc, and both metals are attacked by acids over time.

  1. 1

    Curd contains lactic acid; other sour foods (tamarind, lemon, tomato) also contain acids.

  2. 2

    Kept for a long time, these acids react with the metal of the vessel. Zinc in brass reacts directly with acids; copper is slowly attacked by acids in the presence of air.

  3. 3

    The reaction forms copper and zinc salts, which dissolve in the food.

  4. 4

    These salts are harmful (poisonous) to eat, and they spoil the taste of the food.

  5. Check: this is why such vessels used to be coated with tin (kalai) before storing sour food — the tin layer stops the acid touching the copper ✔.

  6. Answer Curd and sour substances contain acids, which react with copper and brass to form harmful (poisonous) compounds that get into the food and spoil it.

    Common trap: Writing "because acids give hydrogen with copper". Copper does not give hydrogen with dilute acids — the danger is the poisonous salts that slowly form.

Intext p.25, Q1Why HCl and HNO₃ are acidic but alcohol and glucose are not2 marks

Why do HCl, HNOX3\ce{HNO3}HNOX3​, etc., show acidic characters in aqueous solutions while solutions of compounds like alcohol and glucose do not show acidic character?

Given: HCl, HNOX3\ce{HNO3}HNOX3​ (acidic) vs alcohol, glucose (not acidic) — all contain hydrogen. Asked: the reason.

Concept used: acidic character comes from HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions released in water (Topic 4). Why it applies: it is not the hydrogen in the formula but the hydrogen ions in solution that matter.

  1. 1

    In water, HCl and HNOX3\ce{HNO3}HNOX3​ release hydrogen ions: HCl(aq)→HX+(aq)+ClX−(aq)\ce{HCl(aq) -> H+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}HCl(aq)​HX+(aq)+ClX−(aq).

  2. 2

    These HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions (as HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+) give the solutions their acidic properties.

  3. 3

    Alcohol and glucose also contain hydrogen, but they do not release it as HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions in water.

  4. 4

    With no HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions, their solutions show no acidic character (and do not conduct electricity).

  5. Check: the conductivity activity agrees: HCl solution lights the bulb; glucose and alcohol solutions do not ✔.

  6. Answer HCl and HNOX3\ce{HNO3}HNOX3​ ionise in water to give HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions, which cause acidic character. Alcohol and glucose do not produce HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions in water, so they are not acidic.

    Common trap: "Alcohol and glucose have no hydrogen." They do — they just don't release it as ions.
Intext p.25, Q2Why an acid solution conducts1 mark

Why does an aqueous solution of an acid conduct electricity?

Given: an aqueous acid solution. Asked: why it conducts.

Concept used: current in a solution is carried by moving ions (Topic 4). Why it applies: acids form ions in water.

  1. 1

    In water, an acid produces ions — HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) (or HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+) and a negative ion such as ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX−.

  2. 2

    These charged ions can move through the solution.

  3. 3

    Moving ions carry the electric current.

  4. Check: a solution with no ions (glucose) does not conduct — consistent ✔.

  5. Answer Because an acid produces ions (HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq)/HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ and anions) in water, and these ions carry the electric current.

    Common trap: "Because water conducts." Pure (distilled) water conducts very poorly; the ions come from the acid.
Intext p.25, Q3Dry HCl gas and dry litmus2 marks

Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of the dry litmus paper?

Given: dry HCl gas; dry litmus. Asked: why no colour change.

Concept used: HCl releases HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions only in the presence of water (Topic 4). Why it applies: both the gas and the paper are dry.

  1. 1

    Litmus changes colour because of HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions.

  2. 2

    Dry HCl gas consists of HCl molecules; without water, they do not separate into ions.

  3. 3

    With no HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions present, the litmus does not change.

  4. 4

    If the litmus is moist, HCl dissolves in the water: HCl+HX2O→HX3OX++ClX−\ce{HCl + H2O -> H3O+ + Cl-}HCl+HX2​O​HX3​OX++ClX−, and the paper turns red.

  5. Check: in HCl+HX2O→HX3OX++ClX−\ce{HCl + H2O -> H3O+ + Cl-}HCl+HX2​O​HX3​OX++ClX−: H 3 | 3, Cl 1 | 1, O 1 | 1, charge 0 | 0 ✔.

  6. Answer Dry HCl gas does not produce HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions in the absence of water, so it cannot show acidic behaviour and does not change dry litmus paper.

    Common trap: Saying "HCl gas is not an acid". It shows acidic behaviour as soon as water is present.
Intext p.25, Q4Acid to water, not water to acid2 marks

While diluting an acid, why is it recommended that the acid should be added to water and not water to the acid?

Given: diluting a (concentrated) acid. Asked: why acid is added to water.

Concept used: mixing a concentrated acid with water is highly exothermic (Topic 4). Why it applies: the order of mixing decides where the heat goes.

  1. 1

    Dissolving a concentrated acid in water releases a large amount of heat.

  2. 2

    If water is added to the acid, the small amount of water meets a large amount of acid; the heat produced is concentrated in one place.

  3. 3

    The mixture can boil suddenly and splash out, causing acid burns; the glass container may crack from local overheating.

  4. 4

    If the acid is added slowly to water with stirring, the heat spreads through the large volume of water, so the temperature rises safely.

  5. Check: this is why concentrated acid bottles carry a warning sign ✔.

  6. Answer Because the process is highly exothermic. Adding water to acid can make the mixture boil and splash, causing burns, and can break the container; adding acid slowly to water (with stirring) lets the heat spread safely.

    Common trap: "Because the acid becomes stronger." Order of mixing does not change strength — it is about heat and splashing.
Intext p.25, Q5Hydronium ions on dilution1 mark

How is the concentration of hydronium ions (HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+) affected when a solution of an acid is diluted?

Given: an acid solution being diluted. Asked: effect on HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ concentration.

Concept used: dilution spreads the same ions through a larger volume (Topic 4). Why it applies: concentration = amount per unit volume.

  1. 1

    Adding water increases the volume of the solution.

  2. 2

    The number of HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ ions stays about the same, so there are fewer of them per unit volume.

  3. Check: the solution becomes less acidic, and its pH rises towards 7 — as expected for dilution ✔.

  4. Answer The concentration of HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ ions (per unit volume) decreases.

    Common trap: "It increases because more water gives more HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+." The water spreads the ions; it does not add acid.
Intext p.25, Q6Hydroxide ions with excess base1 mark

How is the concentration of hydroxide ions (OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX−) affected when excess base is dissolved in a solution of sodium hydroxide?

Given: more base dissolved in NaOH solution. Asked: effect on OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− concentration.

Concept used: bases release OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions in water (Topic 4). Why it applies: more base in about the same volume means more OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX−.

  1. 1

    Each unit of base dissolved releases OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions: NaOH(s)→HX2ONaX+(aq)+OHX−(aq)\ce{NaOH(s) ->[H2O] Na+(aq) + OH-(aq)}NaOH(s)HX2​O​NaX+(aq)+OHX−(aq).

  2. 2

    Dissolving more base in the same volume adds more OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions per unit volume.

  3. Check: the solution becomes more basic, so its pH rises — consistent ✔.

  4. Answer The concentration of OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions increases.

    Common trap: Confusing this with dilution. Here base is added, not water.

Intext p.28, Q1pH 6 and pH 82 marks

You have two solutions, A and B. The pH of solution A is 6 and pH of solution B is 8. Which solution has more hydrogen ion concentration? Which of this is acidic and which one is basic?

Given: pH of A = 6; pH of B = 8. Asked: which has more HX+\ce{H+}HX+; which is acidic and which basic.

Concept used: below 7 = acidic, above 7 = basic; more HX+\ce{H+}HX+ → lower pH (Topic 5). Why it applies: we only need to compare each pH with 7 and with each other.

  1. 1

    A has the lower pH (6 < 8) → A has more hydrogen ions.

  2. 2

    pH 6 is below 7 → A is acidic.

  3. 3

    pH 8 is above 7 → B is basic.

  4. Check: the more acidic solution should also have more HX+\ce{H+}HX+ — A is both ✔.

  5. Answer A has the higher hydrogen ion concentration. A is acidic; B is basic.

    Common trap: Picking B because 8 is the bigger number. Higher pH means fewer HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions.
Intext p.28, Q2Effect of H⁺(aq) concentration2 marks

What effect does the concentration of HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions have on the nature of the solution?

Given: different HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) concentrations. Asked: the effect on the nature (acidic/basic) of the solution.

Concept used: HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions cause acidic character; pH falls as their concentration rises (Topic 5). Why it applies: the question links the amount of HX+\ce{H+}HX+ to acidity.

  1. 1

    The more HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions there are, the more acidic the solution and the lower its pH.

  2. 2

    The fewer HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions there are, the less acidic it is and the higher its pH.

  3. 3

    When HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions become fewer than OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions, the solution becomes basic (pH above 7).

  4. Check: this matches the pH scale: lots of HX+\ce{H+}HX+ at pH 0, few at pH 14 ✔.

  5. Answer Increasing the HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) concentration makes the solution more acidic (lower pH); decreasing it makes the solution less acidic, and eventually basic (higher pH).

    Common trap: Saying "more HX+\ce{H+}HX+ → higher pH".
Intext p.28, Q3Do basic solutions have H⁺ ions?2 marks

Do basic solutions also have HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions? If yes, then why are these basic?

Given: a basic solution. Asked: whether it contains HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq), and why it is still basic.

Concept used: every aqueous solution contains both HX+\ce{H+}HX+ and OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions (from water); the one present in excess decides the nature (Topics 4–5). Why it applies: water is always present in an aqueous solution.

  1. 1

    Water itself produces a very small number of HX+\ce{H+}HX+ and OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions, so yes, basic solutions contain some HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions.

  2. 2

    In a basic solution, the base adds a large number of OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions.

  3. 3

    So the concentration of OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions is greater than that of HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions.

  4. 4

    The excess of OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions makes the solution basic.

  5. Check: in pure (neutral) water, HX+\ce{H+}HX+ and OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− are equal; adding base tips the balance towards OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ✔.

  6. Answer Yes. Basic solutions do contain HX+(aq)\ce{H+(aq)}HX+(aq) ions, but they contain many more OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions than HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions, so they are basic.

    Common trap: Answering "No — only acids contain HX+\ce{H+}HX+".
Intext p.28, Q4When would a farmer add lime or chalk?2 marks

Under what soil condition do you think a farmer would treat the soil of his fields with quick lime (calcium oxide) or slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) or chalk (calcium carbonate)?

Given: three calcium compounds added to soil. Asked: the soil condition that needs them.

Concept used: bases (and carbonates) neutralise acids (Topics 3 and 5). Why it applies: quicklime and slaked lime are bases, and chalk reacts with acids.

  1. 1

    Quicklime is a basic oxide; slaked lime is a base; chalk is a carbonate — all three neutralise acids.

  2. 2

    Crops grow well only in a suitable pH range.

  3. 3

    So these are added when the soil is too acidic (pH too low), to raise its pH.

  4. Check: adding a base to an acid raises pH — exactly what an acidic soil needs ✔.

  5. Answer When the soil is too acidic. These substances neutralise the excess acid and raise the soil's pH to a level suitable for crops.

    Common trap: Saying "when the soil is basic" — adding lime would make basic soil even more basic.

Exercise Q7Distilled water vs rain water2 marks

Why does distilled water not conduct electricity, whereas rain water does?

Given: distilled water (does not conduct); rain water (conducts). Asked: why.

Concept used: a liquid conducts only if it contains ions (Topic 4). Why it applies: the two waters differ in what is dissolved in them.

  1. 1

    Distilled water is almost pure water; it has hardly any ions, so it does not conduct (practically).

  2. 2

    As rain falls through the air, it dissolves gases such as carbon dioxide (and, in polluted air, sulphur and nitrogen oxides), forming acids.

  3. 3

    These acids, and dust particles carrying salts, give ions in the rain water.

  4. 4

    These ions carry current, so rain water conducts.

  5. Check: this is also why clean rain has a pH of about 5.6 — it contains dissolved acid ✔.

  6. Answer Distilled water contains almost no ions, so it does not conduct. Rain water dissolves acidic gases (like carbon dioxide) and salts from the air, which produce ions; these ions conduct electricity.

    Common trap: "Rain water contains electricity from lightning." The difference is dissolved substances that form ions.
