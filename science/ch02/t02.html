Indicators: telling acids from bases

About 30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Name natural (litmus, turmeric, red cabbage, hydrangea), synthetic (phenolphthalein, methyl orange) and olfactory (onion, vanilla, clove oil) indicators.
  • State the colour of each indicator in acid and in base.
  • Identify an unknown solution as acidic, basic or neutral from indicator results.

A drop of dal or curry falls on a white school shirt and leaves a yellow stain. You rub it with soap — and the stain turns reddish-brown. Panic! Then you rinse it with plenty of water and it goes yellow again. Nothing magical happened: the turmeric in the curry was reporting on the soap.

Quick reminder: sour and bitter

In earlier classes you learnt that acids taste sour (lemon, tamarind, curd) and bases taste bitter and feel soapy (soap, baking soda solution). But you must never taste or touch a substance in the lab to find out — many acids and bases burn the skin. Chemists use a safer reporter instead.

What is an indicator?

An indicator (acid–base indicator) is a substance that shows a different colour — or a different smell — in an acid than in a base. Add a drop of it to a solution and it tells you which kind of solution you have.

There are three kinds.

1. Natural indicators come from plants.

  • Litmus is a purple dye. In a solution that is neither acidic nor basic (neutral) it stays purple. It is sold as blue litmus paper and red litmus paper.
  • Turmeric is yellow. It stays yellow in acids and turns reddish-brown in bases — that is the curry stain and the soap.
  • Red cabbage leaves give a purple extract that turns reddish-pink in acids and green in bases (these colours are not needed for the exam).
  • Coloured petals of flowers such as hydrangea, petunia and geranium also change colour with acids and bases.

2. Synthetic indicators are made in the laboratory.

  • Phenolphthalein is colourless in acids (and in neutral water) and turns pink in bases.
  • Methyl orange is orange as supplied. It turns red in acids and yellow in bases.

3. Olfactory indicators change their smell. "Olfactory" means "to do with smelling".

  • The smell of onion, vanilla essence or clove oil disappears in a basic solution but stays in an acidic solution.

Memory tip

"BaRB" — a Base turns Red litmus Blue; an acid does the opposite. Each litmus paper can change only one way: blue paper detects acids, red paper detects bases. And phenolphthalein turns pink only in bases.

Why one indicator is sometimes not enough

Look at phenolphthalein: it is colourless in an acid and in water. So if a solution stays colourless, you know only that it is not basic. To separate an acid from a neutral solution, you need a second test, such as blue litmus. Good lab work often uses two indicators.

What the lab shows (NCERT Activities 2.1 and 2.2)

Testing lab solutions with four indicators

  • What you do: Put one drop of each solution on a watch-glass and add a drop of red litmus solution, blue litmus solution, phenolphthalein or methyl orange.
  • What you observe:
Solution Red litmus Blue litmus Phenolphthalein Methyl orange
Hydrochloric, sulphuric, nitric, acetic acid no change red colourless red
Sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, ammonium hydroxide blue no change pink yellow
  • What it shows: All four acids behave alike, and all five bases behave alike. Acids must share something, and bases must share something — you will find out what in Topic 4.

Onion strips as an indicator

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Keep strips of clean cloth with chopped onion in a closed bag overnight. Put dilute hydrochloric acid on one strip and dilute sodium hydroxide on another; smell both. Repeat with vanilla essence and clove oil in test tubes.
See
The onion (or vanilla, or clove) smell is still there with the acid but cannot be detected with the base.
Shows
Onion, vanilla and clove oil are olfactory indicators.

Litmus is extracted from lichens, small plants of the division Thallophyta. And a hydrangea bush can grow blue flowers in acidic soil but pink ones in alkaline soil — gardeners sometimes "read" their soil from the flowers.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Identify three unknowns from a results table

Question. Three colourless liquids P, Q and R are tested. Which is acidic, which is basic and which is neutral?

  1. Liquid Blue litmus Phenolphthalein Methyl orange
    P no change pink yellow
    Q red colourless red
    R no change colourless yellow

    Given: three indicator results for each liquid. Asked: acidic, basic or neutral.

    Concept used: blue litmus turns red only in an acid; phenolphthalein turns pink only in a base; methyl orange is red in acid and yellow otherwise. Why it applies: each column rules certain answers in or out.

  2. 1

    P turns phenolphthalein pink — only a base does that → P is basic. (Blue litmus "no change" and methyl orange yellow agree.)

  3. 2

    Q turns blue litmus red and methyl orange red — both signs of an acid → Q is acidic.

  4. 3

    R does not turn blue litmus red, so it is not acidic.

  5. 4

    R leaves phenolphthalein colourless, so it is not basic.

  6. 5

    Not acidic and not basic → R is neutral.

  7. Check: test each answer with the column you did not use: P (basic) leaves blue litmus unchanged ✔; Q (acidic) keeps phenolphthalein colourless ✔; R (neutral) gives methyl orange yellow ✔.

  8. Answer P basic, Q acidic, R neutral.

    Common trap: Calling R acidic because phenolphthalein stayed colourless. Colourless phenolphthalein means not basic — it could be acidic or neutral.

Worked example 2

An indicator for a student who cannot see colours

Question. Asha has a colour-vision difficulty and cannot tell red from green easily. Suggest how she can test whether a solution is acidic or basic, and describe what she would notice.

Given: the student cannot rely on colour. Asked: a suitable test and the result to expect.

Concept used: olfactory indicators change their smell, not their colour. Why it applies: smell does not depend on seeing colours.

  1. 1

    Choose an olfactory indicator — for example, a few drops of vanilla essence (or clove oil, or an onion-soaked cloth strip).

  2. 2

    First smell the indicator on its own so she knows the smell.

  3. 3

    Add a few drops of it to the test solution and shake.

  4. 4

    Smell the mixture carefully by wafting the air towards her nose with her hand — never by sniffing the tube directly.

  5. 5

    If the vanilla smell disappears, the solution is basic.

  6. 6

    If the vanilla smell is still there, the solution is acidic (or neutral; she can then check with a known acid for comparison).

  7. Check: this matches the NCERT activity result — with dilute NaOH the smell cannot be detected; with dilute HCl it remains ✔.

  8. Answer Use an olfactory indicator such as vanilla essence, clove oil or onion: the smell disappears in a base and remains in an acid.

    Common trap: Saying the smell "becomes stronger in an acid". It simply remains; the change happens in a base.

Common mistakes!

  • "Phenolphthalein turns pink in acid."

    It is colourless in acids and in water.

    Pink = base only.

  • "Acids turn litmus blue."

    Acids turn blue litmus red.

    Blue paper tests for acid, red paper tests for base.

  • "Methyl orange is yellow in acid."

    It is red in acid, yellow in base.

    Learn it as a pair: methyl orange — red in acid, yellow in base.

  • "Colourless phenolphthalein means acid."

    Neutral water also leaves it colourless.

    Use a second indicator (blue litmus) to separate acid from neutral.

  • "Onion smell disappears in acid."

    It disappears in base.

    Olfactory: smell gone = base.

Key points

  • An indicator shows a different colour (or smell) in an acid and in a base.
  • Natural: litmus, turmeric, red cabbage, petals (hydrangea, petunia, geranium). Synthetic: phenolphthalein, methyl orange. Olfactory: onion, vanilla, clove oil.
  • Acid: blue litmus → red; methyl orange red; phenolphthalein colourless.
  • Base: red litmus → blue; methyl orange yellow; phenolphthalein pink; turmeric reddish-brown; onion/vanilla/clove smell disappears.
  • Use two indicators when one cannot separate acidic from neutral.
