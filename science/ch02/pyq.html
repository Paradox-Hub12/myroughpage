CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section B, Q181 mark

Question: Rishita summarizes the chlor-alkali process in the following table.

Option Product Formed Use
I Hydrogen At cathode Used as a fuel
II Chlorine At anode Used to make margarines
III Sodium Hydroxide Near cathode To make soap
IV Sodium Hydroxide Near anode To make bleaching powder
Identify the options that are correctly matched.

Given: four rows, each giving a product, where it forms and a use. Asked: the rows that are fully correct.

Concept used: chlor-alkali process — chlorine at the anode, hydrogen at the cathode, sodium hydroxide near the cathode, and the uses of each (Topic 6). Why it applies: a row is correct only if both the place and the use are right.

  1. 1

    I: hydrogen forms at the cathode ✔ and is used as a fuel ✔ → correct.

  2. 2

    II: chlorine forms at the anode ✔, but margarine is made with hydrogen (hardening of oils), not chlorine ✘ → wrong.

  3. 3

    III: sodium hydroxide forms near the cathode ✔ and is used to make soap ✔ → correct.

  4. 4

    IV: sodium hydroxide does not form near the anode ✘ (and bleaching powder is made from chlorine, not NaOH) → wrong.

  5. 5

    Correct rows: I and III → option B.

  6. Check: each product appears with its correct electrode exactly once in the correct rows ✔.

    Why the other options are wrong: A and D include row II (wrong use); C includes II and IV.
    Common trap: Accepting row II because "chlorine at the anode" is right — read the use too.
B. I and III — 1 mark (scheme: II and IV are incorrect, as chlorine is not used to make margarine and sodium hydroxide forms near the cathode)

Question: Which of the following salts contain water of crystallisation: (i) Plaster of Paris, (ii) Washing soda, (iii) Baking soda and (iv) Gypsum.
A. (i), (ii) and (iii) B.

Given: four salts. Asked: which have water of crystallisation.

Concept used: water of crystallisation is shown after a dot in the formula (Topic 7). Why it applies: we only need each formula.

  1. 1

    Plaster of Paris CaSOX4 ⋅ 12 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.1/2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅21​HX2​O → has water ✔.

  2. 2

    Washing soda NaX2COX3 ⋅ 10 HX2O\ce{Na2CO3.10H2O}NaX2​COX3​⋅10HX2​O → has water ✔.

  3. 3

    Baking soda NaHCOX3\ce{NaHCO3}NaHCOX3​ → no water of crystallisation ✘.

  4. 4

    Gypsum CaSOX4 ⋅ 2 HX2O\ce{CaSO4.2H2O}CaSOX4​⋅2HX2​O → has water ✔.

  5. 5

    Statements (i), (ii) and (iv) → option D.

  6. Check: the only option without baking soda is D ✔.

    Why the other options are wrong: A, B and C all include baking soda — its H is part of the hydrogencarbonate ion, not water.
    Common trap: Counting the "H" in NaHCOX3\ce{NaHCO3}NaHCOX3​ as water.

CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section B, Q241 mark

Question:
Assertion (A): Onion extract loses its smell, when it is added to baking soda solution whereas it retains its smell when added to lemon juice.
Reason (R): Onion acts as an olfactory indicator.

Given: an observation about onion in a base and an acid, and a statement about onion. Asked: truth of A and R, and whether R explains A.

Concept used: onion is an olfactory indicator — its smell disappears in a base and stays in an acid (Topic 2). Why it applies: baking soda solution is basic; lemon juice is acidic.

  1. 1

    A: baking soda solution is basic → onion smell is lost; lemon juice is acidic → smell stays → A is true.

  2. 2

    R: onion is an olfactory indicator → true.

  3. 3

    Does R explain A? R only names what onion is; it does not say why its smell is lost in a base and kept in an acid (that baking soda solution is basic and lemon juice acidic, and that onion's smell disappears in basic solutions). The official scheme marks this as not the correct explanation → option B.

  4. Check: both statements are facts from the lesson, so only A or B is possible; the scheme chooses B ✔.

    Why the other options are wrong: C and D each call a true statement false.
    Common trap: Choosing A automatically whenever R "sounds related". In this paper's scheme, a correct explanation must give the reason for the observation, not just a label.
B. Both A and R are true, and R is not the correct explanation of A.

CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section B, Q27 parts A and B (2 of the question's 3 marks)2 marks

Question: Give reason for the following:
A. A solution of sports electrolyte drink conducts electricity, but coconut oil does not.
B. Milkiness disappears when excess of carbon dioxide is passed through lime water.

Given: (A) a sports drink conducts, coconut oil does not; (B) lime water clears with excess COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​. Asked: a reason for each.

Concept used: a liquid conducts only if it contains moving ions (Topic 4); calcium carbonate is insoluble, calcium hydrogencarbonate is soluble (Topic 3). Why it applies: (A) is about ions; (B) is about solubility.

  1. 1

    A

    A sports electrolyte drink contains dissolved salts that give ions (such as NaX+\ce{Na+}NaX+, KX+\ce{K+}KX+, ClX−\ce{Cl-}ClX−) in water.

  2. 2

    Moving ions carry the current → it conducts.

  3. 3

    Coconut oil contains no ions (and no water to form them) → it does not conduct.

  4. 4

    B

    First, Ca(OH)X2(aq)+COX2(g)→CaCOX3(s)+HX2O(l)\ce{Ca(OH)2(aq) + CO2(g) -> CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)}Ca(OH)X2​(aq)+COX2​(g)​CaCOX3​(s)+HX2​O(l) — insoluble calcium carbonate makes the lime water milky.

  5. 5

    With excess COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​: CaCOX3(s)+HX2O(l)+COX2(g)→Ca(HCOX3)X2(aq)\ce{CaCO3(s) + H2O(l) + CO2(g) -> Ca(HCO3)2(aq)}CaCOX3​(s)+HX2​O(l)+COX2​(g)​Ca(HCOX3​)X2​(aq) — calcium hydrogencarbonate is soluble, so the milkiness disappears.

  6. Check: (A) matches the conductivity test (acid/salt solutions conduct, glucose and oils do not); (B) both equations balance (Ca, C, O, H) ✔.

    Common trap: In (A), "because the drink is a liquid" — coconut oil is a liquid too; the difference is ions.
A. A sports drink contains ions (electrolytes) which help conduct electricity. Coconut oil does not contain ions, so it cannot conduct electricity.
1 mark
B. Lime water turns milky due to the formation of insoluble calcium carbonate (CaCOX3\ce{CaCO3}CaCOX3​). When excess COX2\ce{CO2}COX2​ is passed, calcium carbonate reacts further to form soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate, causing the milkiness to disappear.
1 mark

CBSE SQP 2025-26, Section B, Q181 mark

Question: Four statements about the reactions of oxides with dilute hydrochloric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide are listed.
I. Aluminium oxide reacts with both dilute hydrochloric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide.
II. Calcium oxide reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide.
III. Zinc oxide reacts with both dilute hydrochloric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide.
IV. Sulphur dioxide does not react with either dilute hydrochloric acid or aqueous sodium hydroxide.
Which statements are correct?

Given: four statements about oxides with an acid and a base. Asked: which are correct.

Concept used: metal oxides are basic (react with acids); non-metal oxides are acidic (react with bases) (Topic 3). A few metal oxides — aluminium oxide and zinc oxide — react with both; they are called amphoteric (you will meet this word in Chapter 3). Why it applies: each statement claims what an oxide reacts with.

  1. 1

    I: aluminium oxide is amphoteric → reacts with both HCl and NaOH → correct.

  2. 2

    II: calcium oxide is a basic oxide → reacts with HCl, but not with NaOH (a base does not react with a basic oxide) → wrong.

  3. 3

    III: zinc oxide is amphoteric → reacts with both → correct.

  4. 4

    IV: sulphur dioxide is an acidic oxide → it does react with NaOH (Pattern 6): 2 NaOH+SOX2→NaX2SOX3+HX2O\ce{2NaOH + SO2 -> Na2SO3 + H2O}2NaOH+SOX2​​NaX2​SOX3​+HX2​O → wrong.

  5. 5

    Correct statements: I and III → option B.

  6. Check: every option containing II or IV (A, C, D) must be wrong, which leaves B ✔.

    Why the other options are wrong: A includes II (CaO does not react with NaOH); C includes II and IV; D includes IV (SO₂ does react with NaOH).
    Common trap: Accepting IV because "SO₂ is a gas, so it doesn't react". It is an acidic oxide — it reacts with bases.
B. (I) and (III)

CBSE SQP 2025-26, Section B, Q211 mark

Question: Which of the following substances when dissolved in equal volume of water, will have the highest pH value?

Given: two acids and two bases, each dissolved in the same volume of water. Asked: the highest pH.

Concept used: acids have pH below 7, bases above 7; a strong base gives more OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions, so a higher pH (Topic 5). Why it applies: we first rule out the acids, then compare the bases' strength.

  1. 1

    Sulphuric acid and acetic acid are acids → pH below 7 → not the highest.

  2. 2

    Magnesium hydroxide is a mild (weak) base that dissolves only slightly → few OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions (milk of magnesia, pH about 10).

  3. 3

    Sodium hydroxide is a strong base and very soluble → many OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions → pH about 13–14.

  4. 4

    Highest pH → sodium hydroxide, option D.

  5. Check: NaOH is the substance at the top of the pH ruler (about 14) ✔.

    Why the other options are wrong: A and B are acids (pH below 7). C is a base, but a mild one with far fewer OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX− ions than NaOH.
    Common trap: Choosing sulphuric acid because "it is the strongest" — strongest acid means lowest pH.
D. Sodium hydroxide

    First, Ca(OH)X2(aq)+COX2(g)→CaCOX3(s)+HX2O(l)\ce{Ca(OH)2(aq) + CO2(g) -> CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)}Ca(OH)X2​(aq)+COX2​(g)​CaCOX3​(s)+HX2​O(l) — the insoluble CaCOX3\ce{CaCO3}CaCOX3​ makes it milky.

  3. 3

    Calcium hydrogencarbonate is soluble, so the milkiness disappears → option B.

  4. Check: a soluble substance cannot cause milkiness, so calcium carbonate must be the insoluble one ✔.

Question: Sara took 2 mL of dilute NaOH solution in a test tube and added two drops of phenolphthalein solution to it. The solution turned pink in colour. She added dilute HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​ to the above solution drop by drop until the solution in the test tube became colourless. 40 drops of dilute HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​ were used for the change in colour from pink to colourless. When Sara added a drop of NaOH to the solution, the colour changed to back to pink again.
Sara now tried the activity with different volumes of NaOH and recorded her observation in the table given below:

S. No. Volume of dil. NaOH taken (mL) Drops of dil. HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​ used
1 2 20
2 3 30
3 4 40

A. If Sara used concentrated HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​ in place of dilute HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​, how many drops will be required for the change in colour to be observed? (a) 40 (b) < 40 (c) > 40. Justify your answer.
B. Sara measured 20 drops of dil. HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​ and found its volume to be 1 mL. If Sara observed a change in colour of NaOH solution by using 3 mL of HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​, how many mL of NaOH did she add to the test tube initially?

OR
B. Sara takes 10 drops of dilute HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​ in the test tube and adds two drops of phenolphthalein solution to it. Then she adds NaOH dropwise. Sara observes a change in colour after adding 20 drops of NaOH. What change in colour would she observe and why?

Note on the source. The paragraph says 40 drops neutralised 2 mL of NaOH, but row 1 of the table says 2 mL needed 20 drops. The marking scheme's answer to B uses the table (10 drops per mL of NaOH). We follow the table and the scheme.

Given: neutralisation of dilute NaOH by dilute HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​ with phenolphthalein; the table: 2 mL ↔ 20 drops, 3 mL ↔ 30 drops, 4 mL ↔ 40 drops. Asked: A, B (or B-OR).

Concept used: a more concentrated acid supplies more HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions per drop; with fixed solutions the acid needed is proportional to the base; phenolphthalein is colourless in acid and pink in base (Topics 2, 3, 5). Why it applies: every part depends on counting HX+\ce{H+}HX+ against OHX−\ce{OH-}OHX−.

  1. 1

    Part A

    The same NaOH needs the same number of HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions to be neutralised.

  2. 2

    Each drop of concentrated acid carries many more HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions than a drop of dilute acid.

  3. 3

    So fewer drops are needed → (b) < 40.

  4. 4

    Part B

    From the table, drops of acid ÷ mL of NaOH = 20 ÷ 2 = 10 drops per mL of NaOH.

  5. 5

    20 drops = 1 mL of acid, so 3 mL of acid = 3 × 20 = 60 drops.

  6. 6

    NaOH neutralised = 60 drops10 drops per mL=6 mL\dfrac{60\ \text{drops}}{10\ \text{drops per mL}} = 6\ \text{mL}10 drops per mL60 drops​=6 mL.

  7. 7

    Part B (OR)

    At the start the test tube holds only acid → phenolphthalein is colourless.

  8. 8

    As NaOH is added, it neutralises the acid; once the acid is used up and a little NaOH is in excess, the solution becomes basic.

  9. 9

    Phenolphthalein turns pink in a base → the colour changes from colourless to pink.

  10. Check: B: 6 mL NaOH × 10 drops/mL = 60 drops = 3 mL of acid ✔; the pattern 2 → 20, 3 → 30, 4 → 40, 6 → 60 is consistent ✔.

    Why the other choices in A are wrong: (a) 40 — the same number of drops would be needed only if the acid had the same concentration; (c) > 40 — more drops would be needed only for a more dilute acid.

    Common trap: In A, thinking "concentrated = weak, so more drops". Concentration is about how much acid is in each drop.
A. (b) < 40, because concentrated HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​ gives more HX+\ce{H+}HX+ ions than dilute acid. — 1 mark (inferred)
B. 3 mL of HX2SOX4\ce{H2SO4}HX2​SOX4​ = 60 drops, which will neutralise 6 mL of NaOH (from the table: 2 mL ↔ 20 drops, i.e. 10 drops per mL). — 1 mark (inferred)
OR B. The colour will change from colourless to pink. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acids and turns pink in basic solution. — 1 mark (inferred)

Solutions P, Q, R, S have pH 2, 9, 5 and 11. Which correctly represents them in increasing order of hydronium ion concentration?

Given: P 2, Q 9, R 5, S 11. Asked: increasing order of HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ concentration.

Concept used: more HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ → lower pH, so increasing HX3OX+\ce{H3O+}HX3​OX+ = decreasing pH (Topic 5). Why it applies: the order must run from the highest pH to the lowest.

  2. 2
