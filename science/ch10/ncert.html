Intext p.164, Q1Power of accommodation1 mark

What is meant by power of accommodation of the eye?

Given: the term "power of accommodation". Asked: its meaning.

Concept used: accommodation (Topic 1). Why it applies: this is its definition.

  1. 1

    What changes → the focal length of the eye lens.

  2. 2

    How → the ciliary muscles change the curvature of the lens.

  3. 3

    Why → so that both near and distant objects are focused on the retina.

  4. Check: the definition names the focal length (not the lens-to-retina distance) ✔.

  5. Answer The power of accommodation is the ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length, so that the eye can see both nearby and distant objects clearly.

    Write it in the exam (1 mark):

    The ability of the eye lens to adjust (change) its focal length to focus near and far objects on the retina. (1)

    Common trap: "The ability of the eye to move its lens." The lens changes its shape (curvature), not its position.
Intext p.164, Q2Lens for a myopic eye2 marks

A person with a myopic eye cannot see objects beyond 1.2 m distinctly. What should be the type of the corrective lens used to restore proper vision?

Given: myopic eye; far point = 1.2 m. Asked: the type of corrective lens.

Concept used: correction of myopia (Topic 2). Why it applies: the person's far point is nearer than infinity.

  1. 1

    Where the image falls → for a distant object, in front of the retina.

  2. 2

    What is needed → a lens that makes parallel rays from a distant object seem to come from the far point, 1.2 m away.

  3. 3

    Lens → a diverging (concave) lens.

  4. 4

    (Extra, not asked) its focal length would be f=−1.2 mf = -1.2\ \text{m}f=−1.2 m and its power P=1(−1.2 m)≈−0.83 DP = \dfrac{1}{(-1.2\ \text{m})} \approx -0.83\ \text{D}P=(−1.2 m)1​≈−0.83 D.

  5. Check: myopia is always corrected by a lens of negative power ✔.

  6. Answer A concave (diverging) lens of suitable power (about −0.83 D, focal length −1.2 m).

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    Concave lens (diverging lens) (1) of suitable power, which forms the image of distant objects at the far point (1.2 m), so the image falls on the retina. (1)

    Common trap: "Convex lens." A myopic eye already converges light too strongly.
Intext p.164, Q3Far point and near point1 mark

What is the far point and near point of the human eye with normal vision?

Given: a normal eye. Asked: its far point and near point.

Concept used: near point and far point (Topic 1). Why it applies: these are the limits of clear vision.

  1. 1

    Far point → the farthest point up to which the eye sees clearly → infinity.

  2. 2

    Near point → the least distance for distinct vision without strain → about 25 cm (for a young adult).

  3. Check: a normal eye sees clearly between 25 cm and infinity ✔.

  4. Answer Far point = infinity; near point = about 25 cm.

    Write it in the exam (1 mark):

    Far point: infinity; near point: about 25 cm. (½ + ½)

    Common trap: swapping the two, or writing 25 m.
Intext p.164, Q4Difficulty reading the blackboard2 marks

A student has difficulty reading the blackboard while sitting in the last row. What could be the defect the child is suffering from? How can it be corrected?

Given: the board (distant) is not clear. Asked: the defect and its correction.

Concept used: the defect card (Topic 2). Why it applies: only distant vision is affected.

  1. 1

    What is blurred → a distant object (the board).

  2. 2

    Defect → myopia (near-sightedness) — the far point has come closer; the image of the board forms in front of the retina.

  3. 3

    Correction → spectacles with a concave lens of suitable power, which brings the image back onto the retina.

  4. Check: the student can presumably read a book (near), which fits myopia ✔.

  5. Answer Myopia. It is corrected with a concave lens of suitable power.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    Myopia (near-sightedness). (1) Corrected by using a concave lens of suitable power. (1)

    Common trap: "Hypermetropia, because the student is far from the board." The name describes what the person can see: a myopic (near-sighted) person sees near things.

Exercise Q82 marks

Why is a normal eye not able to see clearly the objects placed closer than 25 cm?

Given: an object closer than 25 cm. Asked: why it is not seen clearly.

Concept used: accommodation has a limit; the near point (Topic 1). Why it applies: 25 cm is the near point of a normal eye.

  1. 1

    Near objects need a short focal length → the ciliary muscles contract and the lens becomes thicker.

  2. 2

    There is a limit → the focal length of the eye lens cannot be decreased below a certain minimum.

  3. 3

    At about 25 cm that minimum is reached — this is the near point.

  4. 4

    Closer than 25 cm → the lens cannot bend the light enough, so the image does not form on the retina → the object looks blurred (or the eye strains).

  5. Check: this is why you hold a book at about 25 cm to read comfortably ✔.

  6. Answer The focal length of the eye lens cannot be made smaller than a certain minimum. For a normal eye this limit corresponds to about 25 cm (the near point), so objects closer than that cannot be focused on the retina and look blurred.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    The power of accommodation of the eye is limited — the focal length of the eye lens cannot decrease below a minimum value. (1) So objects closer than the near point (25 cm) are not focused on the retina and appear blurred / cause strain. (1)

    Common trap: "Because the object is too small to see." Size is not the reason; focusing is.
Exercise Q92 marks

What happens to the image distance in the eye when we increase the distance of an object from the eye?

Given: the object moves farther from the eye. Asked: what happens to the image distance in the eye.

Concept used: accommodation (Topic 1). Why it applies: the image must always fall on the retina.

  1. 1

    Image distance in the eye → the distance from the eye lens to the retina.

  2. 2

    That distance is fixed by the size of the eyeball.

  3. 3

    What changes instead → as the object moves away, the ciliary muscles relax, the lens becomes thinner and its focal length increases, keeping the image on the retina.

  4. Check: if the image distance changed, the image would leave the retina and look blurred ✔.

  5. Answer The image distance stays the same (lens to retina). The eye adjusts by increasing the focal length of the eye lens (the lens becomes thinner) as the object moves away.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    The image distance remains the same — it is the distance between the eye lens and the retina. (1) The eye lens increases its focal length (becomes thinner) by accommodation, so the image still forms on the retina. (1)

    Common trap: "The image distance decreases," as it would for a glass lens. In the eye the screen cannot move, so the focal length changes.
Exercise Q103 marks

Why do stars twinkle?

Given: stars seen through the atmosphere. Asked: why they twinkle.

Concept used: atmospheric refraction (Topic 5). Why it applies: starlight passes through layers of air of gradually changing refractive index.

  1. 1

    Refraction → starlight entering the atmosphere is refracted continuously, because the refractive index of the air changes gradually.

  2. 2

    Apparent position → the star appears at a slightly different position from its real one.

  3. 3

    Not stationary → the physical conditions of the atmosphere keep changing, so the path of the light — and the apparent position — keep changing slightly.

  4. 4

    Point source → stars are very far away, so they are point-sized sources.

  5. 5

    Flicker → as the path changes, the amount of starlight entering the eye flickers: the star looks brighter, then fainter — it twinkles.

  6. Check: every step names refraction, not scattering ✔.

  7. Answer Stars twinkle because of atmospheric refraction. Starlight is refracted continuously by air whose refractive index changes gradually and keeps fluctuating. Since a star is a point source, the changing path makes its apparent position and the amount of light reaching the eye flicker, so it looks brighter and fainter by turns.

    Write it in the exam (3 marks):

    Starlight undergoes atmospheric refraction continuously in a medium of gradually changing refractive index. (1) The physical conditions of the atmosphere are not stationary, so the apparent position of the star keeps changing. (1) Stars are point sources, so the amount of light entering the eye flickers — the star appears brighter and fainter — this is twinkling. (1)

    Common trap: "Because of scattering of starlight by dust." Twinkling is a refraction effect.
Exercise Q112 marks

Explain why the planets do not twinkle.

Given: planets seen through the same atmosphere. Asked: why they do not twinkle.

Concept used: point and extended sources (Topic 5). Why it applies: a planet is much closer than a star.

  1. 1

    Closer → planets are much closer to the earth, so they are seen as extended sources (tiny discs).

  2. 2

    Many point sources → a planet can be thought of as a collection of a large number of point-sized sources.

  3. 3

    Averaging → the light from each point flickers, but the total variation in light entering the eye from all of them averages out to zero.

  4. 4

    Result → the twinkling effect is nullified; the planet shines steadily.

  5. Check: the light from planets also passes through the atmosphere — the difference is the size of the source, not the air ✔.

  6. Answer Planets are much closer to the earth and are seen as extended sources. The light from their many points varies in different ways, and the total variation averages out to zero, so the twinkling effect is cancelled.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    Planets are closer to the earth and act as extended sources — a collection of many point sources. (1) The variations in the light from these point sources average out to zero, so there is no twinkling. (1)

    Common trap: "Planets don't twinkle because they don't have their own light." That is not the reason.
Exercise Q122 marks

Why does the sky appear dark instead of blue to an astronaut?

Given: an astronaut far above the atmosphere. Asked: why the sky looks dark.

Concept used: the blue sky is due to scattering by the atmosphere (Topic 6). Why it applies: above the atmosphere there is almost no air.

  1. 1

    On earth → air molecules scatter blue sunlight into our eyes → blue sky.

  2. 2

    Astronaut → is above the atmosphere (or where it is extremely thin) → no particles to scatter sunlight.

  3. 3

    Result → no scattered light reaches the astronaut's eyes from the sky → the sky looks dark.

  4. Check: passengers at very high altitudes also see a dark sky, for the same reason ✔.

  5. Answer At the astronaut's height there is practically no atmosphere, so sunlight is not scattered. No scattered light reaches the eye from the sky, so it looks dark instead of blue.

    Write it in the exam (2 marks):

    The blue colour of the sky is due to scattering of sunlight by air molecules and fine particles in the atmosphere. (1) Above the atmosphere there are no such particles, so there is no scattering and the sky appears dark. (1)

    Common trap: "Because there is no sunlight in space." Sunlight is there; it is just not scattered.
