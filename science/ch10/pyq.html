CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section C, Q39 — Option A5 marks

Question:
I. What is meant by accommodation of the human eye?
II. What is the near point of a normal eye and what does it signify?
III. Name which type of lens is used to correct the defect in which a person cannot see distant objects clearly.
IV. Draw a diagram to show how the choice of your lens in (d) helps to see distant objects clearly?

(Note: the paper numbers the parts I–IV, so "(d)" in part IV means part III.)

Given: four parts on the eye. Asked: accommodation; near point and its meaning; the lens for not seeing distant objects; a diagram of that correction.

Concept used: accommodation and near point (Topic 1); myopia and its correction (Topic 2). Why it applies: each part matches one idea.

  1. 1

    I → accommodation is the ability of the eye lens to change its focal length so that objects at different distances are focused on the retina.

  2. 2

    II → near point: the nearest point, about 25 cm for a normal eye, at which an object is seen distinctly without strain. It signifies the closest distance for comfortable, clear vision — closer than this, the eye cannot focus.

  3. 3

    III → cannot see distant objects → myopia → a concave (diverging) lens.

  4. 4

    IV → draw Fig 10.2 (c): parallel rays from a distant object strike a concave lens in front of the eye, spread out as if coming from the far point O (dashed back-extensions), and are focused on the retina.

  5. Check: the lens in IV is the one named in III ✔.

    Common trap: III — "convex lens". A person who cannot see distant objects is myopic and needs a concave lens.
I. Accommodation: Ability of the eye lens to change its focal length to focus objects at different distances on the retina. (1)
II. Near point: The nearest point (about 25 cm for a normal eye) at which an object can be seen distinctly; it signifies the closest comfortable focus. (1)
III. A diverging (concave) lens is used to correct myopia (short-sightedness). (1)
IV. Diagram: correction of myopia with a concave lens — parallel rays diverged by the lens appear to come from the far point and are focused on the retina. (2)
5 marks

The marking scheme's diagram is drawn with the eye on the left and the light coming from the right — a mirror image of your textbook's Fig 10.2 (c). Either direction is correct.

CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section C, Q39 — Option B5 marks

Question:
I. What is dispersion of light?
II. Which colour of light deviates the least during dispersion through a prism?
III. State why the sky appears dark to an astronaut in outer space.
IV. Draw a simple labelled diagram to show how white light splits into different colours while passing through a glass prism.

Given: four parts on dispersion and the sky. Asked: a definition; the least-deviated colour; why space looks dark; the dispersion diagram.

Concept used: dispersion (Topic 4); scattering (Topic 6). Why it applies: parts I, II and IV are dispersion; part III is scattering.

  1. 1

    I → dispersion is the splitting of white light into its component colours.

  2. 2

    II → red deviates least (violet most).

  3. 3

    III → in outer space there is no atmosphere to scatter sunlight, so no scattered light reaches the eye and the sky looks dark.

  4. 4

    IV → draw Fig 10.5: a glass prism, a white light beam entering one face, the colours spreading out, labelled R (top) and V (bottom), with "White light beam", "Glass prism" and "spectrum".

  5. Check: the least-deviated colour in II is the one at the top of the diagram in IV ✔.

    Common trap: III — "because there is no sunlight in space". Sunlight is there; it is not scattered.
I. Dispersion: Splitting of white light into its component colours due to wavelength-dependent refraction. (1)
II. Red deviates least (longest wavelength). (1)
III. No atmosphere to scatter sunlight — space appears dark. (1)
IV. Diagram of dispersion by a glass prism (white light beam, glass prism, spectrum from R to V). (2)
5 marks
CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section C, Q39 — Option A for visually impaired students5 marks

Question:
I. What is meant by hypermetropia (long-sightedness)?
II. What is the far point of a normal human eye and what does it signify?
III. Name the type of lens used to correct hypermetropia.
IV. Explain why a hypermetropic eye is unable to focus nearby objects on the retina.

Given: four parts on hypermetropia and the far point. Asked: a definition; the far point and its meaning; the lens; the reason for the defect.

Concept used: far point (Topic 1); hypermetropia (Topic 2). Why it applies: each part matches one row of the defect card.

  1. 1

    I → hypermetropia: a person sees distant objects clearly but not nearby ones (the near point is beyond 25 cm).

  2. 2

    II → far point of a normal eye = infinity; it means a normal eye can see even very distant objects clearly.

  3. 3

    III → a convex (converging) lens.

  4. 4

    IV → the eyeball is too short (too small) or the eye lens's focal length is too long (it does not converge enough), so light from a nearby object meets behind the retina. A convex lens adds converging power so the image forms on the retina.

  5. Check: IV gives both causes in your textbook ✔.

    Common trap: IV — writing "the image forms in front of the retina". That is myopia.
I. Hypermetropia is a defect of vision in which a person can see distant objects clearly but cannot see nearby objects clearly. (1)
II. The far point of a normal human eye is at infinity. It signifies that a normal eye can see objects clearly even when they are extremely far away. (1)
III. A convex (converging) lens is used to correct hypermetropia. (1)
IV. A hypermetropic eye cannot focus nearby objects on the retina because (a) the eyeball is too short, or (b) the eye lens is not converging enough (insufficient curvature). As a result, the image of a nearby object forms behind the retina, instead of on it. A convex lens is used to increase the convergence of light so that the image forms on the retina. (2)
5 marks

Your textbook words the second cause as "the focal length of the eye lens is too long" — it means the same as "not converging enough".

CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section C, Q39 — Option B for visually impaired students5 marks

Question:
I. Why does Earth’s atmosphere exhibit Tyndall’s effect?
II. Which colour of light has the shortest wavelength?
III. Why do danger/stop signs use red colour?
IV. State why the sky appears blue even though sunlight is white.

Given: four parts on scattering. Asked: why the atmosphere shows the Tyndall effect; the shortest wavelength; why red signs; why the sky is blue.

Concept used: scattering of light (Topic 6). Why it applies: every part is a scattering effect.

  1. 1

    I → the atmosphere is a heterogeneous mixture of minute particles — smoke, tiny water droplets, dust, molecules of air. These particles scatter a beam of light, making its path visible — the Tyndall effect.

  2. 2

    II → violet has the shortest wavelength (red the longest).

  3. 3

    III → red light is scattered least by fog, smoke or dust, so it is seen clearly from far away.

  4. 4

    IV → sunlight contains all colours; air molecules, smaller than the wavelength of light, scatter shorter wavelengths (blue) more strongly; this scattered blue light reaches our eyes.

  5. Check: II and III fit together: shortest wavelength (violet) is scattered most, longest (red) least ✔.

    Common trap: III — "red is scattered most, so it spreads everywhere". It is scattered least.
I. The earth's atmosphere is a heterogeneous mixture of minute particles. These particles include smoke, tiny water droplets, suspended particles of dust and molecules of air. When a beam of light strikes such fine particles, the path of the beam becomes visible. The phenomenon of scattering of light by the colloidal particles gives rise to Tyndall effect. (2)
II. Violet light has the shortest wavelength. (1)
III. Red light is least scattered by air because it has the longest wavelength. It remains visible over long distances even in fog, smoke, or dust. Hence, red is chosen for alert and stop signals. (1)
IV. Sunlight is white but when it enters the atmosphere, the smaller particles of air scatter shorter wavelengths (blue and violet) more strongly than others. Our eyes are more sensitive to blue than violet, so the sky appears blue. (1)
5 marks

"Our eyes are more sensitive to blue than violet" goes beyond your textbook; for full marks the textbook reason — blue (shorter wavelength) is scattered more strongly than red — is the key point.

CBSE SQP 2025-26, Section C, Q311 mark

Question: Choose the correct option from the below which explains the reason for us to perceive the day sky as blue.
A. As sunlight passes through the atmosphere, shorter wavelengths, such as blue are scattered more than other colors.
B. The sky appears blue because all colors are scattered equally, but blue light is stronger and more visible to the human eye.
C. The blue color of the sky is due to longer wavelengths like red and orange scattering more than shorter wavelengths, making blue stand out more.
D. The atmosphere contains blue-colored particles that give the sky its blue appearance.

Given: four reasons. Asked: the correct one.

Concept used: why the sky is blue (Topic 6). Why it applies: the options differ only in which colours are scattered more.

  1. 1

    A ✔ → fine particles of air scatter shorter wavelengths (blue) more than longer ones.

  2. 2

    B ✘ → colours are not scattered equally.

  3. 3

    C ✘ → it is the other way round — longer wavelengths are scattered less.

  4. 4

    D ✘ → air has no "blue-coloured particles"; the colour comes from scattering.

  5. Check: A matches your textbook's reason in Topic 6 ✔.

    Common trap: B sounds sensible, but its first half ("scattered equally") is false.
A. As sunlight passes through the atmosphere, shorter wavelengths, such as blue are scattered more than other colors.
1 mark

The marking scheme adds "Rayleigh scattering" and "our eyes are more sensitive to blue than violet"; both go beyond your textbook and are not needed to choose A.

The above image shows a corrective measure for a particular defect of vision.

  1. 1

    (i) A convex lens that makes light from a near point seem to come from a farther point → the person's near point has moved away → hypermetropia; lens → convex (converging).

CBSE SQP 2025-26, Section C, Q35 — for visually impaired students3 marks

Question:
(i) What is dispersion of light?
(ii) Explain the condition under which dispersion happens?
(iii) Give one reason that causes presbyopia.

Given: three short parts. Asked: a definition; when dispersion happens; a cause of presbyopia.

Concept used: dispersion (Topic 4); presbyopia (Topic 2). Why it applies: each part is one idea.

  1. 1

    (i) Dispersion is the splitting of white light into its component colours (a spectrum) when it passes through a prism; different colours bend through different angles.

  2. 2

    (ii) Dispersion happens when white light is refracted by a medium such as glass (a prism) or water (raindrops), in which different colours bend through different angles — so they leave along different paths.

  3. 3

    (iii) Presbyopia is caused by the gradual weakening of the ciliary muscles or the diminishing flexibility of the eye lens with age.

  4. Check: (ii) explains why a prism and a raindrop both give a spectrum ✔.

    Common trap: (iii) giving a cause of hypermetropia ("eyeball too small") instead of the age-related cause.
(i) Dispersion of light is the phenomenon in which white light separates into its component colours (spectrum) when it passes through a medium, such as a prism. Different colours of light bend through different angles with respect to incident light, thus becoming distinct. (1) (split inferred)
(ii) Dispersion occurs when light passes from one medium to another where each colour of light bends through a different angle, as in a glass prism or water. (1) (split inferred)
(iii) Presbyopia is caused by the gradual loss of flexibility in the lens of the eye, which occurs with aging. (1) (split inferred)
3 marks

The printed marking scheme has two typing slips in (i) — "lend" for "bend" and "district" for "distinct" — corrected above. Its answer to (ii) also talks about the speed and refractive index differing for each wavelength, which goes beyond your textbook; the shortened answer above keeps to the textbook idea.
