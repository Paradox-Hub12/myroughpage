Topic 6 of 6

Scattering of light

About 30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain the Tyndall effect with examples, and how the colour of scattered light depends on particle size.
  • Explain why the clear sky is blue and why it looks dark at very great heights.
  • Explain why danger signals are red.

When sunlight streams through a gap in the curtains of a dusty room, you can see the beam itself — a bright shaft full of floating specks. Outside, the same sunlight is white, yet the whole sky glows blue. And the warning lights on a tall tower, or the "stop" light of a railway signal, are red, not blue. Tiny particles in the air are behind all three.

Quick reminder (Class 9, Is Matter Around Us Pure?). You cannot see a light beam's path through a true solution (such as salt in water). Through a colloid (such as milk in water), whose particles are bigger, you can. The particles scatter the light — this is the Tyndall effect.

  1. The Tyndall effect in the atmosphere

The air around the earth is not pure and even: it is a heterogeneous mixture of very small particles — smoke, tiny water droplets, floating dust and the molecules of air themselves.

  • When light falls on such tiny particles, we can see the beam's path.
  • The light reaches our eyes after being reflected diffusely (sent off in many directions) by these particles. This is scattering of light.
  • This scattering of light by colloid-sized particles is the Tyndall effect. It also lets us see the particles.

Where you see it

  • A thin shaft of sunlight coming through a small hole into a smoky room.
  • Sunlight streaming through the leaves of a thick forest on a misty morning — the mist droplets scatter it.

  1. The colour depends on the size of the particles

Light of each colour has a wavelength. Blue light has a shorter wavelength than red; the wavelength of red light is about 1.8 times that of blue light. (Across the whole spectrum, violet has the shortest wavelength and red the longest — CBSE's sample paper uses this.)

  • Very fine particles scatter mainly blue light (short wavelengths).
  • Larger particles scatter light of longer wavelengths as well.
  • If the particles are large enough, the scattered light may even look white.

  1. Why the clear sky is blue

  1. Air molecules and the other very fine particles in the sky are smaller than the wavelength of visible light.
  2. Such tiny particles scatter light of shorter wavelengths (the blue end) much more effectively than light of longer wavelengths (the red end).
  3. So as sunlight crosses the atmosphere, blue is scattered much more strongly than red.
  4. This scattered blue light reaches our eyes from every part of the sky → the sky looks blue.

(Beyond the syllabus: violet is scattered even more than blue, but our eyes are more sensitive to blue, so the sky looks blue rather than violet. Both CBSE marking schemes mention this.)

  1. Why the sky looks dark very high up

  • No atmosphere → no scattering. If the earth had no atmosphere, no sunlight would be scattered towards us, and the sky would look dark even in daytime.
  • Passengers flying at very high altitudes see the sky as dark, because the air is so thin there that scattering is not prominent.

  1. Why danger signals are red

Red light is scattered the least by fog or smoke. So a red light travels through fog or smoke without being spread out much, and is seen in the same colour from a distance. That is why 'danger' signal lights are red.

Not in your syllabus this year

the reddish colour of the Sun at sunrise and sunset is excluded from the 2026-27 syllabus, so it is not taught here. (Your textbook mentions it only in passing.)

  1. The scattering chain — write it like this

particle size → which colours it scatters → what we see

  • air molecules (smaller than the wavelength) → mostly blue → blue sky
  • dust, smoke, mist (colloidal size) → beam becomes visible → Tyndall effect
  • fog or smoke, red light → red scattered least → red danger signals seen from far
  • no air → nothing scattered → dark sky

Worked examples

Worked example 1

A torch beam in two rooms

Question. Sonu shines a torch across a dusty storeroom and can see the beam all the way to the wall. In a clean, dust-free laboratory he can see only the spot on the wall, not the beam. Explain the difference.

Given: one room full of dust; one room nearly free of particles. Asked: why the beam is seen in one and not in the other.

Concept used: the Tyndall effect (this topic). Why it applies: a beam is seen only if something sends its light into our eyes.

  1. 1

    Dusty room → the beam strikes many dust particles.

  2. 2

    Each particle scatters (diffusely reflects) some light in all directions — some of it into Sonu's eyes.

  3. 3

    So the path of the beam becomes visible → the Tyndall effect.

  4. 4

    Clean room → almost no particles to scatter light sideways, so no light from the beam reaches his eyes; he sees only the spot where the beam hits the wall.

  5. Check: the same thing happens with a true solution (beam invisible) and a colloid (beam visible) in Class 9 ✔.

  6. Answer In the dusty room, dust particles scatter the torch light sideways into his eyes, so the beam is visible (Tyndall effect). In the clean room there are hardly any particles to scatter the light, so the beam cannot be seen.

    Common trap: "The beam is brighter in the dusty room." The torch is the same; the beam is seen only because of scattering.

Worked example 2

The sky on the Moon

Question. The Moon has no atmosphere. What colour would its sky look to an astronaut standing on it in full sunlight? Explain.

Given: no atmosphere; Sun shining. Asked: the colour of the sky; the reason.

Concept used: the blue sky needs scattering by the atmosphere (this topic). Why it applies: scattering needs particles, and there is no air.

  1. 1

    On earth → air molecules scatter blue sunlight towards us from every direction → the sky is blue.

  2. 2

    On the Moon → no atmosphere → no molecules or particles to scatter sunlight.

  3. 3

    Result → no scattered light reaches the astronaut from the sky; light comes only straight from the Sun and from the ground.

  4. 4

    So the sky looks dark (black), even in full sunlight.

  5. Check: your textbook says the sky would look dark if the earth had no atmosphere ✔.

  6. Answer Dark (black) — there is no atmosphere to scatter sunlight, so no light reaches the eye from the sky.

    Common trap: "Dark because there is no sunlight on the Moon." The Sun is shining; the sky is dark because nothing scatters the light.

Worked example 3

Choosing a warning lamp

Question. A town wants a warning lamp at a railway crossing that is often covered in morning fog. It can choose a blue, a green or a red lamp. Which should it choose? Why?

Given: fog; three colours to choose from. Asked: the best colour and the reason.

Concept used: red light is scattered least by fog or smoke (this topic). Why it applies: the lamp must be seen clearly from far away through fog.

  1. 1

    Blue → short wavelength → scattered strongly by fog → spreads out and fades over distance.

  2. 2

    Green → in the middle of the spectrum → scattered more than red.

  3. 3

    Red → longest wavelength of the three → scattered least by fog.

  4. 4

    So red light passes through the fog best and is seen in the same colour from a distance.

  5. Check: danger signals are red for exactly this reason ✔.

  6. Answer Red — red light is scattered least by fog, so it can be seen clearly, in its own colour, from far away.

    Common trap: "Red, because red is scattered the most and so spreads everywhere." It is the opposite: red is scattered the least.

Common mistakes!

  • "The sky is blue because it reflects the sea."

    The sky is blue far from any sea.

    Air molecules scatter blue light most.

  • "Blue light is absorbed by the air."

    Blue is scattered towards us, not absorbed.

    Use the word scattered.

  • "Danger lights are red because red is scattered most."

    Red is scattered least, so it travels through fog.

    Red: least scattered → seen from far.

  • "The sky is dark for astronauts because there is no sunlight."

    Sunlight is there; nothing scatters it.

    No atmosphere → no scattering → dark sky.

  • "The Tyndall effect is the splitting of light into colours."

    That is dispersion.

    Tyndall effect = scattering by colloidal particles makes the beam visible.

Key points

  • Scattering: small particles send light off in all directions; it makes particles and beams visible — the Tyndall effect (smoke-filled room, misty forest).
  • Particle size decides colour: very fine → blue; larger → longer wavelengths; large enough → white.
  • Blue sky: air molecules, smaller than the wavelength of light, scatter blue (short wavelength) more strongly than red.
  • Dark sky with no atmosphere, or very high up, because there is no (or little) scattering.
  • Red danger signals: red is scattered least by fog or smoke, so it is seen in the same colour from a distance.
