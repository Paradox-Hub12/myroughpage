Wrap-up

Which effect is it?

About 25 minutes · 6 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe myopia and its correction.
  • Describe hypermetropia and its correction.
  • Calculate the focal length and power of a corrective lens.
  • Describe dispersion and the spectrum.
  • Explain twinkling and the apparent position of stars.
  • Explain the blue sky and the dark sky at great heights.

This chapter has two halves. The eye half is about one lens that changes its focal length — and what to do when it cannot change enough. The colourful world half is about what happens to sunlight on its way to your eye: it is bent (by a prism, a raindrop or the air), split into colours, or scattered by tiny particles. Almost every question becomes easy once you decide which effect it is about. This page puts the chapter on three cards and then works through four mixed questions.

Mixed example 2

Sort the effects

Question. Name the cause of each: (a) a star flickering low in the sky; (b) a bright shaft of light between trees on a misty morning; (c) the colours seen in the spray of a fountain; (d) the sky looking black from a very high balloon.

Given: four everyday effects. Asked: the cause of each.

Concept used: the "Which effect is it?" card (Card 3). Why it applies: each effect has exactly one main cause in this chapter.

  1. 1

    (a) Flickering star → atmospheric refraction (the path of the starlight keeps changing).

  2. 2

    (b) Visible shaft in mist → scattering by water droplets — the Tyndall effect.

  3. 3

    (c) Colours in a spray → dispersion with internal reflection in droplets — a rainbow.

  4. 4

    (d) Black sky high up → very little air, so little scattering.

  5. Check: each answer uses a different row of Card 3, and (c) needs the Sun behind the viewer ✔.

  6. Answer (a) atmospheric refraction (b) scattering — Tyndall effect (c) dispersion (rainbow: refraction, dispersion, internal reflection) (d) lack of scattering

    Common trap: (a) "scattering". Scattering gives colour to the sky; it does not make a star's position and brightness flicker.

A myopic student and the board

Mixed example 4

A prism, then the sky

Question. (a) A narrow beam of white light falls on a prism. Name the colour that is deviated least and the colour deviated most. (b) Using the same idea of "colours behaving differently", why is the sky blue and not red?

Given: white light; a prism; the sky. Asked: least and most deviated colours; why the sky is blue.

Concept used: dispersion (Topic 4) and scattering (Topic 6). Why it applies: in both, different colours behave differently — they bend by different amounts in a prism and are scattered by different amounts in air.

  1. 1

    (a) In a prism → red is deviated least, violet most.

  2. 2

    (b) In air → molecules smaller than the wavelength scatter short wavelengths (blue) much more than long wavelengths (red).

  3. 3

    So the light reaching us from the sky (scattered light) is mostly blue.

  4. Check: red is "least" in both cases — least bent and least scattered ✔.

  5. Answer (a) Red least, violet most. (b) Air molecules scatter blue light much more strongly than red, so the scattered light from the sky that reaches us is mainly blue.

    Common trap: saying "dispersion makes the sky blue". The sky's colour is due to scattering, not dispersion.
