Refraction through a glass prism

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe a triangular prism (two triangular bases, three rectangular lateral faces) and define the angle of the prism.
  • Trace the path of a ray through a prism (Activity 10.1, CBSE Practical 13) and label the incident, refracted and emergent rays and the angles i, r, e, A and D (Fig 10.4).
  • Explain the bending at each face and why, unlike a glass slab, the emergent ray is not parallel to the incident ray; define the angle of deviation.

Look at a pencil through the thick glass of a window louvre or a glass paperweight held edge-on, and it seems to jump sideways. Through a rectangular slab, a ray of light comes out in its original direction. Through a triangular prism, it comes out pointing somewhere else. What makes the difference?

Quick reminder (Chapter 9)

When light goes from air into glass it slows down and bends towards the normal. When it goes from glass into air it bends away from the normal. In a rectangular glass slab the two faces are parallel, so the second bending exactly undoes the first: the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray, only shifted sideways (lateral shift).

  1. What a triangular prism is

A triangular glass prism has:

  • two triangular bases (the ends), and
  • three rectangular lateral faces (the sides).

The lateral faces are inclined to each other — they are not parallel. Light usually enters through one lateral face and leaves through another. The angle between these two lateral faces is called the angle of the prism (∠A). In Fig 10.4 the prism is ABC; light enters face AB and leaves face AC, and ∠A is the angle at the top, between them.

  1. Following a ray through the prism (Activity 10.1, CBSE Practical 13)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Pin a white sheet to a drawing board. Stand a glass prism on one of its triangular bases and trace its outline ABC with a sharp pencil. Draw a straight line PE meeting face AB at E, at an angle to the face. Fix two pins upright at P and Q on this line. Looking through the other face, AC, fix two more pins at R and S so that R, S and the images of the pins at P and Q all appear in one straight line. Remove the pins and the prism. Join and extend the line through S and R to meet face AC at F. Join E to F. Draw the normals to AB at E and to AC at F (dashed). Mark and measure ∠i, ∠r and ∠e. Extend the incident ray forwards and the emergent ray backwards until they meet, and measure the angle between them, ∠D. Repeat for two or three different angles of incidence.
See
At E (air → glass) the ray bends towards the normal: ∠r is smaller than ∠i. At F (glass → air) the ray bends away from the normal. The emergent ray FS is not parallel to the incident ray PE; it leaves turned towards the base BC of the prism.
Shows
A prism bends (deviates) light. The two faces are inclined, so the bending at the second face does not undo the bending at the first — both bendings turn the ray the same way.

The names you must use (Fig 10.4)

Line or angle Name
PE incident ray
EF refracted ray (inside the glass)
FS emergent ray
∠i angle of incidence — between the incident ray and the normal at E
∠r angle of refraction — between the refracted ray and the normal at E
∠e angle of emergence — between the emergent ray and the normal at F
∠A angle of the prism
∠D angle of deviation

  1. The angle of deviation

Because of the prism's shape, the emergent ray leaves at an angle to the direction of the incident ray. This angle is the angle of deviation (∠D). To mark it, extend the incident ray PE forwards (dashed) and the emergent ray FS backwards (dashed); ∠D is the angle between these two lines where they cross.

  1. Prism and slab compared

Rectangular glass slab Triangular glass prism
Refracting faces parallel inclined at ∠A
At the first face (air → glass) bends towards the normal bends towards the normal
At the second face (glass → air) bends away from the normal bends away from the normal
Emergent ray parallel to the incident ray, shifted sideways not parallel — turned through ∠D

The bending at each face follows the same rule in both. Only the shape — whether the faces are parallel — decides whether the ray comes out in its old direction.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Name the rays and angles

Question. In a prism tracing, the prism is XYZ with the angle X at the top. A ray KL strikes face XY at L, travels inside as LM, and leaves face XZ at M along MN. Name (a) the incident, refracted and emergent rays; (b) the angle of the prism; (c) the two lines between which you measure the angle of emergence; (d) the two lines between which you measure the angle of deviation.

Given: prism XYZ; rays KL, LM, MN. Asked: the names of the rays and where each angle is measured.

Concept used: the names in Fig 10.4 (this topic). Why it applies: only the letters have changed.

  1. 1

    (a) KL is the incident ray (before the prism); LM is the refracted ray (inside the glass); MN is the emergent ray (after the prism).

  2. 2

    (b) The angle of the prism is the angle between the two faces the light passes through, XY and XZ → ∠X (∠YXZ).

  3. 3

    (c) Angle of emergence → between the emergent ray MN and the normal to XZ at M.

  4. 4

    (d) Angle of deviation → between the incident ray KL extended forwards and the emergent ray MN extended backwards.

  5. Check: each angle of incidence, refraction or emergence is measured from a normal, never from the face itself ✔.

  6. Answer (a) KL incident, LM refracted, MN emergent. (b) ∠X. (c) MN and the normal at M. (d) KL produced and MN produced backwards.

    Common trap: measuring ∠e from the surface XZ instead of from the normal.

Worked example 2

Which drawing is right?

Question. Four students drew the path of a ray through a glass prism (incident on the left face, base at the bottom). Which drawing is correct?

    • (a) The ray bends towards the normal at the first face, away from the normal at the second, and leaves parallel to the incident ray.
    • (b) The ray bends away from the normal at the first face.
    • (c) The ray bends towards the normal at the first face, away from the normal at the second, and leaves turned towards the base.
    • (d) The ray goes through both faces without bending.

    Given: four drawings. Asked: the correct one, with reasons.

    Concept used: bending at each face, and the prism–slab difference (this topic). Why it applies: each drawing breaks or keeps one rule.

  2. 1

    First face (air → glass) → must bend towards the normal → (b) is wrong.

  3. 2

    Ray hits the face at an angle → it must bend → (d) is wrong.

  4. 3

    Faces inclined → the emergent ray is not parallel to the incident ray → (a) is wrong; that is the slab.

  5. 4

    (c) keeps all the rules: towards, then away, then deviated towards the base.

  6. Check: (c) matches the observations of Activity 10.1 ✔.

  7. Answer (c)

    Common trap: (a) — copying the glass-slab picture onto a prism.

Worked example 3

Same rule, different result

Question. "At each face of both a glass slab and a prism, light bends by the same rule. Yet the slab gives no deviation and the prism does." Explain this statement.

Given: slab and prism, same glass. Asked: why one deviates light and the other does not.

Concept used: prism and slab compared (this topic). Why it applies: the only difference is the angle between the faces.

  1. 1

    Same rule → air → glass: towards the normal; glass → air: away from the normal.

  2. 2

    Slab → the faces are parallel, so the normals at the two faces are parallel. The bending away at the second face exactly reverses the bending towards the normal at the first → emergent ray parallel to the incident ray.

  3. 3

    Prism → the faces are inclined at ∠A, so the normals are not parallel. The second bending does not reverse the first; both turn the ray towards the base → the ray is deviated through ∠D.

  4. Check: a prism with ∠A shrinking to zero would have parallel faces — a slab — and no deviation ✔.

  5. Answer The bending at each face follows the same rule, but in a slab the faces are parallel so the two bendings cancel, while in a prism the faces are inclined, so they add up and the emergent ray is turned through the angle of deviation.

    Common trap: "The prism bends light more because it is thicker." Thickness is not the reason — the angle between the faces is.

Common mistakes!

  • Drawing the emergent ray parallel to the incident ray.

    That is the glass slab, with parallel faces.

    In a prism the emergent ray is turned towards the base.

  • Bending away from the normal at the first face.

    Air → glass: the ray slows down and bends towards the normal.

    First face towards; second face away.

  • Measuring ∠i, ∠r or ∠e from the face.

    These angles are defined from the normal.

    Draw the normal (dashed, at 90°) at each point first.

  • Marking ∠D between the incident ray and the normal.

    ∠D is between the incident and emergent rays.

    Extend both rays (dashed) and mark ∠D where they meet.

  • No arrowheads, or normals drawn freehand.

    Examiners look for the direction of light and correct normals.

    Arrowheads on every ray; ruler for the prism and normals.

Key points

  • A triangular prism has two triangular bases and three rectangular lateral faces; the angle of the prism is between the two lateral faces the light passes through.
  • First face (air → glass): bends towards the normal. Second face (glass → air): bends away from the normal.
  • The faces are inclined, so the emergent ray is not parallel to the incident ray; it is turned towards the base.
  • Angle of deviation (∠D): between the incident ray extended and the emergent ray extended backwards.
  • Names: PE incident, EF refracted, FS emergent; ∠i, ∠r, ∠e measured from the normals.
