Defects of vision and their correction

About 60 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe myopia — what the person sees, far point nearer than infinity, image in front of the retina, two causes, correction with a concave lens; draw Fig 10.2.
  • Describe hypermetropia — near point beyond 25 cm, image behind the retina, two causes, correction with a convex lens; draw Fig 10.3.
  • Describe presbyopia and its cause, bifocal lenses (concave upper part, convex lower part), and other corrections (contact lenses, surgery).
  • Calculate the focal length and power of a corrective lens from a far point, a near point or a given power.

Arjun in the last row cannot read the board, but reads his textbook easily. His grandmother reads the newspaper only at arm's length, but watches television without spectacles. Both "can't see properly" — yet they need opposite kinds of lens. Why?

Quick reminder (Topic 1)

A normal eye sees clearly from its near point (about 25 cm) to its far point (infinity), because the eye lens can change its focal length (accommodation). The lens-to-retina distance stays the same.

When the eye gradually loses some of its power of accommodation, or its shape is not right, the image no longer falls on the retina and vision becomes blurred. These are called refractive defects of vision. There are three common ones. Each can be corrected with a suitable spherical lens.

  1. Myopia (near-sightedness)

  • What the person sees: nearby objects clearly, but distant objects are blurred.
  • Far point: nearer than infinity — the person may see clearly only up to a few metres.
  • Where the image of a distant object falls: in front of the retina (Fig 10.2 b).
  • Causes: (i) excessive curvature of the eye lens, or (ii) elongation of the eyeball.
  • Correction: a concave lens of suitable power (Fig 10.2 c).
  • How the lens helps: a concave lens spreads out (diverges) the parallel rays from a distant object, so that they seem to come from the person's far point O. The myopic eye can focus light from O, so the image moves back onto the retina.

Why does an elongated eyeball or an over-curved lens cause this? Either the lens bends light too strongly, or the retina is too far back — so the rays from a distant object meet before they reach the retina, and then spread again into a blur.

  1. Hypermetropia (far-sightedness)

  • What the person sees: distant objects clearly, but nearby objects are blurred.
  • Near point: farther than the normal 25 cm. Such a person must hold reading material much beyond 25 cm to read comfortably.
  • Where the image of a nearby object falls: behind the retina (Fig 10.3 b).
  • Causes: (i) the focal length of the eye lens is too long, or (ii) the eyeball has become too small.
  • Correction: a convex lens of appropriate power (Fig 10.3 c).
  • How the lens helps: a converging lens provides the additional focusing power the eye lacks. Rays from a book at N′ (25 cm) leave the convex lens as if they came from the person's own near point N, which the eye can focus — so the image falls on the retina.

  1. Presbyopia

  • What happens: the power of accommodation usually decreases with age. The near point gradually recedes (moves farther away), so nearby objects become hard to see comfortably and distinctly without spectacles.
  • Cause: the gradual weakening of the ciliary muscles and the diminishing flexibility of the eye lens.
  • Correction: with spectacles — your textbook says such people need corrective eye-glasses (suitable spherical lenses). It does not name the lens, but the near point has moved away just as in hypermetropia, so the glasses for near vision are convex lenses — like the lower, convex part of a bi-focal lens, which your textbook says facilitates near vision.

Bifocal lenses. Sometimes a person has both myopia and hypermetropia. Such people often need bi-focal lenses. A common type has:

  • an upper part that is a concave lens — for distant vision (looking straight ahead);
  • a lower part that is a convex lens — for near vision (looking down to read).

Today, refractive defects can also be corrected with contact lenses or by surgery.

  1. Calculating the corrective lens

Quick reminder (Chapter 9)

The corrective-lens routine

  1. Decide the defect (far point given → myopia; near point given → hypermetropia or presbyopia).
  2. Write the sign table (uuu, vvv, both negative — in front of the lens).
  3. Find fff (myopia: f=−f = -f=− far point; hypermetropia: lens formula).
  4. Convert fff to metres.
  5. P=1fP = \dfrac{1}{f}P=f1​, with its sign.
  6. Name the lens from the sign (negative → concave; positive → convex).

Worked example 5

Which defect is it?

Question. Mr Rao is 62. He watches cricket on television clearly from across the room, but he now has to hold his newspaper at arm's length to read it. Ten years ago he read it easily. (a) Name his defect. (b) Give its cause. (c) What kind of lens will help him read?

Given: age 62; distant vision fine; near point has moved away with age. Asked: the defect, its cause and the lens.

Concept used: the defect card (this topic). Why it applies: only near vision is affected, and it changed as he grew older.

  1. 1

    What is blurred? → nearby objects (the newspaper).

  2. 2

    Which point moved? → the near point receded, gradually, with age.

  3. 3

    Defect → presbyopia.

  4. 4

    Cause → the ciliary muscles weaken and the eye lens becomes less flexible, so accommodation decreases.

  5. 5

    Lens → reading glasses (your textbook: "corrective eye-glasses"). His near point has moved away, as in hypermetropia, so they are convex lenses, which add converging power for near objects.

  6. Check: the defect card's presbyopia column — nearby objects blurred, near point recedes, convex reading glasses ✔.

  7. Answer (a) Presbyopia. (b) Gradual weakening of the ciliary muscles and reduced flexibility of the eye lens with age. (c) A convex lens.

    Common trap: calling it hypermetropia just because near objects are blurred. The clue is age — the near point moved away gradually as he grew older.

Common mistakes!

  • "Myopia — can't see near things."

    Myopic people see near things clearly; distant objects are blurred.

    Myopia = near-sightedness: sees near, not far.

  • "Convex lens for myopia."

    The myopic eye already converges too strongly; a convex lens makes it worse.

    Myopia → concave; hypermetropia → convex (reading glasses for presbyopia are convex too).

  • "Hypermetropia: image in front of the retina."

    In hypermetropia the eye converges too weakly.

    Myopia → image in front of; hypermetropia → image behind the retina.

  • "The far point of a myopic eye is infinity."

    Infinity is the far point of a normal eye.

    Myopic far point: nearer than infinity.

  • "A convex lens has negative power."

    Converging lenses have positive fff, so positive PPP.

    Convex → +; concave → −.

  • "P=1fP = \dfrac{1}{f}P=f1​ with fff in cm."

    The dioptre is defined with fff in metres.

    Convert fff to metres first.

  • "Hypermetropia: fff = the near point."

    The lens must move the image of a book at 25 cm to the near point.

    Use the lens formula with u=−25u = -25u=−25 cm and v=−(near point)v = -(\text{near point})v=−(near point).

  • "Presbyopia is the same as hypermetropia."

    Presbyopia comes with age, from weakening ciliary muscles and a less flexible lens.

    Look for the clue "with age".

Key points

  • Myopia: distant objects blurred; far point nearer than infinity; image in front of the retina; causes — lens too curved, eyeball elongated; corrected by a concave lens.
  • Hypermetropia: nearby objects blurred; near point farther than 25 cm; image behind the retina; causes — focal length too long, eyeball too small; corrected by a convex lens.
  • Presbyopia: near point recedes with age; ciliary muscles weaken and the lens loses flexibility; corrected with spectacles — convex lenses for near vision (as for hypermetropia; your textbook says "corrective eye-glasses"). Bifocals: upper concave (distant), lower convex (near).
  • Myopia numerical: f=−(far point)f = -(\text{far point})f=−(far point). Hypermetropia numerical: lens formula with u=−25u = -25u=−25 cm, v=−(near point)v = -(\text{near point})v=−(near point).
  • P=1fP = \dfrac{1}{f}P=f1​ with fff in metres; concave → negative power, convex → positive power.
