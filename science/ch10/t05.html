Topic 5 of 6

Atmospheric refraction

About 30 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain the wavering of objects seen through hot air above a fire.
  • Explain why stars twinkle, why a star near the horizon appears higher than it is, and why planets do not twinkle (Fig 10.9).
  • Explain advance sunrise and delayed sunset (about 2 minutes) and the flattened look of the Sun's disc (Fig 10.10).

Look across the top of a bonfire or a hot tandoor at a wall behind it: the wall seems to shiver and wobble. On a clear night, stars flicker brightly and dimly, but a planet such as Jupiter shines with a steady light. And on any day, you can see the Sun a little before it has actually risen. All three come from the same cause: the air bends light.

Quick reminder (Chapter 9)

Light bends (refracts) when it passes from one medium into another with a different refractive index. Going into a medium of higher refractive index, it bends towards the normal. Our eye always traces a ray straight back, so we see an object where the bent ray seems to come from.

Atmospheric refraction is the refraction of light by the earth's atmosphere.

  1. Wavering above a fire (atmospheric refraction on a small scale)

  • A fire (or a radiator) heats the layer of air just above it, so this air is hotter than the air higher up.
  • Hotter air is lighter (less dense) and has a slightly lower refractive index than the cooler air above it.
  • The hot air keeps rising and mixing, so the refracting medium is not stationary — its refractive index keeps changing from moment to moment.
  • So light from an object behind the hot air is bent by a slightly different amount each moment, and the apparent position of the object fluctuates — it seems to waver.

  1. Twinkling of stars

  1. Continuous bending: starlight entering the earth's atmosphere passes through layers whose refractive index changes gradually — the air near the ground is denser. The light is refracted continuously before it reaches us, bending towards the normal.
  2. Apparent position: so a star seems to be at a slightly different position from its real one. A star seen near the horizon appears slightly higher than its actual position (Fig 10.9).
  3. The position keeps changing: the air is never still — its temperature and movement keep changing — so the starlight's path shifts a little from moment to moment, and the star's apparent position keeps jumping about slightly.
  4. Point source: stars are very far away, so each one is practically a point-sized source of light.
  5. Result: as the path shifts, sometimes more and sometimes less of the star's light gets into your eye — the star looks brighter one moment and fainter the next. This is twinkling.

  1. Why planets do not twinkle

  • Planets are far nearer to us than stars, so each one looks like a tiny disc — an extended source, not a point.
  • Imagine the disc as many point sources packed together. Each point flickers, but at any moment some are brighter while others are dimmer.
  • The total variation in the light entering the eye from all of them averages out to zero, so the twinkling effect is cancelled. The planet shines steadily.

  1. Advance sunrise and delayed sunset (Fig 10.10)

  • Actual sunrise means the Sun actually crossing the horizon.
  • When the Sun is still just below the horizon, its light is bent by the atmosphere towards the observer, so the Sun appears to be above the horizon.
  • So we see the Sun about 2 minutes before actual sunrise, and for about 2 minutes after actual sunset.
  • The apparent flattening of the Sun's disc at sunrise and sunset is due to the same effect — atmospheric refraction. (Beyond the syllabus: light from the lower edge of the Sun, nearer the horizon, is bent a little more than light from the upper edge, so the disc looks squashed from top to bottom.)
Not in your syllabus this year

why the Sun looks reddish at sunrise and sunset (that is a scattering effect, and it is excluded from the 2026-27 syllabus). The early sunrise and late sunset you learn here is a refraction effect and is in the syllabus.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

Near the horizon or overhead?

Question. On a clear night Aman looks at two stars, one low near the horizon and one almost directly overhead. (a) Which star's apparent position is shifted more from its real position? (b) In which direction does it appear shifted? Give a reason.

Given: one star near the horizon, one overhead. Asked: which is shifted more, and which way.

Concept used: atmospheric refraction of starlight (this topic). Why it applies: starlight bends as it passes through air whose refractive index increases towards the ground.

  1. 1

    Star near the horizon → its light enters the atmosphere at a slant and travels a long way through layers of changing refractive index → it bends a lot.

  2. 2

    Bending direction → towards the normal, i.e. downwards, curving towards the ground.

  3. 3

    What the eye does → it traces the arriving ray straight back, to a point above the real star.

  4. 4

    Star overhead → its light comes down almost along the normal to the layers, and light along the normal is hardly bent (Chapter 9) → very little shift.

  5. Check: Fig 10.9 draws the star near the horizon and shows the apparent position higher ✔.

  6. Answer (a) The star near the horizon. (b) It appears higher than its real position, because its light bends downwards through the denser lower air and the eye traces the ray straight back.

    Common trap: "The star appears lower." The ray bends down towards us, so the line traced back points up.

Worked example 2

How much longer is the day?

Question. Because of atmospheric refraction, by about how many minutes is the time from apparent sunrise to apparent sunset longer than the time from actual sunrise to actual sunset? Explain.

Given: advance sunrise and delayed sunset. Asked: the extra time in a day.

Concept used: advance sunrise and delayed sunset (this topic). Why it applies: both ends of the day are affected.

  1. 1

    Morning → the Sun is seen about 2 minutes before actual sunrise.

  2. 2

    Evening → the Sun is still seen about 2 minutes after actual sunset.

  3. 3

    Total → 2 minutes + 2 minutes = about 4 minutes.

  4. 4

    Reason → when the Sun is just below the horizon, the atmosphere bends its light towards us, so it appears above the horizon.

  5. Check: both effects add daylight, so the extra time is the sum ✔.

  6. Answer About 4 minutes longer — about 2 minutes at sunrise and 2 minutes at sunset, because the atmosphere bends sunlight from below the horizon towards us.

    Common trap: "We see the Sun early because its light takes time to reach us." That travel time does not make the Sun appear early; the cause is refraction by the atmosphere.

Worked example 3

The shimmering highway

Question. On a hot afternoon, a bus seen across the surface of a hot road appears to shiver. Explain this using refraction.

Given: a very hot road surface heating the air just above it. Asked: why the bus seems to shiver.

Concept used: wavering through hot air (this topic). Why it applies: this is the same small-scale atmospheric refraction as above a fire.

  1. 1

    Hot layer → the air just above the road is hotter than the air higher up.

  2. 2

    Refractive index → hot air is less dense and has a slightly lower refractive index.

  3. 3

    Not stationary → the hot air keeps rising and mixing, so the refractive index along the light's path keeps changing.

  4. 4

    Result → light from the bus is bent by a slightly different amount each moment, so its apparent position fluctuates — it seems to shiver.

  5. Check: it is the same chain as the wall seen across a bonfire ✔.

  6. Answer The air just above the hot road is hotter and less dense, with a slightly lower refractive index, and it keeps moving. Light from the bus is refracted by changing amounts, so the bus's apparent position keeps changing and it seems to shiver.

    Common trap: "The heat makes the bus vibrate." The bus is still; only the path of its light keeps changing.

Common mistakes!

  • "Stars twinkle because of scattering."

    Scattering does not move a star's apparent position.

    Twinkling is due to atmospheric refraction.

  • "Planets don't twinkle because they don't make their own light."

    Their reflected light is bent in the same way.

    Planets are extended sources; the variations average out.

  • "The star is really where we see it."

    The ray is bent on its way down.

    The apparent position is higher than the real one (near the horizon).

  • "We see the Sun early because light takes time to travel."

    Travel time does not make the Sun appear before it rises.

    The cause is refraction by the atmosphere.

  • "The Sun is red at sunrise because of refraction."

    Its colour is a scattering effect — and it is not in this year's syllabus.

    Refraction gives early sunrise, late sunset and the flattened disc.

Key points

  • Atmospheric refraction: refraction of light by the earth's atmosphere, whose refractive index changes gradually and keeps fluctuating.
  • Hot air above a fire has a lower refractive index and keeps moving → objects behind it waver.
  • Stars twinkle: point sources; the path of their light keeps changing → apparent position and brightness flicker. A star near the horizon appears higher than it is.
  • Planets do not twinkle: extended sources; variations from many points average out.
  • Advance sunrise and delayed sunset: the Sun is seen about 2 minutes before actual sunrise and after actual sunset; the Sun's disc looks flattened.
