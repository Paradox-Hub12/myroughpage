Topic 4 of 6

Dispersion, recombination and the rainbow

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe dispersion of white light by a prism, the spectrum and the order VIBGYOR, and explain it by different colours bending through different angles — red least, violet most (Fig 10.5).
  • Describe Newton's two-prism experiment (Fig 10.6) and what white light means.
  • Explain how a rainbow forms — refraction and dispersion, internal reflection, refraction again — and why it is seen opposite the Sun (Figs 10.7, 10.8).

A glass prism on a sunny window-sill throws a strip of colours on the wall. Where did the colours come from — the glass, or the sunlight? And why does a spray of water from a garden hose, with the Sun behind you, show the same colours in the same order as a rainbow?

Quick reminder (Topic 3)

A prism bends a ray of light at each face and turns it towards its base through the angle of deviation.

  1. White light splits into colours (Activity 10.2)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Make a narrow slit in a thick sheet of cardboard and let sunlight fall on it, to get a narrow beam of white light. Let this beam fall on one face of a glass prism. Rotate the prism gently until the light leaving it lands on a screen placed close by.
See
A beautiful band of colours appears on the screen. From the end bent least to the end bent most, the colours are Red, Orange, Yellow, Green, Blue, Indigo, Violet. Read from the violet end, the letters spell VIBGYOR — the textbook's own way to remember the order.
Shows
White light (sunlight) is made of several colours, and the prism separates them.

Two words to learn

  • Spectrum: the band of the coloured components of a light beam.
  • Dispersion: the splitting of white light into its component colours.

You may not be able to pick out all seven colours separately — the band changes gradually — but each colour is distinct from the others.

  1. Why the colours separate

When white light passes through a prism, different colours bend through different angles with respect to the incident ray:

  • red light bends the least;
  • violet light bends the most;
  • the other colours lie in between, in the order of VIBGYOR.

So each colour leaves the prism along a different path, and the colours land at different places on the screen. With the prism standing on its base, as in Fig 10.5, violet is bent most towards the base and appears at the bottom of the spectrum, and red appears at the top.

The prism does not add any colour. The colours were already in the white light; the prism only sends them in different directions.

  1. Newton's two prisms — putting white light back together (Fig 10.6)

Isaac Newton was the first person to get the spectrum of sunlight with a glass prism.

  1. He tried to split a colour of the spectrum further with another similar prism. He got no more colours.
  2. Next, he set a second, identical prism upside down (inverted) beside the first, so that every colour of the spectrum went into it.
  3. What came out of this second prism was a beam of white light.

The inverted prism bends each colour back by the same amount the first prism bent it, so the colours recombine into white light. From this Newton concluded that sunlight is a mixture of seven colours.

White light

any light that gives a spectrum similar to that of sunlight.

  1. The rainbow — a spectrum in the sky (Figs 10.7, 10.8)

A rainbow is a spectrum made by nature, seen in the sky after a shower of rain. Countless tiny water droplets hanging in the air disperse sunlight; each droplet behaves like a small prism. Inside each raindrop, sunlight is:

  1. refracted and dispersed as it enters the drop;
  2. reflected internally at the back of the drop;
  3. refracted again as it comes out of the drop.

Thanks to the dispersion and the internal reflection, each colour comes out of the drops at its own angle, so your eye receives different colours from different parts of the sky.

  • A rainbow is always formed in the direction opposite to the Sun — the Sun must be behind you.
  • On a sunny day you can make the same thing happen with the spray of a waterfall or a fountain — stand with the Sun at your back.

The rainbow chain (write it like this): sunlight enters a raindrop → refraction + dispersion → internal reflection at the back → refraction on leaving → colours spread out → seen opposite the Sun.

Worked example 1

Reading a spectrum

Question. Kabir gets a spectrum on a screen from a prism standing on its base. (a) Which colour is nearest the base end of the spectrum? (b) Which colour is bent least? (c) Why do the colours separate at all?

Given: prism on its base; spectrum on a screen. Asked: the colour near the base; the least-bent colour; the reason.

Concept used: different colours bend through different angles (this topic). Why it applies: the spectrum is spread out by how much each colour bends.

  1. 1

    (a) A prism turns light towards its base; violet bends the most → violet is nearest the base end (the bottom).

  2. 2

    (b) Red bends the least → it is at the top.

  3. 3

    (c) Each colour bends through a different angle, so each leaves the prism along a different path.

  4. Check: the order from the top is R, O, Y, G, B, I, V — VIBGYOR read from the bottom ✔.

  5. Answer (a) violet (b) red (c) different colours bend through different angles, so they leave along different paths.

    Common trap: "Violet bends least because it is at the end." Violet is at the end nearest the base because it bends most.

Worked example 2

Two prisms, two results

Question. Shreya passes the green part of a spectrum through a second prism and sees only green. Then she places a second identical prism upside down right next to the first and sees white light come out. Explain both results.

Given: green light through a second prism; then an inverted identical prism. Asked: why no new colours in the first case; why white light in the second.

Concept used: Newton's two-prism experiments (this topic). Why it applies: these are Newton's two observations.

  1. 1

    First result → a prism only separates colours already present; green from the spectrum is a single colour, so no further colours appear.

  2. 2

    Second result → the inverted prism bends each colour back by the same amount it was bent by the first.

  3. 3

    So all seven colours come out along the same path again → they recombine into white light.

  4. Check: both results agree that white light is made of colours and that a prism adds none ✔.

  5. Answer A prism cannot split a single colour any further, so green stays green. An identical prism placed upside down reverses the bending of every colour, so the colours recombine into white light.

    Common trap: thinking the second prism "makes" white light. It only brings the separated colours back together.

Worked example 3

Where to look for a rainbow

Question. At 8 a.m. the Sun is low in the east. Meera sprays a fine mist of water with a garden hose. In which direction should she face to see a rainbow in the spray? Which three things happen to sunlight inside each droplet?

Given: Sun in the east; water droplets in the air. Asked: which way to face; the three steps.

Concept used: the rainbow forms opposite the Sun (this topic). Why it applies: the spray acts like a rain shower.

  1. 1

    Direction → the rainbow is opposite the Sun → face the west, with the Sun behind her.

  2. 2

    Step 1 → sunlight is refracted and dispersed as it enters a droplet.

  3. 3

    Step 2 → it is reflected internally at the back of the droplet.

  4. 4

    Step 3 → it is refracted again as it leaves the droplet, and the colours spread out towards her eye.

  5. Check: east and west are opposite directions, so the Sun is behind her ✔.

  6. Answer She should face west, away from the Sun. In each droplet sunlight is refracted and dispersed, reflected internally, and refracted again on leaving.

    Common trap: looking towards the Sun for a rainbow, or writing "total internal reflection" — your textbook says internal reflection.

Common mistakes!

  • "Violet bends the least."

    The order of bending is red least, violet most.

    Red least, violet most.

  • Writing the colours in the wrong order, or reversing them on the screen.

    In Fig 10.5, violet is nearest the base.

    Learn VIBGYOR; violet nearest the base.

  • "The prism colours the light."

    A second prism adds no new colours; an inverted one gives white light back.

    The colours are already in white light; the prism separates them.

  • "A rainbow forms towards the Sun."

    Your textbook: a rainbow is always formed in a direction opposite to that of the Sun.

    A rainbow is always opposite the Sun — Sun behind you.

  • "Rainbow: total internal reflection" or "dispersion only".

    Your textbook names three steps.

    Refraction + dispersion → internal reflection → refraction.

  • "Dispersion is the bending of light."

    Bending is refraction; dispersion is the splitting into colours.

    Dispersion = splitting of white light into its component colours.

Key points

  • Dispersion: the splitting of white light into its component colours. Spectrum: the band of colours — VIBGYOR.
  • Colours separate because each bends through a different angle: red least, violet most.
  • Newton: a second similar prism gives no new colours; an identical inverted prism recombines the spectrum into white light. White light = any light giving a spectrum like sunlight.
  • Rainbow: tiny water droplets act like prisms — refraction and dispersion → internal reflection → refraction again; always seen opposite the Sun.
