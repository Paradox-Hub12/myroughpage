The whole chapter on one page

The human eye — a ball about 2.3 cm across, like a camera. Light enters through the cornea (most refraction happens at its outer surface); the iris controls the size of the pupil, which controls the amount of light; the crystalline lens makes the fine adjustment of focal length; a real, inverted image forms on the retina, whose light-sensitive cells send electrical signals through the optic nerve to the brain. Accommodation: ciliary muscles relaxed → thin lens → longer focal length (distant objects); contracted → thicker lens → shorter focal length (near objects). Near point (least distance of distinct vision) ≈ 25 cm; far point = infinity; a normal eye sees clearly between them. Cataract: the lens becomes milky and cloudy in old age; surgery restores vision.

Defects — Myopia (near-sightedness): distant objects blurred; far point nearer than infinity; image in front of the retina; causes — lens too curved, eyeball elongated; concave lens. Hypermetropia (far-sightedness): nearby objects blurred; near point beyond 25 cm; image behind the retina; causes — focal length too long, eyeball too small; convex lens. Presbyopia: near point recedes with age (ciliary muscles weaken, lens less flexible); corrected with spectacles — convex lenses for near vision (as in hypermetropia), or bi-focals. Bifocals: upper concave (distant), lower convex (near). Contact lenses and surgery can also correct defects.

Prism — two triangular bases, three rectangular lateral faces; angle of the prism between two lateral faces. A ray bends towards the normal entering and away from it leaving; the faces are inclined, so the emergent ray is deviated through the angle of deviation (not parallel, as in a slab).

Dispersion — splitting of white light into its colours; spectrum VIBGYOR; red bends least, violet most. Newton: a second similar prism gives no new colours; an identical inverted prism recombines them into white light. Rainbow: droplets act like prisms — refraction and dispersion → internal reflection → refraction; always opposite the Sun.

Atmospheric refraction — hot air above a fire makes objects waver. Stars twinkle: starlight is refracted by air of gradually changing, fluctuating refractive index; stars are point sources, so position and brightness flicker; near the horizon a star appears higher. Planets are extended sources — the flicker averages out. The Sun is seen about 2 minutes before actual sunrise and after actual sunset; its disc looks flattened.

Scattering — Tyndall effect: colloidal particles (smoke, mist, dust) scatter light and make a beam's path visible. Very fine particles scatter mainly blue; larger ones longer wavelengths; large enough → white. Blue sky: air molecules, smaller than the wavelength, scatter blue more than red. Dark sky without atmosphere or very high up. Red danger signals: red is scattered least by fog and smoke. (The colour of the Sun at sunrise and sunset is not in this year's syllabus.)

Key definitions

(your own words are fine — just keep every key term)

  • Power of accommodation: the ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length.
  • Near point (least distance of distinct vision): the minimum distance at which an object is seen most distinctly without strain — about 25 cm.
  • Far point: the farthest point up to which the eye sees clearly — infinity for a normal eye.
  • Myopia / hypermetropia / presbyopia: see the summary above.
  • Angle of the prism: the angle between the two lateral faces through which light passes.
  • Angle of deviation: the angle between the incident ray and the emergent ray.
  • Dispersion: the splitting of white light into its component colours.
  • Spectrum: the band of the coloured components of a light beam.
  • White light: any light that gives a spectrum similar to that of sunlight.
  • Atmospheric refraction: the refraction of light by the earth's atmosphere.
  • Tyndall effect: scattering of light by colloidal particles, which makes the path of a beam visible.
