Topic 1 of 6

The human eye and accommodation

About 35 minutes · 4 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Name the parts of the eye in Fig 10.1 and state the job of the cornea, iris, pupil, crystalline lens, ciliary muscles, retina and optic nerve.
  • Describe how the eye forms a real, inverted image on the retina, that most refraction happens at the outer surface of the cornea, and how signals reach the brain.
  • Explain accommodation — ciliary muscles relaxed, thin lens, longer focal length for distant objects; contracted, thicker lens, shorter focal length for near objects.
  • Define the near point (about 25 cm) and far point (infinity) of a normal eye, state the range of vision, and describe cataract.

Look at something far away — a tree, a building, the sky. Now look at your fingertip held about 30 cm from your face. Both are sharp, one after the other, and you did nothing on purpose. A camera has to move its lens to do this. How does your eye manage it without moving anything?

Quick reminder (Chapter 9)

A convex lens brings parallel rays together. When an object is far beyond its focus, a convex lens forms a real, inverted image on the other side — the kind of image you can catch on a screen.

  1. The eye works like a camera

Your eye is a ball about 2.3 cm across. Like a camera, it has a lens system at the front and a light-sensitive screen at the back. The screen is the retina. Light from an object passes through the front of the eye, is bent (refracted), and forms an image on the retina. The retina turns that image into electrical signals for the brain.

  1. The parts and their jobs (Fig 10.1)

Follow the light from the front of the eye to the back.

Part What it is Its job
Cornea A thin, transparent membrane that bulges out at the front of the eyeball Light enters the eye here. Most of the bending (refraction) of light happens at the outer surface of the cornea.
Iris A dark, muscular diaphragm just behind the cornea Controls the size of the pupil.
Pupil The opening in the middle of the iris Regulates and controls how much light enters the eye.
Crystalline lens (eye lens) A lens made of a fibrous, jelly-like material Makes the fine adjustment of focal length so that objects at different distances are focused on the retina.
Ciliary muscles Muscles holding the eye lens Change the curvature of the eye lens, and so its focal length.
Retina A thin, delicate layer lining the back of the eye, packed with huge numbers of light-sensitive cells The screen on which the image forms. When light falls on its cells, they send out electrical signals.
Optic nerve The nerve leaving the back of the eye Carries the signals to the brain.

Your textbook's figure also labels the aqueous humour (in front of the lens) and the vitreous humour (filling the eyeball behind the lens). It does not describe what they do, so learning where they are is enough.

  1. The image on the retina

The eye lens system is converging, and the object is far beyond its focus, so the image on the retina is real and inverted (upside down). The light-sensitive cells send signals through the optic nerve; the brain interprets them, so we see objects as they really are — upright.

Remember the order of the light's journey

cornea → (aqueous humour) → pupil → eye lens → (vitreous humour) → retina → optic nerve → brain.

  1. Power of accommodation

A camera focuses by moving its lens. Your eye cannot move its lens backwards and forwards, and the distance from the lens to the retina stays the same. Instead, it changes the focal length of the eye lens.

  • Looking at a distant object → the ciliary muscles relax → the eye lens becomes thin (less curved) → its focal length increases → the distant object is focused on the retina.
  • Looking at a nearby object → the ciliary muscles contract → the curvature of the eye lens increases and it becomes thicker → its focal length decreases → the nearby object is focused on the retina.
Accommodation

is the ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length so that both near and distant objects are seen clearly.

Memory hook (a picture, not a rule): relaxed and thin for things far away; working hard and thick for things up close.

  1. Near point and far point

The eye lens cannot become thicker without limit — its focal length cannot be made smaller than a certain minimum. That is why a page held right against your nose is blurred, or makes your eyes strain.

  • Near point (also called the least distance of distinct vision): the minimum distance at which an object can be seen most distinctly without strain. For a young adult with normal vision it is about 25 cm.
  • Far point: the most distant point that the eye can still see sharply. For a normal eye it is infinity — even very distant objects are clear.

So a normal eye sees sharply from about 25 cm all the way out to infinity.

  1. Cataract

In old age the crystalline lens sometimes becomes milky and cloudy. This is called cataract. Less light gets through clearly, causing partial or complete loss of vision. Vision can be restored by cataract surgery.

Did you know?Not in board exam

Eyes can be donated after death. A donated cornea can restore sight to a person with corneal blindness, and one pair of eyes can give vision to up to four such people. People of any age can pledge their eyes, including those who wear spectacles or have had cataract surgery. The eyes must be removed within a few hours of death, so the family informs the nearest eye bank quickly.

Worked examples

Worked example 1

From a hoarding to a phone

Question. Riya is reading a hoarding across the road. She then looks down at a message on her phone, held about 35 cm from her eyes. What happens to (a) her ciliary muscles, (b) the shape of her eye lens and (c) its focal length? Why must this happen?

Given: first a distant object (the hoarding), then a near object (the phone at 35 cm). Asked: what the muscles, lens shape and focal length do, and why.

Concept used: accommodation (this topic). Why it applies: the same eye must focus first a distant and then a near object on the retina.

  1. 1

    For the hoarding → the ciliary muscles were relaxed and the lens was thin, with a longer focal length.

  2. 2

    For the phone → the ciliary muscles contract.

  3. 3

    Lens shape → the curvature of the eye lens increases; the lens becomes thicker.

  4. 4

    Focal length → a more curved lens has a shorter focal length, so the focal length decreases.

  5. 5

    Why → the retina stays at the same distance from the lens, so only a change in focal length can bring the image of the nearer phone onto the retina.

  6. Check: 35 cm is farther than the near point (about 25 cm), so a normal eye can focus it ✔.

  7. Answer (a) The ciliary muscles contract. (b) The eye lens becomes thicker (more curved). (c) Its focal length decreases. This keeps the image of the near object on the retina.

    Common trap: "The muscles relax for near objects." It is the other way round — relaxed for far, contracted for near.

Worked example 2

Name the part from its job

Question. Name the part of the eye that (a) controls the size of the pupil, (b) is the screen on which the image forms, (c) carries signals to the brain, (d) does most of the bending of light.

Given: four jobs. Asked: the part that does each.

Concept used: parts of the eye and their jobs (this topic). Why it applies: each job belongs to one part.

  1. 1

    (a) The iris is a muscular diaphragm that controls the size of the pupil.

  2. 2

    (b) The retina is the light-sensitive screen.

  3. 3

    (c) The optic nerve carries the electrical signals.

  4. 4

    (d) Most refraction happens at the outer surface of the cornea — not at the eye lens.

  5. Check: the order of the light's journey is cornea → pupil → lens → retina → optic nerve, and each answer fits its place ✔.

  6. Answer (a) iris (b) retina (c) optic nerve (d) cornea (its outer surface)

    Common trap: (d) "the eye lens". The eye lens only makes the fine adjustment; the cornea does most of the bending.

Worked example 3

Inside or outside the range?

Question. A young adult has normal vision. Which of these can she see clearly without strain? (a) A word printed on a ticket held 12 cm from her eyes. (b) A clock on a wall 6 m away. (c) The Moon.

Given: normal eye; objects at 12 cm, 6 m and very far away. Asked: which are seen clearly without strain.

Concept used: near point and far point of a normal eye (this topic). Why it applies: a normal eye sees clearly between its near point and its far point.

  1. 1

    Range of a normal eye → from the near point (about 25 cm) to the far point (infinity).

  2. 2

    (a) 12 cm is closer than 25 cm → outside the range → not clear without strain.

  3. 3

    (b) 6 m is between 25 cm and infinity → clear.

  4. 4

    (c) The Moon is very far away — practically at infinity, the far point → clear.

  5. Check: 12 cm < 25 cm, and 6 m = 600 cm > 25 cm ✔.

  6. Answer She sees (b) and (c) clearly. The ticket at 12 cm is closer than her near point, so it looks blurred or strains her eyes.

    Common trap: "Infinity is too far to see." The far point of a normal eye is infinity — distant objects are seen with the eye fully relaxed.

Common mistakes!

  • "The ciliary muscles relax to see near objects."

    Relaxed muscles leave the lens thin, which suits distant objects.

    Relaxed → thin → far. Contracted → thick → near.

  • "A thicker lens has a longer focal length."

    A more curved lens bends light more, so its focal length is shorter.

    Thicker (more curved) → shorter focal length.

  • "The near point is 25 m" or "2.5 cm".

    It is about 25 cm for a young adult with normal vision.

    Learn it with its unit: 25 cm.

  • "The far point of a normal eye is 25 cm."

    25 cm is the near point.

    Far point of a normal eye = infinity.

  • "The pupil (or iris) forms the image."

    The pupil is an opening; the iris controls its size.

    The cornea and eye lens form the image on the retina.

  • "Most bending happens at the eye lens."

    The eye lens only fine-tunes the focus.

    Most refraction happens at the outer surface of the cornea.

  • "The image on the retina is upright."

    A converging lens system forms a real, inverted image.

    Image on retina: real and inverted; the brain interprets it.

Key points

  • The eye is like a camera: the cornea and eye lens form a real, inverted image on the retina; the optic nerve carries signals to the brain.
  • Most refraction happens at the outer surface of the cornea; the eye lens makes the fine adjustment.
  • The iris controls the size of the pupil, which controls how much light enters.
  • Accommodation: ciliary muscles relaxed → thin lens → longer focal length (distant objects); contracted → thicker lens → shorter focal length (near objects).
  • Near point ≈ 25 cm; far point = infinity; a normal eye sees clearly between them.
  • Cataract: the eye lens becomes milky and cloudy in old age; cataract surgery can restore vision.
