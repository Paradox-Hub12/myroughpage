Topic 7 of 7

Electric power and electrical energy

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Define electric power and the watt and use P = VI = I²R = V²/R (interrelation of P, V, I and R), including power ratings such as "220 V, 60 W".
  • Calculate electrical energy in J, W h and kW h (1 kW h = 3.6 × 10⁶ J) and the cost of electricity.
  • Choose a fuse rating from an appliance's power and voltage.

Your electricity bill does not charge you for "current" or for "volts". It charges you for units — and a geyser running for half an hour can use more units than a ceiling fan running all evening. What is a "unit", and how do you work out the bill?

Quick reminder (Class 9, Work and Energy). Power is the rate of doing work — also the rate at which energy is used. Its unit is the watt (W): 1 W = 1 J/s; 1 kW = 1000 W.

  1. Electric power

In Topic 6, the energy supplied in time ttt was VItVItVIt. Dividing by ttt gives the rate of using (dissipating) electric energy — the electric power:

P=VIP = VIP=VISwipe to see the whole equation →

Using Ohm's law (V=IRV = IRV=IR, or I=VRI = \dfrac{V}{R}I=RV​), the same power can be written in two more ways:

P=VI=I2R=V2RP = VI = I^2 R = \dfrac{V^2}{R}P=VI=I2R=RV2​Swipe to see the whole equation →

These three forms are the interrelation between P, V, I and R. Choose the one that uses the quantities you are given:

You know Use
VVV and III P=VIP = VIP=VI
III and RRR P=I2RP = I^2RP=I2R
VVV and RRR P=V2RP = \dfrac{V^2}{R}P=RV2​

The SI unit of electric power is the watt (W) — the power used by a device that carries 1 A when operated at a p.d. of 1 V:

1 W=1 V×1 A=1 V A1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{V} \times 1\ \text{A} = 1\ \text{V A}1 W=1 V×1 A=1 V ASwipe to see the whole equation →

The watt is small, so in practice we often use the kilowatt (1 kW = 1000 W).

  1. Power ratings

An appliance is labelled with its rating — for example "220 V, 60 W". This means it uses 60 W when connected to 220 V. From the rating you can find:

  • its normal current: I=PVI = \dfrac{P}{V}I=VP​;
  • its resistance: R=V2PR = \dfrac{V^2}{P}R=PV2​ (taken as constant).

If the same appliance is run on a different p.d., and we take its resistance as unchanged (as textbook problems do), its power changes: P=V2RP = \dfrac{V^2}{R}P=RV2​. Half the p.d. gives one-quarter of the power. So a "100 W" bulb uses 100 W only at its rated voltage.

  1. Electrical energy and the "unit"

Electrical energy used = power × time:

E=P×tE = P \times tE=P×tSwipe to see the whole equation →
  • In joules: PPP in W, ttt in s.
  • In watt hours (W h): PPP in W, ttt in h. 1 W h is the energy of 1 W kept up for 1 hour.
  • In kilowatt hours (kW h): PPP in kW, ttt in h. The kilowatt hour is the commercial unit of electrical energy — the "unit" on your bill.
1 kW h=1000 W×3600 s=3.6×106 W s=3.6×106 J1\ \text{kW h} = 1000\ \text{W} \times 3600\ \text{s} = 3.6 \times 10^6\ \text{W s} = 3.6 \times 10^6\ \text{J}1 kW h=1000 W×3600 s=3.6×106 W s=3.6×106 JSwipe to see the whole equation →

Cost of electricity = number of units (kW h) × price per unit.

Did you know?Not in board exam

Many people think that electrons are "used up" in a circuit. They are not. We pay the electricity company for the energy it supplies to move electrons through our bulbs, fans and motors — not for the electrons.

  1. Choosing a fuse

The fuse (Topic 6) must let the normal current through but melt if the current rises well above it. So:

  1. find the normal current: I=PVI = \dfrac{P}{V}I=VP​;
  2. choose the standard rating just above it (ratings 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 5 A, 10 A …).

Common mistakes!

  • "kW h is a unit of power."

    kW × h is power × time = energy.

    kW h = the commercial unit of energy.

  • Multiplying watts by hours and calling it "units".

    1 unit = 1 kW h.

    Convert W to kW (÷ 1000) first.

  • "A 100 W bulb always uses 100 W."

    100 W is the power at its rated voltage.

    At another p.d., P=V2RP = \dfrac{V^2}{R}P=RV2​.

  • "The 100 W bulb has the larger resistance."

    R=V2PR = \dfrac{V^2}{P}R=PV2​ — more power, less resistance (same VVV).

    Work it out: 20 W → 2420 Ω; 100 W → 484 Ω.

  • "P=IR2P = IR^2P=IR2".

    Only VIVIVI, I2RI^2RI2R and V2R\dfrac{V^2}{R}RV2​ are correct.

    Check units: A² × Ω = W.

  • Choosing a fuse below the normal current.

    It would melt in normal use.

    Normal current PV\dfrac{P}{V}VP​, then the next rating above.

Key points

  • Electric power = rate of using electrical energy: P=VI=I2R=V2RP = VI = I^2R = \dfrac{V^2}{R}P=VI=I2R=RV2​. Unit watt: 1 W = 1 V × 1 A; 1 kW = 1000 W.
  • A rating "V, P" gives the current I=PVI = \dfrac{P}{V}I=VP​ and the resistance R=V2PR = \dfrac{V^2}{P}R=PV2​; at another p.d. the power changes as V2V^2V2.
  • Energy E=PtE = PtE=Pt: J (W × s), W h, or kW h (the commercial "unit"); 1 kW h = 3.6 × 10⁶ J. Cost = units × rate.
  • Fuse choice: normal current PV\dfrac{P}{V}VP​, then the standard rating just above it.
