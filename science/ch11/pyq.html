CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section C, Q34 — Option A2 marks

Question:
I. State Joule's Law of heating.
II. What happens to the heat produced in a conductor if the resistance is doubled while keeping other variables constant?

Given: two parts on the heating effect. Asked: Joule's law; the effect of doubling RRR.

Concept used: Joule's law, H=I2RtH = I^2RtH=I2Rt (Topic 6). Why it applies: part II is one of the law's three proportionalities.

  1. 1

    I → the heat produced in a resistor is directly proportional to (i) the square of the current for a given resistance, (ii) the resistance for a given current, (iii) the time for which the current flows: H=I2RtH = I^2RtH=I2Rt.

  2. 2

    II → with III and ttt the same, H∝RH \propto RH∝R, so doubling RRR doubles the heat.

  3. Check: I2(2R)t=2×I2RtI^2(2R)t = 2 \times I^2RtI2(2R)t=2×I2Rt ✔.

    Common trap: using H=V2RtH = \dfrac{V^2}{R}tH=RV2​t in II and answering "halves". The question keeps the other variables — current and time — constant, so use H=I2RtH = I^2RtH=I2Rt.
I. Joule's law of heating: the heat produced in a resistor is (i) directly proportional to the square of the current for a given resistance, (ii) directly proportional to the resistance for a given current, and (iii) directly proportional to the time for which the current flows: H=I2RtH = I^2RtH=I2Rt. (1)
II. For the same current and time, H=I2(2R)t=2I2RtH = I^2(2R)t = 2I^2RtH=I2(2R)t=2I2Rt — the heat doubles. (1)
2 marks
CBSE SQP 2026-27, Section C, Q34 — Option B2 marks

Question:
I. Define resistivity.
II. Justify with an example why resistivity is an intrinsic property of a material.

Given: two parts on resistivity. Asked: the definition; why it belongs to the material.

Concept used: R=ρlAR = \rho\dfrac{l}{A}R=ρAl​ (Topic 4). Why it applies: rearranged, ρ=RAl\rho = \dfrac{RA}{l}ρ=lRA​ cancels out the size of the wire.

  1. 1

    I → resistivity ρ\rhoρ is the resistance of a wire of the material of unit length and unit area of cross-section, from R=ρlAR = \rho\dfrac{l}{A}R=ρAl​ (unit Ω m).

  2. 2

    II → take two copper wires of different lengths and thicknesses. Their resistances differ, but RAl\dfrac{RA}{l}lRA​ works out to the same value for both — it depends only on the material (at a given temperature), not on the size or shape.

  3. Check: your textbook calls resistivity "a characteristic property of the material" ✔.

    Common trap: saying that resistivity depends on length or area. Those change the resistance.
I. Resistivity (ρ\rhoρ) of a material is the resistance of a wire of that material having unit length and unit area of cross-section; R=ρLAR = \rho\dfrac{L}{A}R=ρAL​. (1)
II. Resistivity depends only on the material, not on the dimensions. Example: two copper wires of different lengths and thicknesses give the same ρ\rhoρ when calculated as RAL\dfrac{RA}{L}LRA​. (1)
2 marks

Your textbook adds that resistivity changes with temperature, so "depends only on the material" means at a given temperature.
