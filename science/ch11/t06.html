Topic 6 of 7

Heating effect of electric current

About 35 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain where the energy of the source goes and derive H = VIt and H = I²Rt; state Joule's law of heating (three proportionalities).
  • Calculate the heat produced in a resistor.
  • Explain the applications — heating appliances, the bulb filament (tungsten; inert gas; thermal isolation) and the fuse (in series; melts; ratings) — and why a heater's cord does not glow.

A phone charger feels warm after an hour. A fan's motor gets warm after a long run. An electric iron gets hot on purpose. In all three, the same thing is happening: some of the electrical energy is turning into heat. Sometimes we want it; sometimes we do not.

Quick reminder (Topic 2)

To keep a current flowing, the cell or battery must keep spending its energy: moving a charge QQQ through a p.d. VVV takes work W=VQW = VQW=VQ.

  1. Where does the source's energy go?

A cell's chemical action keeps a p.d. across its terminals, and this sets the electrons moving through the resistors joined to it. The source keeps spending energy to maintain the current.

  • Part of this energy may do useful work — for example, turning the blades of a fan.
  • The rest may become heat that raises the temperature of the gadget — which is why a fan warms up after long use.
  • If the circuit is purely resistive (only resistors joined to the source), all the energy from the source is turned into heat.

This production of heat by a current is called the heating effect of electric current. Electric heaters and irons are built to use it.

  1. How much heat? — Joule's law of heating

Let a steady current III flow for a time ttt through a resistor RRR, with a p.d. VVV across it (Fig 11.13). In that time a charge Q=ItQ = ItQ=It moves through the p.d. VVV, so the work done (energy supplied) is VQVQVQ:

H=VQ=V×It⇒H=VItH = VQ = V \times It \quad\Rightarrow\quad H = VItH=VQ=V×It⇒H=VItSwipe to see the whole equation →

Using Ohm's law, V=IRV = IRV=IR:

H=I2RtH = I^2 R tH=I2RtSwipe to see the whole equation →

This is Joule's law of heating. It says that the heat produced in a resistor is:

  1. directly proportional to the square of the current for a given resistance (H∝I2H \propto I^2H∝I2);
  2. directly proportional to the resistance for a given current (H∝RH \propto RH∝R);
  3. directly proportional to the time for which the current flows (H∝tH \propto tH∝t).

Heat is in joules (J) when III is in A, RRR in Ω and ttt in seconds. If you know the supply voltage, work out the current first with I=VRI = \dfrac{V}{R}I=RV​, and then use H=I2RtH = I^2RtH=I2Rt.

Because of the square, doubling the current gives four times the heat; three times the current gives nine times the heat.

  1. Useful applications

  • Heating appliances: the electric laundry iron, toaster, oven, kettle and room heater all use Joule heating.
  • The electric bulb: the filament has to hold on to its heat, so that it becomes white-hot and glows — but it must not melt. So it is made of a strong metal with a high melting point, tungsten (melting point 3380 °C). The filament is thermally isolated as far as possible, using insulating supports. The bulb is filled with chemically inactive nitrogen and argon gases to make the filament last longer. Even so, most of the power consumed appears as heat, and only a small part is radiated as light.
  • The electric fuse: see The electric fuse below.

  1. Unwanted heating

Heat is produced whenever there is a current in a conductor — it cannot be avoided. In many cases this is undesirable: it turns useful electrical energy into heat, and it can raise the temperature of the parts of a circuit and change their properties.

Why the element glows but the cord does not. The heater's element and its connecting cord carry the same current (they are in series). The element has a high resistance (an alloy such as nichrome), while the copper cord has a very low resistance. Since H=I2RtH = I^2RtH=I2Rt, with the same III and ttt almost all the heat is produced in the element — it becomes red hot while the cord stays nearly cool.

  1. The electric fuse

A fuse protects circuits and appliances by stopping any unduly high current.

  • It is a short wire of a metal or alloy whose melting point suits the job (for example aluminium, copper, iron or lead).
  • It is joined in series with the appliance it guards.
  • If a current larger than its rated value flows, the fuse wire heats up (H=I2RtH = I^2RtH=I2Rt), melts and breaks the circuit — so the current stops before the appliance is damaged.
  • The wire usually sits inside a porcelain cartridge (or one of a similar material) capped with metal ends.
  • Fuses for home use are rated 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 5 A, 10 A, and so on. The rating must be a little above the normal current of the appliance (Topic 7 shows how to choose it).

(Overloading, short-circuiting and the main fuse of a house are studied in Chapter 12.)

Worked example 4

Filament and leads

Question. In a lamp, the tungsten filament glows white-hot, while the copper wires joined to it stay cool. Explain why, and give two features of the bulb that help the filament.

Given: the filament and the leads carry the same current. Asked: why only the filament glows; two helpful features.

Concept used: H=I2RtH = I^2RtH=I2Rt; the bulb filament (this topic). Why it applies: same current, very different resistances.

  1. 1

    Same current → the filament and the leads are in series.

  2. 2

    Different resistance → the thin tungsten filament has a high resistance; the copper leads have a very low resistance.

  3. 3

    Joule's law → with the same III and ttt, H∝RH \propto RH∝R, so almost all the heat is produced in the filament.

  4. 4

    Features → tungsten has a very high melting point (3380 °C), so it can glow without melting; the bulb is filled with nitrogen and argon (chemically inactive), which makes the filament last longer; the filament is thermally isolated by insulating supports so that it keeps its heat.

  5. Check: the same reasoning explains the heater element and its cord ✔.

  6. Answer The filament has a much higher resistance than the leads; with the same current, H=I2RtH = I^2RtH=I2Rt puts most of the heat in the filament. Helpful features: tungsten's high melting point; inert nitrogen and argon in the bulb (any two, or thermal isolation).

    Common trap: "The filament gets more current." The current is the same — it is in series with the leads.

Common mistakes!

  • "Double the current, double the heat."

    H∝I2H \propto I^2H∝I2.

    Double the current → four times the heat.

  • Time in minutes or hours in H=I2RtH = I^2RtH=I2Rt.

    The formula gives joules only with seconds.

    min × 60; h × 3600.

  • "The element glows because it carries more current than the cord."

    Element and cord are in series — the same current.

    The element has the higher resistance, so more heat.

  • "A fuse is connected across the appliance."

    It would not stop the appliance's current.

    Fuse in series with the device.

  • "Fuse wire is made of a metal with a very high melting point."

    It must melt when the current is too large.

    A metal or alloy of suitable (fairly low) melting point.

  • "A bigger fuse rating is always safer."

    A rating far above the normal current would not melt in time.

    A rating a little above the normal current.

Key points

  • In a purely resistive circuit, all the energy from the source becomes heat — the heating effect of electric current.
  • H=VIt=I2RtH = VIt = I^2RtH=VIt=I2Rt (Joule's law of heating): H∝I2H \propto I^2H∝I2, H∝RH \propto RH∝R, H∝tH \propto tH∝t. Units: J, A, Ω, s.
  • Uses: iron, toaster, oven, kettle, heater; the bulb filament (tungsten, m.p. 3380 °C; thermally isolated; nitrogen and argon); the fuse.
  • A fuse is a wire of suitable melting point, in series with the device; too large a current melts it and breaks the circuit. Ratings: 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 5 A, 10 A …
  • Unwanted heating wastes energy and can change the properties of components.
