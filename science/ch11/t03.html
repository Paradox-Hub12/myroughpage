Ohm's law

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe Activity 11.1 (CBSE Practical 4), tabulate V and I, plot the V–I graph and find R from it (Figs 11.2–11.3).
  • State Ohm's law with its condition (temperature remains the same), define resistance and the ohm, and use V = IR in all three forms.
  • Explain resistance as opposition to the flow of electrons, the rheostat, and good conductors, resistors, poor conductors and insulators (Activity 11.2).

Add a second cell to a torch and the bulb glows more brightly — more push, more current. But exactly how much more? Double the push and the current in a wire doubles too, as long as the wire does not get hot. That simple rule is Ohm's law.

Quick reminder (Topics 1 and 2)

Current III is measured with an ammeter in series; potential difference VVV with a voltmeter in parallel.

  1. Ohm's law

Ohm's law

the potential difference VVV across the ends of a given metallic wire in a circuit is directly proportional to the current III flowing through it, provided its temperature remains the same.

V∝I⇒VI=constant=R⇒V=IRV \propto I \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dfrac{V}{I} = \text{constant} = R \quad\Rightarrow\quad V = IRV∝I⇒IV​=constant=R⇒V=IRSwipe to see the whole equation →

RRR is the resistance of the conductor — the property of a conductor to resist the flow of charges through it. For a given metallic wire it is constant at a given temperature. Its SI unit is the ohm (Ω):

1 ohm=1 volt1 ampere1\ \text{ohm} = \dfrac{1\ \text{volt}}{1\ \text{ampere}}1 ohm=1 ampere1 volt​Swipe to see the whole equation →

A conductor has a resistance of 1 Ω if a p.d. of 1 V across it drives a current of 1 A through it.

The three forms you will use

To find Use
p.d. V=IRV = IRV=IR
resistance R=VIR = \dfrac{V}{I}R=IV​
current I=VRI = \dfrac{V}{R}I=RV​

From I=VRI = \dfrac{V}{R}I=RV​: for the same p.d., the current is inversely proportional to the resistance — double the resistance, half the current.

Reading the graph. The resistance is the slope of the V–I graph: pick two points on the straight line and find R=change in Vchange in IR = \dfrac{\text{change in } V}{\text{change in } I}R=change in Ichange in V​. A steeper line means a larger resistance.

Resistance does not change because you change the voltage. R=VIR = \dfrac{V}{I}R=IV​ tells you how to measure RRR; if you double VVV, the current doubles too, and the ratio stays the same.

  1. Changing the current — the rheostat

Often we need to increase or decrease the current without changing the cell. A component that does this is a variable resistance; the usual one is a rheostat. Sliding its contact changes how much resistance is in the circuit, and so changes the current.

  1. Why do different things allow different currents? (Activity 11.2)

Lab notebook · do → see → shows
Do
Join four cells (1.5 V each), an ammeter and a plug key in series, leaving a gap XY. Put in the gap, one at a time, a nichrome wire, a torch bulb and a 10 W bulb, and note the ammeter reading each time (take the key out after each reading).
See
the ammeter reading is different for each component.
Shows
different components offer different resistance to the current.

Why? The electrons in a conductor are not completely free: the atoms they pass among pull on them and hold them back. So their motion is slowed down — this is resistance.

Of the same size, a component that… is called
offers a low resistance a good conductor
has some appreciable resistance a resistor
offers a higher resistance a poor conductor
offers an even higher resistance an insulator

Quick check · 1 of 3MCQ1 mark

For a metallic wire kept at a constant temperature, the graph of potential difference V against current I is:

A curve would mean VI\dfrac{V}{I}IV​ changes; for a wire at constant temperature it does not.
A horizontal line would mean the same VVV for every current.
With V=0V = 0V=0 there is no current, so the line must start at the origin.
V∝IV \propto IV∝I gives a straight line through the origin.
Quick check · 2 of 3MCQ1 mark

The p.d. across a resistor is doubled while its temperature stays the same. Its resistance:

RRR is constant at a steady temperature; I=VRI = \dfrac{V}{R}I=RV​ doubles.
Resistance does not depend on the p.d. applied.
Nothing about the resistor has changed.
If RRR doubled as well, the current would not change.

Common mistakes!

  • "R=VIR = \dfrac{V}{I}R=IV​, so doubling V doubles R."

    At a steady temperature RRR is fixed; the current doubles too.

    RRR is a property of the conductor; use it to find the new current.

  • Stating Ohm's law without the condition.

    The law holds only if the temperature remains the same.

    Always add "provided its temperature remains the same".

  • A V–I line drawn not through the origin, or joining the dots.

    No p.d. means no current, so the line starts at (0, 0).

    One ruled straight line through the origin.

  • Writing "V=IRV = \dfrac{I}{R}V=RI​" or "I=VRI = VRI=VR".

    Only V=IRV = IRV=IR, R=VIR = \dfrac{V}{I}R=IV​, I=VRI = \dfrac{V}{R}I=RV​ are correct.

    Check the units: V = A × Ω.

  • Using mA with Ω and V.

    I=VRI = \dfrac{V}{R}I=RV​ gives amperes.

    Convert mA to A first.

Key points

  • Ohm's law: V∝IV \propto IV∝I for a metallic wire, provided its temperature remains the same; V=IRV = IRV=IR.
  • Resistance R=VIR = \dfrac{V}{I}R=IV​ — the property of a conductor to resist the flow of charges; unit ohm (Ω); 1 Ω = 1 V/1 A.
  • The V–I graph is a straight line through the origin; its slope is RRR.
  • I=VRI = \dfrac{V}{R}I=RV​: double the resistance, half the current. A rheostat varies the resistance to control the current.
  • Electrons are held back by the atoms they move among — that is resistance. Same size: good conductor < resistor < poor conductor < insulator (in resistance).
