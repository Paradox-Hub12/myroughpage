Topic 4 of 7

Resistance and resistivity

About 40 minutes · 3 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Describe Activity 11.3 and state how resistance depends on length, area of cross-section and material (R ∝ l, R ∝ 1/A).
  • Use R = ρl/A (with area from diameter); define resistivity and its SI unit (Ω m); state that it is a property of the material and changes with temperature.
  • Use Table 11.2 to compare conductors, alloys and insulators and explain why alloys are used in heating devices, tungsten in bulb filaments, and copper and aluminium in transmission lines.

The thick cable to an electric geyser and the thin wire inside a mobile charger are both copper — yet one is much thicker than the other. The long coiled element of a toaster is not copper at all. Why do the length, the thickness and the metal of a wire matter so much?

Quick reminder (Topic 3)

Resistance R=VIR = \dfrac{V}{I}R=IV​. With the same cell, a larger resistance gives a smaller current — so the ammeter reading tells you which wire has more resistance.

  1. The rule: R=ρlAR = \rho \dfrac{l}{A}R=ρAl​

Careful measurements show that, for a uniform metallic conductor,

R∝landR∝1AR \propto l \qquad \text{and} \qquad R \propto \dfrac{1}{A}R∝landR∝A1​Swipe to see the whole equation →
  • Longer wire → more resistance (double the length, double the resistance).
  • Thicker wire (larger area AAA) → less resistance (double the area, half the resistance).

Combining the two:

R=ρ lAR = \rho\, \dfrac{l}{A}R=ρAl​Swipe to see the whole equation →

The constant ρ\rhoρ (Greek "rho") is the electrical resistivity of the material.

Putting l=1l = 1l=1 m and A=1A = 1A=1 m² in R=ρlAR = \rho\dfrac{l}{A}R=ρAl​ gives R=ρR = \rhoR=ρ. So the resistivity of a material is the resistance of a wire of that material of unit length and unit area of cross-section. This is the definition to write in the exam.

  • Unit of resistivity: from ρ=RAl\rho = \dfrac{RA}{l}ρ=lRA​, the unit is Ω×m2m=\dfrac{\Omega \times \text{m}^2}{\text{m}} =mΩ×m2​= Ω m (ohm metre).
  • Resistivity is a property of the material, not of the shape: a long thin copper wire and a short thick copper wire have different resistances but the same resistivity.
  • Both resistance and resistivity change with temperature.
Quick reminder (area of a wire)

A wire's cross-section is a circle. If its diameter is ddd, its radius is d2\dfrac{d}{2}2d​ and its area is A=πr2=πd24A = \pi r^2 = \dfrac{\pi d^2}{4}A=πr2=4πd2​. Convert first: 1 mm=10−3 m1\ \text{mm} = 10^{-3}\ \text{m}1 mm=10−3 m, so 1 mm2=10−6 m21\ \text{mm}^2 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^21 mm2=10−6 m2. Doubling the diameter makes the area four times larger.

What the table tells us:

  • Metals and alloys have tiny resistivities — most metals about 10−810^{-8}10−8 Ω m (mercury and manganese nearer 10−610^{-6}10−6), alloys about 10−510^{-5}10−5 to 10−410^{-4}10−4 Ω m (your textbook gives the range as 10−810^{-8}10−8 to 10−610^{-6}10−6 Ω m) — they are good conductors. Insulators such as rubber and glass lie between about 101210^{12}1012 and 101710^{17}1017 Ω m.
  • Lower resistivity = better conductor. Silver is the best conductor in the table.
  • An alloy generally has a higher resistivity than the metals it is made from.

  1. Choosing the right material

Use Material Why
Heating elements (electric iron, toaster) alloys such as nichrome high resistivity, and alloys do not oxidise (burn) readily at high temperatures
Filaments of electric bulbs tungsten used almost exclusively (its very high melting point is explained in Topic 6)
Transmission lines copper and aluminium very low resistivity, so little energy is wasted in the wires

Worked example 4

Which material, and why?

Question. Use the resistivities in the table above. (a) Which is the better conductor, aluminium or iron? (b) A heater element must get very hot. Should it be made of copper or nichrome? Give two reasons.

Given: the resistivity table. Asked: the better conductor; the heater material with reasons.

Concept used: lower resistivity = better conductor; uses of alloys (this topic). Why it applies: both parts compare materials.

  1. 1

    (a) Aluminium 2.63×10−82.63 \times 10^{-8}2.63×10−8 Ω m; iron 10.0×10−810.0 \times 10^{-8}10.0×10−8 Ω m → aluminium is lower.

  2. 2

    So aluminium is the better conductor.

  3. 3

    (b) Nichrome's resistivity (100×10−6100 \times 10^{-6}100×10−6 Ω m) is thousands of times copper's (1.62×10−81.62 \times 10^{-8}1.62×10−8 Ω m) → a nichrome element of a convenient size has a high resistance.

  4. 4

Common mistakes!

  • "A longer wire has a higher resistivity."

    Length changes the resistance; resistivity belongs to the material.

    Resistance: lll, AAA, material, temperature. Resistivity: material and temperature only.

  • "Diameter doubled → resistance halved."

    Area depends on the square of the diameter.

    Diameter ×2 → area ×4 → resistance ÷4.

  • Using the diameter in πr2\pi r^2πr2.

    rrr is half the diameter.

    A=π(d2)2=πd24A = \pi\left(\dfrac{d}{2}\right)^2 = \dfrac{\pi d^2}{4}A=π(2d​)2=4πd2​.

  • Leaving mm or mm² in the formula.

    ρ\rhoρ is in Ω m, so lll must be in m and AAA in m².

    1 mm = 10−310^{-3}10−3 m; 1 mm² = 10−610^{-6}10−6 m².

  • "Copper for heater coils because it conducts well."

    A heater needs high resistance and must not oxidise when hot.

    Alloys such as nichrome.

Key points

  • Resistance depends on length (R∝lR \propto lR∝l), area of cross-section (R∝1AR \propto \dfrac{1}{A}R∝A1​) and material: R=ρlAR = \rho\dfrac{l}{A}R=ρAl​.
  • Resistivity ρ\rhoρ is the resistance of a wire of the material of unit length and unit area of cross-section — a characteristic property of the material; SI unit Ω m. Resistance and resistivity both vary with temperature.
  • Most metals about 10−810^{-8}10−8 Ω m; alloys higher (nichrome 10−410^{-4}10−4 Ω m) — all good conductors; insulators: 101210^{12}1012 to 101710^{17}1017 Ω m. Alloys have higher resistivity than their metals.
  • Uses: alloys (nichrome) for heating elements — high resistivity, do not oxidise readily; tungsten for bulb filaments; copper and aluminium for transmission lines — low resistivity.
  • Area from diameter: A=πd24A = \dfrac{\pi d^2}{4}A=4πd2​; always work in metres.
