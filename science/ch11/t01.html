Topic 1 of 7

Electric current and the electric circuit

About 35 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Define electric current as the rate of flow of charge, use I = Q/t with SI units (C, A, mA, µA), and find the number of electrons in a given charge.
  • Describe an electric circuit, the job of a switch, the conventional direction of current (opposite to electron flow) and the ammeter (always in series) (Fig 11.1).

Press the switch of a torch and the bulb lights at once. Slide it off and the light dies. Nothing you can see moves inside the wires — so what is flowing, and what exactly does the switch do?

Quick reminder (Classes 6–7)

A cell has two terminals, + and −. A bulb glows only when there is an unbroken path of conductors (metal wires) from one terminal, through the bulb, back to the other terminal. Insulators such as plastic and rubber do not let electricity pass.

  1. Electric current — a flow of charge

You know a water current (water flowing in a river) and an air current (moving air). In the same way, when electric charge flows through a conductor such as a metal wire, we say there is an electric current in it.

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge — the amount of charge that flows through a cross-section of the conductor in one second. If a net charge QQQ flows through a cross-section in time ttt, the current is

Quick reminder (powers of ten)

"Milli" means one-thousandth (10−310^{-3}10−3) and "micro" means one-millionth (10−610^{-6}10−6). So 250 mA =250×10−3= 250 \times 10^{-3}=250×10−3 A =0.25= 0.25=0.25 A. Always change minutes to seconds (× 60) before using I=QtI = \dfrac{Q}{t}I=tQ​.

  1. What carries the charge in a wire?

In a metal wire, the moving charges are electrons. Each electron carries a tiny negative charge of 1.6×10−191.6 \times 10^{-19}1.6×10−19 C. One coulomb is the charge of nearly 6×10186 \times 10^{18}6×1018 electrons. To find how many electrons make up a charge QQQ:

n=Qe,e=1.6×10−19 Cn = \dfrac{Q}{e}, \qquad e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}n=eQ​,e=1.6×10−19 CSwipe to see the whole equation →

  1. Which way does current flow?

Electricity was studied long before electrons were discovered. Scientists then imagined current as a flow of positive charge, from the + terminal of the cell, round the circuit, to the − terminal. We still use this conventional current.

  • Conventional current: from + to − through the outside circuit.
  • Electrons: they are negative, so they actually drift the opposite way — from − to + through the outside circuit.

So the direction of electric current is opposite to the direction of flow of electrons. In every circuit diagram, the arrow marked III shows the conventional current.

  1. The electric circuit

A continuous and closed path of an electric current is called an electric circuit. A switch (or plug key) makes or breaks a conducting link in that path:

  • switch on (plug in) → the path is closed → current flows → the bulb glows;
  • switch off, or a break anywhere → the path is open → current stops everywhere at once.

Current is not used up as it goes round. The same current that leaves the cell passes through the bulb and returns to the cell. What the bulb takes from the moving charges is energy, not the charges themselves (you will see this in Topics 2 and 7).

  1. Measuring current — the ammeter

An ammeter measures current. It is always connected in series — that is, in the path of the current, so that the whole current to be measured passes through it. Its + terminal is joined to the side that leads back to the + terminal of the cell.

Fig 11.1 shows a typical circuit: a cell, an electric bulb, an ammeter and a plug key, all in one loop. The current flows from the + terminal of the cell, through the bulb and the ammeter, back to the − terminal.

Worked example 4

Directions and the ammeter

Question. In a torch, two cells, a switch and a bulb are joined in one loop. (a) In which direction does the conventional current flow through the bulb, and in which direction do the electrons move? (b) Where must an ammeter be placed to measure the current through the bulb, and how?

Given: a single loop with cells, a switch and a bulb. Asked: directions; how to connect the ammeter.

Concept used: conventional current and the ammeter (this topic). Why it applies: both questions are about one closed loop.

  1. 1

    (a) Conventional current → leaves the + terminal of the cells, goes through the bulb, returns to the − terminal.

  2. 2

    Electrons → move the opposite way: from the − terminal, through the bulb, to the + terminal.

  3. 3

    (b) Ammeter → in series, anywhere in the loop (for example between the switch and the bulb), so that the whole current passes through it.

  4. 4

    Terminals → its + terminal on the side that leads back to the + of the cells.

  5. Check: in a single loop the current is the same everywhere, so the ammeter reads the bulb's current wherever it is placed in the loop ✔.

  6. Answer (a) Current: from + through the bulb to −; electrons: the opposite way. (b) In series in the loop, + terminal towards the + of the cells.

    Common trap: putting the ammeter across the bulb (in parallel). An ammeter must carry the current it measures, so it goes in the loop.

Common mistakes!

  • "The bulb uses up some current, so less current returns to the cell."

    Charge is not used up; the same current flows in every part of a single loop.

    The bulb takes energy from the moving charges, not the charges.

  • Drawing the current arrow from − to + through the wires.

    That is the direction of electron flow.

    Conventional current: + → − outside the cell.

  • Putting the ammeter across a component.

    An ammeter must carry the whole current it measures.

    Ammeter in series, + terminal towards the cell's +.

  • Using minutes, or mA, in I=QtI = \dfrac{Q}{t}I=tQ​.

    The formula gives amperes only with coulombs and seconds.

    Convert: min × 60 → s; mA × 10−310^{-3}10−3 → A.

  • "The number of electrons is 10−1910^{-19}10−19."

    One electron's charge is tiny, so a coulomb holds a huge number of them.

    n=Q1.6×10−19n = \dfrac{Q}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}n=1.6×10−19Q​ — a large number.

Key points

  • Electric current is the rate of flow of charge: I=QtI = \dfrac{Q}{t}I=tQ​. SI units: charge in coulomb (C), current in ampere (A); 1 A = 1 C/s; 1 mA = 10−310^{-3}10−3 A, 1 µA = 10−610^{-6}10−6 A.
  • In metal wires the charges that move are electrons (e=1.6×10−19e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}e=1.6×10−19 C); 1 C is the charge of nearly 6×10186 \times 10^{18}6×1018 electrons.
  • Conventional current flows from + to − outside the cell — opposite to the flow of electrons.
  • An electric circuit is a continuous, closed path; a switch makes or breaks it. Current is not used up round the loop.
  • An ammeter measures current and is always connected in series.
