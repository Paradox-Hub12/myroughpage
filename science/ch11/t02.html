Potential difference and circuit diagrams

About 35 minutes · 2 things to learn
By the end you can…
  • Explain why charges flow (potential difference, maintained by a cell or battery through chemical action), define p.d. and the volt, use V = W/Q, and state that a voltmeter is connected in parallel.
  • Draw and read circuit diagrams with the symbols of Table 11.1, with the ammeter in series, the voltmeter in parallel and terminals the right way round.

A copper wire lying on the table is full of electrons, yet no current flows in it. Join its ends to a cell and a current flows at once. What does the cell give the wire that it did not have before?

Quick reminder (Class 9, Work and Energy). Work is done when a force moves something; work and energy are both measured in joules (J).

  1. Why charges flow — potential difference

Think of water in a horizontal pipe: it does not flow by itself. Join one end to a tank kept higher up, and the two ends of the pipe are at different pressures — now water flows. (This is only an analogy; gravity plays no part in a wire.)

Electrons drift along a wire only when its two ends differ in "electric pressure". This is called the potential difference (p.d.). A cell produces a potential difference between its terminals by the chemical action inside it — even when no current is drawn. When the cell is joined to a conductor, this p.d. sets the charges moving and a current flows. To keep the current going, the cell must keep spending the chemical energy stored in it. A battery is a combination of one or more cells.

  1. Defining potential difference and the volt

The electric potential difference between two points in a circuit carrying a current is the work done to move a unit charge from one point to the other:

V=WQV = \dfrac{W}{Q}V=QW​Swipe to see the whole equation →

The SI unit of potential difference is the volt (V). One volt is the p.d. between two points of a current-carrying conductor when 1 joule of work is done to move 1 coulomb of charge from one point to the other:

1 V=1 J1 C=1 J C−11\ \text{V} = \dfrac{1\ \text{J}}{1\ \text{C}} = 1\ \text{J C}^{-1}1 V=1 C1 J​=1 J C−1Swipe to see the whole equation →

So a 3 V cell gives 3 J of energy to every coulomb of charge that passes through it.

  1. Measuring potential difference — the voltmeter

A voltmeter measures potential difference. It is always connected in parallel — across the two points between which the p.d. is to be measured. Its + terminal goes to the point nearer the cell's + terminal.

  1. Circuit diagrams and their symbols

Drawing real cells, bulbs and wires takes too long. Instead we draw a circuit diagram — a schematic drawing in which each component is shown by a standard symbol (Table 11.1 of your textbook):

Component How the symbol looks
An electric cell a long thin line (+) and a short thick line (−), side by side
A battery (cells in a row) several cells joined + to −, long line of one next to short line of the next
Plug key or switch (open) two small brackets facing each other with a gap: ( )
Plug key or switch (closed) the same brackets with a dot between them: (•)
A wire joint two lines meeting, with a solid dot at the joint
Wires crossing without joining one line with a small hump where it crosses the other
Electric bulb a small loop (filament) on a line
A resistor of resistance RRR a zig-zag line
Variable resistance (rheostat) a zig-zag with an arrow across it (or an arrow touching it)
Ammeter a circle with A, terminals marked + and −
Voltmeter a circle with V, terminals marked + and −

Rules for a neat circuit diagram

  1. Draw the wires as straight ruled lines with square corners.
  2. Use only the standard symbols; label each resistor with its value.
  3. Put the ammeter in series and the voltmeter in parallel with the part it measures.
  4. Join each meter's + terminal towards the + terminal of the cell or battery.
  5. Show the direction of current with an arrow marked III.

Worked example 3

From words to a circuit diagram

Question. Draw a circuit diagram for: a battery of three cells, an electric bulb, a 6 Ω resistor, an ammeter and a plug key, all in series, and a voltmeter to measure the potential difference across the resistor.

Given: the list of components and how they are joined. Asked: the circuit diagram.

Concept used: the symbols and rules for circuit diagrams (this topic). Why it applies: every item has a standard symbol, and the meters have fixed connections.

  1. 1

    Draw a rectangle with a ruler — this is the single loop.

  2. 2

    Battery → on one side, three cells joined long line to short line; mark + at one end and − at the other.

  3. 3

    In series round the loop → the bulb, the 6 Ω resistor (zig-zag, labelled), the ammeter (circle with A) and the closed plug key (•). Their order round the loop does not matter.

  4. 4

    Ammeter terminals → + on the side that leads back to the battery's +.

  5. 5

    Voltmeter → draw it outside the loop, and join its two leads to the two ends of the resistor (solid dots at the joints). Its + terminal goes to the resistor end nearer the battery's +.

  6. 6

    Arrow → mark III from the battery's + terminal round the loop.

  7. Check: follow the loop with a finger: battery → bulb → resistor → ammeter → key → battery, with no branch except the voltmeter across the resistor ✔.

  8. Answer The diagram is shown below.

    Common trap: joining the voltmeter across the whole loop or across the battery when the question asks for the p.d. across the resistor.

Worked example 4

Spot the wrong connections

Question. Ravi's diagram shows a cell, a key and a resistor in a loop. He has drawn the voltmeter in the loop (in series) and the ammeter across the resistor (in parallel). (a) What is wrong? (b) How should he redraw it?

Given: a voltmeter in series and an ammeter in parallel. Asked: the errors; the correct diagram.

Concept used: ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel (Topics 1 and 2). Why it applies: the two meters have been swapped.

  1. 1

    (a) The ammeter must carry the whole current, so it must be in series — not across the resistor.

  2. 2

    The voltmeter measures the p.d. across two points, so it must be in parallel — not in the loop.

  3. 3

    (b) Redraw: move the ammeter into the loop (in series with the cell, key and resistor), + terminal towards the cell's +.

  4. 4

    Join the voltmeter's two leads to the two ends of the resistor, + terminal on the end nearer the cell's +.

  5. Check: now the current passes through the ammeter on its way round, and the voltmeter sits across the resistor alone ✔.

  6. Answer (a) The meters are swapped: the ammeter is in parallel and the voltmeter in series. (b) Ammeter in series in the loop; voltmeter in parallel across the resistor; both with + towards the cell's +.

    Common trap: fixing only one meter. Check both connections and both pairs of terminals.

Key points

  • Charges flow only when there is a potential difference across the conductor; a cell maintains it by chemical action, and spends its chemical energy to keep the current going.
  • Potential difference = work done per unit charge: V=WQV = \dfrac{W}{Q}V=QW​. Unit: volt; 1 V=1 J C−11\ \text{V} = 1\ \text{J C}^{-1}1 V=1 J C−1.
  • A voltmeter measures p.d. and is connected in parallel; an ammeter is connected in series.
  • Circuit diagrams use standard symbols (Table 11.1): cell (long line +), battery, plug key, joint, crossing, bulb, resistor, rheostat, ammeter, voltmeter.
